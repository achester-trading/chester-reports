### Reader's note

Every other paper in the library is about the market or about the machine that reads it. This one is about the person between the two. The reports can be right, the register can be complete, the Doctrine can be followed to the letter on most days, and money can still be lost in the gap between a correct read and the trade that was supposed to express it. That gap is behavioral, and it is the subject here.

The paper has a narrow claim and a wide one. The narrow claim is that the four failure modes the Doctrine already names — size creep, thesis attachment, overtrading in chop, and narrative capture by one's own research — are each preceded by a tell that can be seen in advance and each leave a mark in a ledger field that can be counted afterwards. The wide claim is that the behavioral literature, read for a trader rather than for an investor, explains why those four keep happening to intelligent people, and that the Doctrine's rules, the register and the day-trading order's rules work because they constrain execution without constraining direction. The operator keeps every view; the system takes away a handful of the ways a view gets turned into a loss.

The paper is written from principles, now, on purpose. The guide once deferred it by six months so that it could be written from the operator's own data. The ruling of 6 November reversed that: written now, it says in advance what the register should show if the system works, and Draft 2 can then be checked against what it said. Written afterwards, it would be an explanation of results already known, and an explanation fitted to known results is the narrative fallacy this paper warns against.

Three boundaries keep it short. **The Operating Doctrine owns the rules**: this paper cites them by number and never restates or amends them. **Portfolio Construction owns sizing across books**: where a failure mode is about size, this paper describes the behavior and leaves the arithmetic there. **This paper owns the operator**: the habits, the pressures and the tells. It makes no forecast in its body; the one dated read is Appendix B.

### Prerequisites

The paper assumes the reader knows the four books, which are defined here where they are first used and taken from the Operating Doctrine's Part IV. **Book A, allocation**, holds positions for one to six months and is run by exposure bands set from the regime. **Book B, swing**, holds positions for one to six weeks on trend, momentum and variant perception. **Book C, tactical**, holds positions for one to five days on event reactions, dealer positioning and session structure. **Book D, opportunistic**, has no fixed horizon and takes small, defined-risk positions on behavioral and alternative data, forced flows and mispriced optionality. It also assumes the vocabulary of R, the unit of risk a position carries from entry to its stop, which Evidence and Inference teaches, and the three regime dials (Macro, Volatility and Gamma) that the Doctrine's Part V defines. The register, the Decision Packet and the post-mortem fields are described in Building and Validating a Systematic Book and in the Doctrine's Part IX. The reports themselves are in The Reporting System.

The paper uses no dollar figure and no account figure anywhere. Every size is in R or in percent of risk capital, and every result is in R. That is a standing rule for this paper, not a convention of style.

# Part I — The Gap Between the Read and the Trade

## Chapter 1 — Why a right view still loses money

A read is a statement about the world: the regime is Tightening, the dealer book is short gamma below the flip, the 10-year's move is term premium rather than expected short rates. A trade is a statement about the world plus four other things. It says when the read will pay, how much of the book should depend on it, what will be done if it is wrong, and what will be done if it is right. The read can be correct and any one of those four can be wrong, and each of them is decided by a person at a moment, under whatever pressure that moment carries.

The cleanest way to see the gap is to hold the read fixed. Suppose a Book B thesis is correct — the market does, over six weeks, go where the thesis said — and consider what can still go wrong. The entry can be early, so that the position is stopped out on the way to being right; the timing was the error. The size can be large, so that the drawdown before the move forces an exit the plan never called for; the size was the error. The stop can be moved, so that a loss the plan capped at one R becomes three; the exit was the error. The profit can be taken at the first green day out of relief, so that a two-R plan pays half an R; the exit again. Or the position can be right and the trader, emboldened, can open three more like it in the same factor, so that the next wrong read costs four times what it should; the size again, now spread across names. In every one of those cases the reports did their job. The loss was made after them.

The Doctrine's Part II says the same thing in its own terms: the operator's deficit was never analytical; it was behavioral and structural. The Wizards' literature says it more bluntly, and the Doctrine's Rule 23 carries Livermore's version of it — a good market observer is not the same as a good risk manager, and a book that has only the first will be right often and still lose. This paper takes that sentence as its starting point and asks a narrower question: what, specifically, happens in the minutes and days between the read and the trade, and where does it leave a mark?

Three properties of the gap make it hard to close by effort alone. The first is that it is **asymmetric in time**: the read is formed slowly, with the reports open and the coffee warm; the trade decisions that matter most — move the stop, add, take the profit, open another — are taken quickly, with a position on and a number moving. The second is that it is **invisible in the P&L**: a loss made by a moved stop and a loss made by a wrong thesis look identical in the account, so a trader who reads only the account learns nothing about which one happened. The third is that it is **self-concealing**: the mind that made the decision also writes the story of why, and the story almost always credits the read and blames the market. Each of those properties is a reason to put the gap's record outside the trader's head, which is what the register is for.

What the operator does differently because of this chapter is small and specific: when a result is reviewed, the first question is not "was the read right?" but "which of the five decisions — the read, the timing, the size, the exit, the follow-on — made the result?" The Doctrine's six post-mortem questions (its Section 9.2) are that question in categorical form, and Chapter 6 below shows that each failure mode lives mostly in one of them.

## Chapter 2 — Size creep

**What it is.** Size creep is the gradual rise of position size above the size the plan sets, without any change in the plan. It rarely arrives as a decision to trade bigger. It arrives as a series of small rounding choices, each defensible: the setup is a little better than usual, so the size is a little larger than ordinary; the last three trades won, so the account can stand it; the stop is a little tighter, so more contracts fit inside the same risk; this one is really two ideas, so it gets the size of two. After a month of such choices the ordinary-tier position carries one and a half times the ordinary risk, and nobody decided that it should.

Size creep has two engines, and they run in opposite market conditions. After a run of wins it is fed by overconfidence and by what the literature calls the house-money effect: gains feel like the market's money rather than one's own, and risking them feels cheaper than risking capital (Chapter 8). After a run of losses it is fed by the break-even effect: a trader behind for the week takes larger risks to get back to even, because the reference point is the start of the week, not the current account (Chapter 8 again). The first engine produces the large position that gives back a month; the second produces the large position that turns a bad week into a tripped switch. Both feel, from the inside, like conviction.

**The tell.** Size creep announces itself before it costs anything. The earliest tell is a size chosen before the tier test is run — a number in mind before the calculator has been asked. The second is a packet whose stop has been tightened to make a desired size fit, which shows up as a stop closer to entry than the structure the Technical Indicators paper would place it at. The third is the phrase "this one is different" in a packet's thesis line, or its equivalents ("cleaner than usual", "the best setup this month"). The fourth, after losses, is a size request on the morning after a red day that is larger than the size on the morning before it.

**The field that catches it.** The register stamps the tier on every packet, and the sizing calculator computes the tier from the regime stamps and refuses a tier the stamps do not support (the Doctrine's Rule 14). Size creep therefore cannot reach an exceptional tier by accident. What it can do is creep inside a tier — larger risk within ordinary, or ordinary sizes taken more often on marginal setups. The fields that catch that are the risk at entry in R and percent of capital, read against the tier's standard, and the time-of-day and written-at stamps, read against the previous session's result. The cut that shows it is the ratio of realised risk at entry to the tier's standard, by the result of the prior five decisions. If that ratio rises after wins or after losses, size creep is present, and the cut will say which engine drives it. Portfolio Construction owns what the right size is; this paper owns the drift away from it.

## Chapter 3 — Thesis attachment

**What it is.** Thesis attachment is the persistence of a position after the evidence that justified it has gone, because the trader has come to own the thesis rather than the position. The Doctrine's account of the operator's record names its most expensive form: a correct short thesis into a collapse, followed by losses as the market recovered faster than the thesis could be abandoned. The read had been right; the attachment outlived it. The attachment is not stupidity. It is the ordinary result of having done the work: the more research a thesis took, the more it costs to admit it has stopped being true, and the more sophisticated the trader, the better the arguments available for holding on.

Attachment has three recognisable behaviours. The stop is moved or "given room" because the thesis is still right in the trader's view. The time stop is waived because the move is "just taking longer". And the position is added to on adverse movement — averaging down — because a lower price is a better entry for a thesis still believed. The Doctrine forbids the third outright (Doctrine Rule 4: never add to a loser), sets the second by rule for counter-trend trades (Doctrine Rule 27: a five-session time stop, closed whatever the thesis), and requires the first to be written before entry and honoured without renegotiation (Doctrine Rule 5). The rules exist because the behaviour does.

**The tell.** The tell of attachment is in language before it is in price. A thesis restated in stronger terms after an adverse move — "this is now even more compelling" — is the clearest. A second is the search for confirming material after entry: the trader who, with a losing short on, reads only the bear case is no longer reading; the operator's own voices register makes this easy to see, because the sources opened after entry can be compared with those opened before it. A third is a request to change the exit plan in the middle of a drawdown, which the Doctrine's Rule 20 already treats as a change made under pain. A fourth, specific to this book, is a short position carried into a Top & Bottom bottom signal: the Doctrine's Rule 26 ends a short campaign on that signal, not on conviction, precisely because attachment reads the signal as noise.

**The field that catches it.** The packet records the exit before entry: the price at which the thesis is wrong, the time by which it must have worked and the profit plan. Attachment shows up as the difference between that record and what happened. The post-mortem's six questions then classify the loss. A loss marked "thesis wrong" whose position was held past its written time stop is attachment, whatever else it was. The cut that shows it is the share of losing decisions that exited later than their written time or price, and the mean R of those against the mean R of losses that exited on plan. If the late exits lose materially more, attachment is costing money. The with-trend versus counter-trend cut the Doctrine prints (its Section 9.1) is the same failure mode measured from the other side, since a counter-trend position is the one most often held on a thesis against the tape.

## Chapter 4 — Overtrading in chop

**What it is.** Overtrading in chop is the multiplication of decisions in a market that offers none. A choppy market — range-bound, positive gamma, low realised volatility, frequent small reversals — produces many setups that look like the beginnings of a move and few that become one. Each false start invites an entry; each stop-out invites a re-entry to "get it back"; each small win in the range invites another. At the end of the week the decision count is high, the expectancy is negative after costs, and the trader is tired.

The mechanism is partly the market's and partly the trader's. The market's part is that a range produces a high frequency of patterns a breakout or momentum setup will read as signals, and the false-signal rate is highest exactly when the regime is quiet. The trader's part is the feeling that an open market is an obligation, the boredom of a slow tape, and the break-even effect after the first stop-out of the day. The Doctrine's Rule 10 answers the first with the claim that doing nothing is a position; its Rule 8 answers the regime with the trust matrix, which reads breakout setups in calm positive-gamma chop as Off. The day-trading order adds a rule aimed squarely at the third: no flip inside thirty minutes, so that a stop-out cannot be turned straight into the opposite position (Chapter 13).

**The tell.** The tell is a count. More than the session's usual number of entries before noon is one form. A re-entry on the same instrument within the hour of a stop-out is another, and the 07:00 anchor's day-trade checklist already prints the rule against it. A third is entries outside the day's prepared list, which on a volatile day the Doctrine forbids (its Rule 3) and which on a quiet day are the most likely sign that boredom is choosing the trades. A fourth is a regime stamp that reads Positive gamma and Calm on a decision whose setup type the trust matrix reads as Off in that state.

**The field that catches it.** The register's decision count by session and by regime stamp, the time-between-decisions stamp, and the setup type against the trust matrix's reading at entry. The cut that shows it is expectancy per decision against decisions per session, within each Gamma dial state. A book that overtrades chop shows expectancy falling as the count rises in Positive gamma and not in Negative. The attention budget of the Doctrine's Section 10.2 — seven packet approvals a week, the rest deferred as abstentions graded like any other — is a ceiling on the count, and its graded abstentions are the measurement: if the deferred packets would not have paid, the budget saved money and the overtrading was real.

## Chapter 5 — Narrative capture by one's own research

**What it is.** Narrative capture is the failure mode peculiar to a trader who owns a research system. The system produces a story — a regime, a scenario, a tail family, a theme — and the story becomes a lens through which every later print is read, so that new information confirms the story rather than tests it. The more articulate the research, the stronger the capture, because a well-written thesis supplies a ready explanation for any adverse print ("that is the early phase the paper describes"). The guide's own standing caution names it as the sophistication trap: being highly informed substituting for being right, with every loss articulately explained.

The library makes this failure mode more likely, not less, and that is worth saying plainly. Thirty-odd papers, a Monthly, a Weekly, a daily close and a dozen dated ledgers produce a great deal of narrative. A trader who has read the Twenty-Five can find a tail scenario consistent with almost any week, and a trader who has read Foundations can place almost any print inside a five-force story. Neither paper is wrong to exist. The danger is in the direction of travel: the story arriving first and the print being fitted to it, rather than the print arriving first and the story being tested against it. The Doctrine's Rule 9 — price reaction outranks the headline — is the market-facing guard; this chapter is about the guard on the reader.

**The tell.** The tell is a thesis line that cites a report section rather than an edge. "The Monthly says Tightening" is a context, not an edge; a packet whose only reason is a narrative is one the Doctrine's Rule 6 would block, because it names no counterparty and no persistence argument. A second tell is a cluster of positions across books that share one narrative — the same factor, the same scenario — which the Doctrine's heat cap counts once (its Rule 16) and which the operator experiences as diversification. A third is the absence of a falsifier: a thesis that cannot say what print would end it has been captured, because the narrative has absorbed every possible outcome. A fourth, specific to the library, is reading a dated appendix figure as if it were the timeless body's claim: the dated appendices are the papers' current read, and a current read stated in a paper's voice carries more authority than it should.

**The field that catches it.** The packet's edge family and its three edge questions (what the edge is, who is on the other side, why it persists) are required fields. The narrative register records each story as a graded object with its implied outcome and horizon, so a narrative can be scored like a forecast. The heat calculator records correlated exposure. The cut that shows capture is the expectancy of decisions whose thesis line cites a narrative-register story, against those whose thesis line cites a setup or a measured signal. The tell in the register is a narrative that keeps generating decisions after its own graded outcomes have turned against it.

## Chapter 6 — The four together, and the field that catches each

The four failure modes are distinct, but they are not independent. Size creep makes attachment expensive: the larger the position, the more painful the stop, and the stronger the pull to move it. Overtrading in chop produces the losses that feed the break-even effect, which feeds size creep. Narrative capture supplies the arguments that make attachment feel like discipline. A bad month is usually two or three of them in sequence, and the sequence usually starts with the one the trader is least alert to.

Each also lives mostly in one of the Doctrine's post-mortem categories, which is what makes them countable. Size creep is a size error. Attachment is an exit error recorded against a thesis that had already failed, and it is often marked "thesis wrong" when the more accurate mark is "held past the plan". Overtrading in chop is a regime error at the level of the setup, and a timing error at the level of the trade. Narrative capture is a thesis error whose thesis was never an edge. The seventh field, the rule-break flag, catches the moment any of them crosses a written rule.

The summary below is a cheat sheet for the four chapters above; each line is argued there.

| Failure mode | Engine | Earliest tell | Field that catches it | The cut that shows it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Size creep | House money after wins; break-even after losses | A size chosen before the tier test | Risk at entry against the tier; prior-result stamp | Risk ratio by the prior five results |
| Thesis attachment | Owning the thesis, not the position | The thesis restated stronger after an adverse move | Written exit against the actual exit | Late exits' mean R against on-plan losses |
| Overtrading in chop | False signals in a quiet range; boredom | Re-entry within the hour of a stop-out | Decisions per session; setup against the trust matrix | Expectancy against count, by Gamma state |
| Narrative capture | The story read before the print | A thesis line that cites a report, not an edge | The three edge questions; the narrative register | Narrative-cited against signal-cited expectancy |

What the operator does differently because of Part I is to read the register by failure mode before reading it by result. A month with a positive result and a rising risk ratio is a warning; a month with a negative result and every exit on plan is variance. The account cannot tell those two apart. The fields can.

# Part II — The Canon

*A note on the closing clauses in this Part. Each empirical claim ends with the study's sample and period and the date it was cited for this draft. The findings are as their authors published them. None was re-run here, and Appendix A lists every one for re-verification at the operator's read.*

## Chapter 7 — Loss aversion and the disposition effect

The behavioral literature most often read by investors is about portfolios: under-diversification, home bias, the cost of fees. Most of it matters little to a trader running defined-risk positions with written exits. What does matter is the smaller body of work on how people treat a position once they hold it, because that is where the gap of Part I lives. Six findings carry most of the weight, and this Part takes them in turn. Each is stated as a mechanism, with the sample that supports it and the conditions under which it has held or failed.

**Loss aversion** is the finding that a loss is felt more strongly than a gain of the same size. In prospect theory's account, people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point rather than as final wealth, and the value function is steeper for losses than for gains. The usual estimate is that a loss weighs a little over twice as much as an equal gain *(Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 elicitation, a median loss-aversion coefficient of about 2.25 from a small sample of graduate students choosing among hypothetical gambles; cited 6 November 2026)*. The coefficient varies widely across people and tasks, and some later work finds it much smaller for small stakes or routine exchanges. The direction has proved robust; the size has not.

For a trader, loss aversion matters less as a single number than through the reference point it implies. A position is not felt as a contribution to the account. It is felt as a gain or a loss **against its entry price**, and the entry price becomes the most important number on the screen, though the market does not know it exists. Everything else in this chapter follows from that.

**The disposition effect** is loss aversion's signature in trading records: the tendency to sell winners too soon and hold losers too long. Shefrin and Statman named it in 1985. Odean's study of a discount broker's accounts gave it its best-known measurement: investors realised gains at about one and a half times the rate at which they realised losses, and the winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept *(10,000 accounts at a US discount brokerage, 1987–1993; the proportion of gains realised about 0.15 against 0.10 for losses; the sold winners outperformed the held losers by about three percentage points over the following year; cited 6 November 2026)*.

The mechanism is the reference point. Below entry, the position sits in the region of the value function where people take risk to avoid a sure loss, so holding — a gamble on recovery — is preferred to selling, which makes the loss real. Above entry, it sits in the region where people avoid risk to keep a sure gain, so selling is preferred to holding. Some economists doubt that prospect theory alone produces the effect, and propose instead a pleasure in realising a gain and a pain in realising a loss at the moment of sale *(the realisation-utility account, and Kaustia's 2010 test against prospect theory's predictions on Finnish investors; cited 6 November 2026)*. For the operator the distinction matters little. Either way, the moment of selling is where the bias acts, and that is the moment the Doctrine takes away from discretion.

**Where it has held and where it has failed.** The effect is strongest among individual investors and weakest among professionals, but it does not vanish with professionalism. Futures floor traders who held losers longer earned less than those who did not *(Locke and Mann's study of Chicago Mercantile Exchange floor traders, 1995; cited 6 November 2026)*. Israeli investors with professional managers showed less of the effect than those who traded independently, though still some *(Shapira and Venezia, Israeli brokerage accounts, 1994; cited 6 November 2026)*. The effect also declines with experience, partly because investors learn and partly because the worst-affected stop trading *(Seru, Shumway and Stoffman, Finnish individual investors, 1995–2003; cited 6 November 2026)*.

It reverses in one documented condition: when the position is part of a written plan executed by rule. The literature's natural experiments are fund managers with mandated sell disciplines and stop-loss rules. That is the condition the Doctrine imposes, and it is the reason the operator's ledger should show less of the effect than the literature's retail samples. If it does not, the rules are not being followed.

**What the operator does differently.** The entry price is noted once, in the packet, and then treated as irrelevant to every decision except the stop. A question that begins "it's only down…" or "it's already up…" is answered by the plan, not by the P&L. Doctrine Rule 17 (exits follow the plan, not the P&L) and Doctrine Rule 5 (exit written before entry) are, in the language of this chapter, the disposition effect made illegal.

## Chapter 8 — House money and the break-even effect

Loss aversion predicts that people are cautious after gains and reckless only to avoid a sure loss. Experience suggests something more specific. Thaler and Johnson's experiments separated two effects that run against the simplest reading *(gambles with real and hypothetical stakes offered to student subjects after a prior gain or loss, 1990; cited 6 November 2026)*:

- **The house-money effect.** After a gain, people become **more** willing to take risk, as though the gain were someone else's money that can be lost without hurting.
- **The break-even effect.** After a loss, people become more willing to take a gamble that offers a chance to get back to even, and less willing to take one that does not.

Both effects act through the reference point. Whether a gain is "house money" depends on whether the trader still counts it as separate from capital. Whether a loss provokes risk-seeking depends on whether a chance to break even is on offer.

The professional evidence is direct. Proprietary traders in Treasury bond futures who lost money in the morning took **more** risk in the afternoon, and that afternoon trading moved prices in a way that cost them *(Coval and Shumway, proprietary traders on the Chicago Board of Trade, 1998; cited 6 November 2026)*. These were full-time professionals trading their own firms' capital, the population least expected to show the bias. They showed it within a single session.

A later refinement makes the effect usable. Imas found that the break-even effect depends on whether the loss has been **realised** *(laboratory experiments with real stakes, published 2016; cited 6 November 2026)*. After a paper loss, still open, people take more risk, trying to get back to even. After a realised loss, closed and booked, they take less. Closing a position resets the reference point, and an open loss keeps the old one alive. That is the strongest argument in the literature for the resting stop, which realises the loss mechanically instead of leaving it open to be fought. It is also the argument for not "managing" a losing day by holding its positions into the close.

**Conditions.** Both effects are stronger when the trader's reference period is short — the day, the week — and weaker when it is long. The Doctrine's choice of a thirteen-week score over a weekly one is, among other things, a choice to lengthen the reference period (Doctrine Rule 22). Both effects also grow with stakes relative to the trader's comfort, which is one reason the Doctrine's kill-switch ladder works in percentages of capital rather than in figures that feel like money.

**What the operator does differently.** A red morning is not a reason to change anything in the afternoon. The resting stop realises a loss so that it stops shaping the next decision. A green week is not a budget. It is one week of a thirteen-week sample, and the Doctrine's tier test, not the week's result, sets the next size.

## Chapter 9 — Overconfidence after a run

Overconfidence comes in two forms that matter here. The first is miscalibration: confidence intervals too narrow, so that outcomes the trader rated very unlikely happen far more often than the rating implied. The second is self-attribution: wins credited to skill and losses to luck, so that a run of wins raises self-assessed skill faster than the evidence warrants. Gervais and Odean modelled the second as "learning to be overconfident". A trader who updates on wins more than on losses becomes most overconfident after a run of success, which is exactly when size creep begins *(the theory, published 2001; cited 6 November 2026)*.

The cost shows up in turnover. Households that traded most earned the least net of costs *(Barber and Odean, 66,465 households at a US discount broker, 1991–1996; the most active fifth earned about 11 percent a year net against about 18 percent for the market; cited 6 November 2026)*. Men, who in the same data reported more confidence in their own ability, traded about half as much again as women and paid for it in returns *(Barber and Odean, the same broker's accounts, published 2001; cited 6 November 2026)*. The day-trading evidence is starker:

- **Taiwan.** Fewer than one percent of day traders were predictably profitable net of fees, across the whole population over fifteen years *(Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean, Taiwan Stock Exchange day traders, 1992–2006; cited 6 November 2026)*.
- **Brazil.** Of the individuals who persisted at day trading index futures for more than three hundred days, about ninety-seven percent lost money *(Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti, Brazilian mini-index futures, 2013–2015; cited 6 November 2026)*.

**Conditions.** These are population results, and their main lesson is about the base rate, not about any single trader. Evidence and Inference makes the same point about the operator's own sample: a winning streak of twenty decisions is weak evidence of skill, because a break-even process produces such streaks often. The overconfidence literature adds the behavioral half: a winning streak also changes the trader. It is therefore not only weak evidence but a hazard, because it raises size and lowers the bar for entry at the moment the sample least supports either.

Overconfidence has one well-documented counterweight. The "outside view" — asking how often trades like this one have worked, before asking how good this one looks — reduces the error in planning and forecasting tasks. Kahneman and Lovallo's account of timid choices and bold forecasts describes it, and the pre-mortem is a version of it: imagine the trade has failed and write the reason *(Kahneman and Lovallo, 1993; Klein's pre-mortem, 2007; cited 6 November 2026)*. Base Rates is the library's outside view, and the Doctrine's tier-2 rule of the change ladder (its Section 10.1) institutionalises it: a conditional rate is never printed without the unconditional base rate beside it.

**What the operator does differently.** After a run of wins, the next packet gets the base rate read first and the exceptional tier is not discussed. A run is recorded as a run. Doctrine Rule 15 (quarter-Kelly is a ceiling the operator cannot see until the register has measured the edge) is the sizing half of that discipline, and Portfolio Construction carries its arithmetic.

## Chapter 10 — Recency and anchoring

**Recency** is the overweighting of recent experience in judging what comes next. Surveyed expectations of future stock returns rise after strong past returns and fall after weak ones, which is the opposite of what valuation-based models of expected return say *(Greenwood and Shleifer, six survey series of investor expectations, 1963–2011; cited 6 November 2026)*. Recency bias is extrapolation, and for a trader it has two faces. In a trend it is useful: extrapolation is roughly what trend-following systems do on purpose, and it pays when trends persist. At a turn it is ruinous: the most recent regime is the one most confidently extrapolated just as it ends.

The Doctrine's Section 5.4 calls transitions "the danger zone" for this reason. Its dials carry a Transition or Flip-zone state with the card's most restrictive rules. A trader whose expectations are anchored on the last three months will size for a world that has just stopped.

Recency also shapes which failure mode the trader is guarding against. After a period of chop, the trader guards against overtrading and misses the trend when it arrives. After a trend, the trader guards against missing the move and overtrades the chop that follows. The guard is always set for the last war. That is an argument for a fixed checklist, which does not have a memory, over a judgment, which does.

**Anchoring** is the tendency for an arbitrary number in view to pull an estimate toward it. The classic demonstrations used numbers with no relevance at all *(Tversky and Kahneman's laboratory demonstrations, 1974; cited 6 November 2026)*. Three anchors matter in trading.

- **The entry price.** This is the reference point of Chapter 7, and the most consequential anchor.
- **Prominent prior levels.** Round numbers, prior highs and the 52-week high draw attention and judgment toward them. Stocks near their 52-week highs tend to keep outperforming, consistent with investors anchoring on the high and under-reacting to news that should carry the price through it *(George and Hwang, US stocks, 1963–2001; cited 6 November 2026)*. Technical Indicators treats such attention levels as reflexive: real because other people anchor on them too.
- **The trader's own prior forecast.** Once a target has been written down it becomes an anchor, and new information is weighed by its distance from the target rather than on its merits. The library makes this third anchor easy to acquire, because every report states a read.

**Conditions.** Anchoring is strongest when the trader is uncertain and the anchor is salient. It is weakest when a structured process forces the estimate to be built from independent inputs. The packet's required fields are such a process. A stop "on the right side of the structure", as the day-trading checklist puts it, is anchored on the market's levels rather than on the entry price or on a round number of R.

**What the operator does differently.** Exits are set from structure, not from the entry price or a round number. A forecast once made is graded, not defended. A target is a field in a packet, not a belief.

## Chapter 11 — The narrative fallacy

The narrative fallacy is Taleb's name for a habit Kahneman described in other terms: fitting a coherent story to a sequence of facts, and then taking the coherence of the story as evidence that it is true *(Taleb's Black Swan, 2007; Kahneman's "what you see is all there is", 2011; cited 6 November 2026)*. Shiller extended it to markets as a cause, not only a bias: narratives spread like infections and move prices by moving behaviour *(Narrative Economics, 2019; cited 6 November 2026)*. Both readings are true at once, and that is the difficulty. Narratives do move markets, so a trader cannot ignore them. Narratives are also the trader's own most persuasive error, so a trader cannot trust them.

The best evidence on the cost of narrative thinking comes from forecasting rather than trading. Tetlock's long study of expert political forecasts found that experts who explained the world through one big idea forecast worse than those who drew on many small ones and changed their minds. The most confident narrators were the least accurate *(Tetlock, some 280 experts' forecasts gathered over about two decades, published 2005; cited 6 November 2026)*. The later forecasting tournaments found the reverse pattern among the best forecasters: frequent small updates, probabilities rather than stories, and keeping score.

**Conditions.** The narrative fallacy is most dangerous when the story is well made, the trader made it, and the evidence is ambiguous. All three hold for a trader with a research library. It is least dangerous when every story must carry a falsifier and a graded outcome. That is what the narrative register does, and Part IV reads three of the register's items as examples.

The hot-hand literature adds a caution in the other direction. For decades the belief that streaks carry information was treated as a textbook fallacy. The original basketball evidence was later shown to carry a statistical bias that hid a real, if small, hot hand *(Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky, 1985; the correction by Miller and Sanjurjo, 2018; cited 6 November 2026)*. The moral is not that streaks mean something. It is that a confident debunking is a narrative too, and that only the count settles it.

**What the operator does differently.** Every story the operator acts on carries its falsifier in the packet, and stories are graded in the narrative register like any other forecast. A loss explained by a story gets a post-mortem category before it gets an explanation.

## Chapter 12 — The Wizards' lessons as counters

The Doctrine's Part III traces its rules to the Market Wizards literature and to what the Doctrine calls the Market Wizards Alpha Framework. It finds that traders who agreed on almost nothing about markets shared an operating system, and that the operating system was mostly behavioral. Four of the framework's lessons are restated here as counters to the six mechanisms above. The restatement is the point. A lesson remembered as wisdom is applied when the trader feels like it; a lesson understood as the counter to a named mechanism is applied when the mechanism is active, which is when it is needed.

**Edge before signal** (Doctrine Rule 6) counters the narrative fallacy and overconfidence. A trade must name its edge family, say who is on the other side, and say why the edge persists. A story can produce a reason to trade, but it cannot produce a counterparty or a persistence argument, so the three edge questions screen out trades that are only stories. They also slow the overconfident trader, because an edge has to be stated in terms someone else could check.

**Asymmetry before hit rate** (Doctrine Rule 7) counters the disposition effect and loss aversion. The disposition effect is, in effect, a strategy for a high hit rate: take small gains quickly, let losses run in hope. The result is many small wins and a few large losses, the profile the Doctrine's Rule 7 describes as "a losing strategy that feels like a winning one". Optimising expectancy rather than the count of green days reverses the incentive. A small loss taken on plan is the cost of a large gain held on plan, and the hit rate is printed after the average win and loss, not before.

**Size to the opportunity** (Doctrine Rule 14) counters house money, the break-even effect and size creep. Each of those sets size by the trader's recent P&L. Sizing to the opportunity sets it by the configuration — the dials, the trust level, an independent confirmation — through a calculator that cannot see the week's result. Druckenmiller's "be a pig" is half of the lesson. The other half, which matters more to this book, is that the large size comes from the checklist and never from the mood.

**Doing nothing is a position** (Doctrine Rule 10) counters recency and overtrading in chop. The open market is not an obligation. A pass is logged with a reason, scored as a decision, and counted as restraint in the Weekly. The lumpy-year model in the Doctrine's Part VII says most of a good year is made in a minority of weeks, and the job in the others is not to lose much while waiting. Recency makes waiting feel like falling behind. The logged pass makes it a recorded choice instead.

| Mechanism (Chapters 7–11) | Its signature in a trading record | The Wizards' counter | Where the system holds it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loss aversion, disposition | Small wins, large losses; late exits | Asymmetry before hit rate | Doctrine Rules 5, 7 and 17; brackets at entry |
| House money, break-even | Size up after wins; risk up after a red morning | Size to the opportunity | Doctrine Rule 14; the tier calculator; the kill-switch ladder |
| Overconfidence | Rising turnover after a run | Size to the opportunity; the outside view | Doctrine Rule 15; Base Rates beside every conditional |

| Narrative fallacy | Positions that share one story | Edge before signal | Doctrine Rule 6; the narrative register; the heat cap |

# Part III — The System's Answer

## Chapter 13 — Rules that constrain execution, not direction

The system's answer to Parts I and II has one design principle, and it is worth stating before the parts. **The rules constrain how a view is executed. They never constrain what the view is.** The operator may be long or short, early or late, contrarian or with the crowd. No rule in the Doctrine, the register or the day-trading order says what to think about the market. Every rule says something about what happens after the thinking: how large, how far, how long, how often, and with what written down first.

The principle comes from the literature in Part II. The behavioral mechanisms do not, in the main, make views wrong. They make the handling of views wrong: the exit taken by the P&L, the size set by the week, the re-entry made out of pique. A rule that tried to correct views would be a second research system competing with the first, and the operator would rightly ignore it. A rule that corrects handling leaves the operator's judgment intact and removes the specific moments at which judgment is known to fail. This is also why the rules can be tested. A rule that constrains execution has a counterfactual that the register can compute (Appendix A).

The answer has four layers, and each is owned elsewhere. This chapter describes what each does to the behavior of Part I; the rules themselves are in the Operating Doctrine and the order that holds them.

**The Doctrine.** The Operating Doctrine's twenty-nine rules are the frame. For the four failure modes, a handful do most of the work:

- **Size.** The tier test and its calculator set size (Doctrine Rule 14). The kill-switch ladder stops trading after a bad day, week or month and allows re-entry only at the next scheduled session (Doctrine Rules 1 and 2).
- **Exits.** The exit is written before entry (Doctrine Rule 5). No add is made to a loser (Doctrine Rule 4). A counter-trend position carries a five-session time stop (Doctrine Rule 27).
- **Volatile days.** The protocol halves everything and allows action only from the list (Doctrine Rule 3).
- **Packets.** The edge questions are required (Doctrine Rule 6), and no trade happens without a packet (Doctrine Rule 18).
- **Changing the rules.** A rule changes only at its sitting, never in a drawdown (Doctrine Rule 20).

Each answers a mechanism named in Part II. The Doctrine's own Part VIII ties each rule to its Wizard origin and to the operator's history. This paper adds only the behavioral mechanism the rule interrupts, and Chapter 12's table carries the mapping.

**The register.** The register is the record that makes behavior countable. Its Decision Packets are immutable once written. They carry the regime stamp, the tier, the edge family and the written exit, and the Doctrine's Section 9.2 adds the fields this paper needs: the six post-mortem categories, the rule-break flag, the time written and the time executed, machine-drafted or operator-written, the volatile-day flag, and the reason for any pass. The register enforces what can be enforced at write time. A packet missing a required field is blocked; an exceptional tier the stamps do not support is refused. It records everything else for the post-mortem. Its behavioral function is the one Chapter 1 named: it moves the record of the gap out of the trader's head, where the story is written by the mind that made the decision, and into a ledger, where it is written before the outcome is known.

**The day-trading order's rules.** The Day-Trading Layer (DTH-1) is a tactics layer for Books B and C, not a fifth book (the Doctrine's Section 10.3). The rules filed with it on 6 November 2026, as Addendum A to Amendment #5, are the most behavioral rules the system has, because they act at the shortest horizon, where the mechanisms of Part II act fastest. As the brief for this paper lists them, they are five.

- **A resting stop.** It is placed in the market at entry, not held in the head. Chapter 8 gave the reason: an open loss keeps the old reference point alive, and a resting stop realises the loss before the break-even effect can fight it.
- **A one-line thesis.** It is written before entry. One line is short enough that it cannot be revised into a different thesis after an adverse move, which is Chapter 3's tell of attachment.
- **The 1% and 2% loss limits.** They are read here as a day's and a week's limits, the shape the Card already gives Book C; the order's own text governs their exact terms. They are the break-even effect's circuit breaker.
- **No flip inside thirty minutes.** A stopped-out long cannot be turned straight into a short. This is the rule aimed most directly at overtrading in chop and at the pique trade after a stop-out.
- **The view line.** Chapter 15 takes this one up.

The 07:00 anchor already prints the day-trade checklist as text, never with a price or a size. Since 27 October 2026 the order's manual ledger has lived outside the public repository, and the Weekly prints only its aggregates: counts, R, the rule-broken share and the largest loss in R, never a currency figure or a row (D41).

**The scorecards.** Several measures turn the record into a score, and each is designed to be read before the week's P&L.

- **The Card's scoreboard.** It prints the rolling thirteen-week expectancy in R by book and by the regime stamped at entry, with n and an interval, "accruing" below its minimum sample, and the count of rule breaks.
- **The DTH-1 ledger reader.** It prints the trades of the week and to date, the share with no rule broken, the mean and median R, the win rate and the largest loss in R.
- **The dealer scorecard and the ES ledger.** They grade the reports' own levels and leans, so the operator can see whether the read was right before asking whether the trade was.
- **Decision quality and the error corpus.** They decompose the forecasts' Brier score and collect the graded misses, and the post-mortem file each Saturday puts one miss at a time in front of the operator with the Doctrine's questions blank.

What the scorecards share is that they measure process and outcome apart. The rule-break count is printed above the result for exactly the reason Chapter 1 gave: the account cannot tell a moved stop from a wrong thesis, and the count can.

## Chapter 14 — What the rules cannot do

A paper that described only what the rules do would be making the Doctrine's case. This chapter is the other half, because a trader who believes the system has solved the behavioral problem has acquired a new narrative and with it a new failure mode. Six limits are real.

**The rules cannot make a view right.** They cap the cost of a wrong view; they do not reduce the number of wrong views. A book whose reads are no better than chance will lose its costs under perfect compliance. The rules then make the loss slow and measurable, which is worth a great deal, but it is not profit. Edge is the reports' job and the operator's. The rules only stop edge being thrown away.

**The rules cannot see a decision that is not recorded.** The register catches what passes through a packet. A trade placed outside the system — on a phone, in another account, "just to test something" — leaves no mark, and the behavioral failure modes are exactly the states in which such trades are placed. The execution layer's gates narrow this where the broker integration reaches. They do not close it, and no rule can. The defence is the operator's own commitment, and the measure is indirect: a gap between the account's result and the register's is a sign that decisions are happening off the books.

**The rules cannot prevent a rule from being argued away.** Every rule has a respectable-sounding exception: this stop is in a bad place; this volatile day is not really volatile; this add is not an add because the thesis changed. The Doctrine answers with the change ladder (Doctrine Rule 20): rules change at their sitting, never in a drawdown. That works only if the operator treats an exception argued mid-session as a rule break, logged as one, rather than as a judgment call. Narrative capture's most dangerous target is the rulebook itself.

**The rules cannot fix the incentive set by the target.** The Doctrine states the operator's aspiration openly and then refuses to let it drive sizing, because sizing to a P&L goal makes the position largest when the edge is weakest. No rule can stop a target from being felt. The thirteen-week score and the benchmark test against Book A alone and the 60/40 are the system's attempt to replace the target as the thing the operator watches. Whether it succeeds is a behavioral question, and Draft 2 should be able to answer it from the pass log and the size ratio of Chapter 2.

**The rules cannot bind a person who is not present.** Most of the operator's day is spent away from the screen, and the Doctrine treats that as the book's most valuable structural fact (its Section 2.3). The volatile-day protocol, the brackets at entry and the prepared list exist so that the decisions are taken when the operator is calm and executed when the operator is absent. A rule that requires judgment in the moment fails when the moment arrives during a meeting. The design answer is to move judgment earlier, not to add rules.

**The rules cannot measure what they prevent.** A trade not taken because the attention budget deferred it is graded as an abstention. A trade not taken because the operator felt the temptation and declined it leaves nothing. The system sees the restraint it imposes, not the restraint the operator supplies, and the second is probably the larger. That asymmetry matters for Draft 2. A falling rule-break count could mean better behavior or fewer temptations noticed, and the register alone cannot tell them apart. The pass log's one-line reasons are the only window, which is why the Doctrine asks for them.

**What the operator does differently.** The operator treats compliance as necessary and never as sufficient: a month with zero rule breaks and negative expectancy is information about the edge, not a success. The operator logs exceptions argued in the moment as breaks, and treats a gap between the account and the register as a red flag.

## Chapter 15 — The view line

Most of what an experienced trader knows is never written down. It arrives as a feeling about the tape — this rally is tired, this dip will be bought, the open will fade — and it is acted on or ignored without a record. If acted on, it is graded only through the trade, and the trade carries size, timing and exit decisions that blur the grade. If ignored, it is never graded at all. The gut is the one forecaster in the system with no scorecard, and Chapter 9's overconfidence literature says that an ungraded forecaster drifts toward overconfidence, because the memory of the calls that worked outlasts the memory of the ones that did not.

The view line is the day-trading order's answer, and this paper reads it as the most important behavioral instrument the system has added. In this paper's reading of the order, it is a dated, one-line directional call with a horizon — the instrument, the direction and the time by which it should be true — written before the session and graded against the stored bars **whether or not a trade follows**. Three properties make it work, and each is a counter to a mechanism in Part II.

**It is written before the outcome.** Hindsight cannot rewrite a call already in the ledger, so the narrative fallacy's habit of remembering a vague feeling as a precise prediction is closed off. A line written at 08:15 that says "ES lower by the close" is either right or wrong at 16:00.

**It is separated from the trade.** Because the view is graded without a position, its grade carries no size, timing or exit error. The view line is the only place in the system where the operator's raw read is measured alone. Its grade answers the question Chapter 1 said the account could not: is the gap between the read and the trade, or is the read itself the problem?

**It is cheap.** A line costs seconds, so the sample grows at the rate of the operator's opinions, not the rate of trades. A trader who takes three day trades a week may hold a dozen views. The view-line ledger reaches a usable sample several times faster than the trade ledger, and Evidence and Inference's arithmetic says that this matters more than anything else about it.

The view line also supplies the comparison Part I needs most. Set its hit rate and calibration beside the trade ledger's results on the same days, and the difference is the gap itself. If the views are right and the trades lose, the problem is execution, and the rules of Chapter 13 should bind harder. If the views are no better than chance and the trades still win, the profit came from structure — the dealer read, the setup list — and the gut should be trusted less. If both are weak, the edge is absent and no rule will create it.

**What the view line cannot do.** It cannot grade a view held too loosely to write down, and it invites a quiet form of gaming: lines written only on confident days. The defence is to write a line every session, including "no view", and to count the no-view lines, which are themselves a record of restraint. The view line is a diagnostic, not a signal. Nothing in the system trades it, and no rule cites its record until the change ladder admits it.

| Instrument | What it grades | What it cannot see |
|---|---|---|
| The trade ledger | The read, timing, size and exit together | Which of them made the result |
| The view line | The read alone, every session | Views not written down |
| The rule-break count | Compliance with written rules | Restraint the operator supplies unprompted |
| The pass log | Declined packets and their reasons | Temptations never drafted |

# Part IV — Routed Items as Behavioral Objects

## Chapter 16 — The dip-buying cohort and the rally trap

The signal-triage register routes three items to this paper. Two arrive together from the September rounds: the dip-buying cohort and the rally trap. Both appear in the register's behavioral inventory under SR-8, "bear-market rallies as structure". SR-8's market claim belongs to Tops and Bottoms and the Top & Bottom report. In a bear market, rallies are large, frequent and indistinguishable in real time from a new bull *(the Nasdaq Composite from March 2000 to October 2002, five rallies of 12 to 45 percent inside a 78 percent decline; the register's SR-8 entry, filed 19 September 2026)*. What this paper owns is the behavior on either side of those rallies: the people who buy them, and the trader who mistakes them for a bottom.

**The dip-buying cohort** is a population, not a person. It is the group of investors whose experience has taught them that buying a decline works, because in their trading lives it has. A decade and more of rising markets with fast recoveries reinforced the behavior every time it was tried. Chapter 10's recency mechanism is the core of it: expectations extrapolated from the recent past, and a rule of thumb ("buy the dip") that is really a summary of the last regime. The cohort is not foolish. In a bull market its rule is correct and profitable, and the people who refused it paid the cost the Doctrine's Rule 29 describes. The cohort's behavior becomes a hazard only at a regime change, when the rule keeps firing and the regime no longer pays it.

As a behavioral object, the cohort matters to the operator in two ways. The first is as fuel. Its buying powers the sharp rallies inside a bear, and the Positioning & Flows paper's fuel metrics are the measured version: leveraged-ETF creations on declines, retail flow into falling names, the margin balance. A rally driven by the cohort is a flow event, not a change in the regime. It ends when the cohort's capital or patience does. The second is as a mirror. The operator's own history includes the opposite bias, a short preference and a reluctance to hold the long side, and the cohort is the population on the other side of that bias. When the operator is short and the cohort is buying, the trade is a contest between two learned habits, and the market decides which regime it is in.

**The rally trap** is the trader's half of the same structure: taking a bear-market rally as the bottom, and re-entering the long side at full size on the strength of a move that is, by base rate, more likely to fail than to hold. The mechanisms are three of Part II's at once.

- **Recency.** The rally is the most recent and most vivid evidence, and it overwrites the bear that preceded it.
- **The narrative fallacy.** A rally always arrives with a story ("the Fed has turned", "the worst is priced"), and the story makes the move feel explained, and therefore durable.
- **The break-even effect.** A trader who lost on the way down feels the rally as the chance to get back to even, and takes more risk to catch it.

The rally trap is also the operator's own documented failure mode in reverse. The Doctrine records a short campaign that was right into a collapse and wrong through the recovery. That was the same misreading of a turn, made from the other side.

The system's answer is a gate and a flag. The gate is the false-bottom audit's proposal for Book A. Once the Top & Bottom top side has fired and price is below a falling 200-day average, re-entry needs the bottom composite plus confirmation, and rallies in that state are Book C material, not Book A's. The flag is the Daily Cascade's "rally inside bear" line on the 07:00 anchor. The proposed Doctrine rule words the behavioral point exactly: in a declared bear regime a rally is a mechanism, not a signal, and size should assume that a bear rally and a new bull are indistinguishable in real time. The gate's own evidence is weak so far: the audit found one confirmation element that discriminated, against a pre-registered requirement of two *(the false-bottom audit, n 16 rallies, ledger of 18 October 2026)*. The Doctrine monthly decides what follows from that. For this paper the weakness is itself the lesson. The confirmation the trader most wants — something that says this rally is the real one — is the thing the data says is hardest to have.

**What the operator does differently.** In a declared bear, a rally is traded in Book C at Book C's size, with a time stop. A move up does not reopen Book A until the gate opens. When the cohort is visibly buying, the operator reads it as flow, not as information about the regime.

## Chapter 17 — The dated public call

The third routed item is newer. Item 132, filed on 6 November 2026 with Batch 25, adds a "dated public call" entry type to the narrative register. A dated public call is a forecast posted publicly with dates attached, in the form "on this day this will happen", by someone with a following. It is captured with its claims, its dates, the forecaster's incentive tags (a position disclosed, a product sold) and its engagement at capture. Each dated claim is then scored at its expiry as a hit, a miss or unresolved. The register's first row is a fifteen-day crash calendar posted by an X account. Appendix B names its dates, because its claims are forecasts and forecasts belong only in the dated appendix. A count of such calls, both crash calls and melt-up calls, becomes an attention input to the Capitulation Count, carrying no direction.

As a behavioral object, the dated public call is the narrative fallacy in its most contagious form, and it is worth understanding why it spreads.

- **It is vivid.** It names days and numbers, and specific predictions feel more credible than vague ones, the reverse of how they should be weighed.
- **It is incentivised.** The forecaster who discloses a short position and sells a community subscription is, as the order puts it, talking the book twice over. A call that pays its author whether or not it is right is a call whose accuracy is not its purpose.
- **It is self-selecting.** The calls that go viral are the extreme ones, so the population of calls a trader sees is a biased sample of all the calls made.
- **It is unscored.** Until the register's entry type existed, nobody kept the tally, and a forecaster with one famous hit and fifty forgotten misses traded on the hit.

The danger to the operator is anchoring and narrative capture rather than direct imitation. A trader who reads a detailed crash calendar does not usually sell because of it. The calendar, though, becomes an anchor (Chapter 10): a set of dates and levels against which the next week's prints are read. It also becomes a story that the operator's own bearish temperament finds congenial. A short-biased trader who reads a viral crash call is reading a confident statement of the trader's own prior, and the overconfidence literature says that agreement from an unscored source raises confidence without raising accuracy.

The register's treatment is the right one, and it generalises. The call is captured as data about other people's attention, not as a view. It is scored at expiry, so the forecaster's record becomes visible. It enters the system only as a two-sided count, so a wave of crash calls is read as a sentiment reading, potentially a contrarian one, not as information about the crash. And the operator's own view line (Chapter 15) is the same object with no audience and no incentive: a dated, falsifiable call, scored at expiry. The dated public call is what a view line looks like when its author is paid for attention rather than accuracy. Reading the two side by side is a useful discipline.

**What the operator does differently.** A viral call is read once, as data, and its dates go into the register rather than into the operator's head. The operator writes the view line before reading the digest, not after. When a call agrees with the operator's own prior, the agreement is noted as a reason for more scrutiny, not less.
