## Preamble

The Monthly Macro Working Manual treats liquidity, inflation, and sovereign dynamics as three pillars among ten. The Daily Cascade field guide treats rate signals as inputs to intraday execution. Both are correct at their level of resolution and both compress the same underlying machine into a summary.

This paper decompresses it.

The claim justifying a dedicated document is that **rates and liquidity are not one input among many — they are the transmission medium through which almost every other input reaches asset prices.** Growth matters because it changes the policy path. Inflation matters because it changes the policy path. Fiscal deficits matter because they change the supply of duration and the quantity of system cash. Geopolitics matters because it changes energy prices, which changes inflation, which changes the policy path. Strip out the rate and liquidity channel and most macro analysis loses its mechanism.

The paper has five parts.

**Part I** establishes historical context — not as antiquarianism but because the current configuration has close precedents, and the precedents carry information. The 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord in particular is more relevant to 2026 than almost anything from the past thirty years.

**Part II** is the reference core: a complete inventory of the levers that move rates and liquidity, organized by who controls them. Six lever families, roughly forty distinct instruments. Most macro commentary discusses three or four of them.

**Part III** maps transmission — how a movement in any lever propagates to asset prices, and where the chain breaks.

**Part IV** builds forward scenarios at four horizons: twelve months, thirty-six months, five years, and ten years. Each is probability-weighted, each specifies its triggers and falsifiers, and each explicitly incorporates political dynamics rather than treating policy as a technocratic process insulated from politics. In 2026 that insulation cannot be assumed.

**Part V** connects everything back to the tactical signals across the five reports — Disruptive Themes, Monthly Macro, Top & Bottom, Alternative Asset, and Daily Cascade — explaining what each signal measures, which scenario it discriminates, and how much lead time it provides.

### Where We Are Standing

Every scenario in Part IV is anchored to conditions as of late August 2026. Stating them explicitly makes the paper auditable later.

The federal funds target range is **3.50 to 3.75 percent**, unchanged since December 2025 following three consecutive cuts in late 2025. The July 29 FOMC held rates by a **9-3 vote**, with three dissents favoring a hike. Kevin Warsh has chaired since May 2026 and has deliberately compressed the statement, reduced forward guidance, established five internal task forces including one on communications, declined to submit his own dot to the Summary of Economic Projections, and floated reducing the number of FOMC meetings per year.

Inflation is disinflating at the headline and sticky underneath. **July CPI came in at 3.4 percent year-over-year with core at 2.5 percent**, both down a tenth from June. **July PCE ran 3.7 percent year-over-year**, slightly hotter than expected. At Jackson Hole on August 28, Warsh said the recent prints, while better than expected, did not indicate meaningful improvement in underlying inflation trends. Markets subsequently priced roughly a **50 percent probability of a September hike and above 70 percent of a hike by December**.

The curve is doing something specific and important. The **two-year sits near 4.21 percent, the ten-year near 4.65, and the thirty-year near 5.17 to 5.26 percent.** The ten-year is at roughly the 98th percentile of its five-year range while the two-year is near the 57th. **August's thirty-year auction cleared at 5.22 percent — the highest awarded yield at that tenor since 2001.** The front end is responding to disinflation and a softening labor market; the long end is repricing fiscal supply and term premium. This is a bear steepener driven by the back end, not a conventional hiking cycle.

Credit has registered almost none of it. **High yield spreads sit near 251 basis points, approximately the fifth percentile of their five-year range.** Investment grade is near the thirtieth. The gap between what the long end is saying about the cost of capital and what credit spreads are saying about the price of risk is the single most conspicuous inconsistency in the current market.

The balance sheet story has already turned. In December 2025 the FOMC stated it would **initiate purchases of shorter-term Treasury securities as needed to maintain an ample supply of reserves** — the formal end of quantitative tightening and the beginning of reserve-management operations. Separately, **Treasury has expanded its debt buyback program**, which has itself become a subject of commentary about fiscal stress and dollar risk.

The labor market has begun to soften. July payrolls disappointed and were characterized in some accounts as a contraction. Unemployment sits near 4.3 percent, consistent with the June SEP projection.

Politically, the President has publicly praised Warsh while criticizing other Fed officials, some of whom he has suggested hold political motivations. The administration's preference for lower rates is explicit and has been for some time.

That is the board. Now the history that explains how the pieces got arranged this way.

## Part I — Historical Context

### Why History Matters Here Specifically

Monetary regimes are institutional arrangements, not natural laws. They are constructed, they persist for decades, and they end — usually in response to a crisis that the existing arrangement handled badly. Each regime generates its own set of reliable relationships, and those relationships stop working when the regime changes.

A reader who learned markets in one regime and did not notice the transition will apply relationships that no longer hold. This has happened to entire cohorts of investors repeatedly. The specific value of the history below is that it teaches what a regime transition looks like from the inside, which is the thing that is hardest to see in real time.

### 1907–1913: The Fed Exists Because Private Rescue Failed

The Panic of 1907 was arrested by J.P. Morgan personally organizing a private-sector liquidity pool. It worked, and the fact that a single aging banker was the country's lender of last resort was correctly understood as intolerable. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 created a public institution to perform the function.

The founding purpose was **elastic currency** — the capacity to expand the money supply during a liquidity panic and contract it afterward. Everything the Fed does today is a descendant of that function. Note what was *not* in the original mandate: full employment, price stability targets, financial asset support. Those accreted later.

### 1929–1951: Depression, War Finance, and the Accord

The Fed's performance during the Depression is the founding trauma of modern central banking, and Ben Bernanke's academic work on it directly shaped the 2008 response. The compressed lesson: the Fed allowed the money supply to contract sharply while banks failed, converting a severe recession into a catastrophe.

More relevant to 2026 is what came next. **From 1942 through 1951, the Federal Reserve pegged Treasury yields** to hold down the government's cost of financing the war — roughly three-eighths of a percent on bills and two and a half percent on long bonds. This is yield curve control, and it was not a theoretical exercise. **The Fed subordinated monetary policy entirely to the Treasury's financing needs for nine years.**

The arrangement broke when Korean War inflation made the peg untenable. The Fed wanted to raise rates; the Treasury and the White House refused. The dispute escalated to a genuine institutional confrontation, resolved in the **Treasury-Fed Accord of March 1951**, which freed the Fed from the obligation to support Treasury prices and established the operational independence that has been assumed ever since.

**This is the most relevant historical episode for the current moment, and it is worth sitting with.** The Accord is seventy-five years old. It was a negotiated settlement between two arms of government under specific political conditions, not a constitutional guarantee. It established a norm. Norms hold until they do not. Any framework that treats Fed independence as a permanent structural feature rather than a contingent institutional arrangement is making an assumption that the historical record does not support.

### 1965–1982: The Great Inflation and the Volcker Reset

Inflation rose through the late 1960s and 1970s under a combination of fiscal expansion, oil shocks, the collapse of Bretton Woods in 1971, and a Federal Reserve that accommodated because the political cost of not accommodating was high.

The specific failure worth understanding is **stop-go policy**. The Fed tightened when inflation rose, then eased when unemployment rose, repeatedly, without ever holding restriction long enough to break the inflation. Each cycle ratcheted expectations higher, because households and firms learned that the Fed would blink. By the late 1970s, inflation expectations were unanchored and the Phillips curve tradeoff had deteriorated badly — each round of disinflation cost more unemployment than the last.

Paul Volcker's tenure beginning in 1979 resolved it through brute force: the funds rate went above twenty percent, two recessions followed, unemployment reached nearly eleven percent, and inflation broke. The political cost was enormous, and Volcker was subject to sustained public and congressional attack.

**Two lessons transfer.** First, the cost of disinflation depends almost entirely on whether expectations are anchored — anchored expectations allow disinflation without severe recession, and unanchored expectations do not. Second, **credibility is the asset that anchors expectations, it is built slowly, and it is spent quickly.** Every subsequent Fed has understood that its most valuable possession is the market's belief that it will do what it says.

### 1983–2007: The Great Moderation and the Birth of the Put

Two decades of declining inflation, declining rate volatility, and increasingly confident monetary policy. Alan Greenspan's Fed responded to the 1987 crash, the 1998 LTCM collapse, and the 2000 dot-com unwind with liquidity provision and rate cuts, establishing the market's expectation of a **"Greenspan put"** — the belief that the Fed would intervene to arrest asset price declines.

Whether this was correct policy remains contested. What is not contested is that the expectation became embedded, changed risk-taking behavior across the system, and has never been dislodged. Every subsequent chair has inherited it.

### 2008–2015: The Regime Break

The financial crisis produced the single largest structural change in the machinery this paper describes.

**Quantitative easing** began: the Fed purchased Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities on a scale without precedent, expanding its balance sheet by trillions. The intent was to compress long-term yields when the short rate was already at zero.

**The operating framework changed from scarce reserves to ample reserves.** Before 2008, the Fed steered the funds rate by managing a small quantity of reserves through daily open-market operations — a corridor system. After 2008, reserves were abundant, and the Fed began paying **interest on reserve balances**, using that administered rate as a floor to steer money market rates. This is a floor system, and it means **the word "reserves" describes a fundamentally different thing before and after 2008.** Analysis importing pre-2008 intuitions about reserve quantities into the current system is confused at the foundation.

**Zero interest rate policy** persisted for seven years, and **forward guidance** became a primary policy tool — the Fed managing expectations about the future path as a substitute for further cuts it could not make.

The **2013 taper tantrum** demonstrated the new regime's fragility. Bernanke's suggestion that asset purchases might slow produced a violent global selloff in bonds and emerging markets. Nothing had actually changed; the mere signal of a change in the *rate of change* of purchases was sufficient. This established that in a QE regime, communications carry the force that rate moves carried previously.

### 2015–2019: The Normalization Attempt and Its Limit

The Fed raised rates gradually from 2015 and began balance sheet runoff in 2017. The episode worth studying is **September 2019**, when reserves fell below the ample threshold without warning and overnight repo rates spiked violently in a matter of hours. The Fed intervened with emergency operations and resumed balance sheet growth.

**The critical detail is that nobody — including the Fed, which had the best data — identified the threshold in advance.** Reserve scarcity gave essentially no warning. The Standing Repo Facility was subsequently created as a backstop specifically to prevent a recurrence, and that facility exists today. But the underlying lesson stands: the ample level is discovered by breaching it.

### 2020: Everything, At Once

March 2020 produced a dysfunction in the Treasury market itself — the world's deepest and most liquid asset became difficult to transact in size, as leveraged relative-value positions unwound and dealers hit balance sheet constraints. The Fed responded with unlimited purchases, emergency facilities under Section 13(3) covering corporate credit including high yield for the first time, expanded FX swap lines to foreign central banks, and regulatory relief on the supplementary leverage ratio to free dealer balance sheet capacity.

Simultaneously, fiscal policy delivered direct transfers to households on a scale not previously attempted in peacetime.

**Two structural consequences persist.** The Fed's willingness to purchase corporate credit established a precedent that credit markets now partially price. And the combination of monetary and fiscal expansion delivered directly into household bank accounts — rather than into bank reserves as in 2008 — is the mechanism most credibly identified as the difference between a decade of QE without inflation and eighteen months of pandemic response with a great deal of it.

### 2021–2023: Transitory, Then the Fastest Tightening in Forty Years

Inflation accelerated through 2021. The Fed, the majority of professional forecasters, and the market as priced concluded it would fade as supply chains normalized. **The error was near-universal, which is the instructive part** — consensus provided no protection whatever.

By 2022 the Fed was far behind and tightened at the fastest pace since the Volcker era, moving the funds rate from zero to above five percent inside eighteen months while beginning balance sheet reduction.

**March 2023** demonstrated a risk category supervision had not prioritized. Silicon Valley Bank failed from interest-rate risk in a held-to-maturity securities portfolio combined with concentrated uninsured deposits — not from credit losses. The run was coordinated digitally and executed through mobile banking, compressing into hours a process that historically took days. The Fed created the Bank Term Funding Program, which lent against securities at par rather than market value, neutralizing the unrealized-loss problem by fiat.

The **2023–2024 disinflation then occurred without the severe labor market damage that essentially every model said was required.** This was the second consecutive failure of the Phillips curve framework, in the opposite direction from the first.

### 2024–2026: Normalization, Reversal, and a New Chair

Rates came down from the peak through 2025, with three consecutive cuts landing the range at 3.50 to 3.75 percent in December 2025. That same meeting announced purchases of shorter-dated Treasuries as needed to maintain ample reserves — **the effective end of quantitative tightening**, roughly six years after the September 2019 lesson about how it ends badly.

Kevin Warsh became chair in May 2026, nominated by an administration with an explicit preference for lower rates. Warsh's public record favors a smaller balance sheet, lighter bank regulation, and less forward guidance. He has argued that artificial-intelligence-driven productivity gains could lower inflation and create room to ease — a thesis that, if it holds, permits accommodation justified on data rather than on politics.

The current configuration is the result: **a policy rate pinned by a divided committee, a long end repricing fiscal supply, a Treasury issuing heavily and buying back opportunistically, credit spreads at cycle tights, and a chair whose institutional position sits at the intersection of a hawkish committee and a dovish White House.**

### What the History Teaches

Five compressed lessons carry forward into Parts II through V.

**Regimes end, and the transition is invisible from inside.** Every arrangement described above felt permanent to the people operating within it. The relationships each generated stopped working when the arrangement changed.

**Independence is a norm, not a law.** The 1942–1951 peg and the 1951 Accord establish both that subordination has happened and that the current arrangement was negotiated rather than given.

**Credibility is the binding constraint.** It determines whether disinflation is cheap or catastrophic, and it is the only reason a central bank can tolerate an inflation overshoot without a wage-price spiral.

**Plumbing failures arrive without warning.** September 2019 and March 2020 both moved from normal to acute inside a single trading session. The instruments that give advance notice are the money-market spreads, not the aggregates.

**The mechanism matters more than the aggregate.** A decade of QE produced no inflation; eighteen months of fiscal transfers produced a great deal. Both expanded the money supply. The difference was where the money went.

## Part II — The Levers

### How to Read This Section

What follows is an inventory of every instrument that materially moves interest rates or system liquidity, organized by who controls it. Roughly fifty instruments across six families. Most macro commentary discusses four or five of them, which is why most macro commentary is surprised by moves originating in the other forty-five.

Each lever is described by **what it is**, **who pulls it**, **how it transmits**, **how fast it works**, and **its current setting** as of late August 2026.

A structural point before the inventory. The levers divide into two categories that behave completely differently. **Price levers** set the cost of money — the funds rate, the administered rates, the discount rate. They work through expectations and repricing, they are fast, and they are highly visible. **Quantity levers** change the amount of money and duration in the system — balance sheet operations, Treasury issuance, regulatory capacity. They work through supply and demand for specific instruments, they are slower, they are far less visible, and **they are where most of the surprises come from.**

The current regime is unusual because the price lever is pinned by a divided committee while several quantity levers are moving significantly. That combination is precisely what produces the bear steepener described in the preamble.

---

### Family A — The Federal Reserve's Price Levers

**A1. The federal funds target range.** The headline policy rate, set by FOMC vote at each meeting. It is a *range* rather than a point because in an ample-reserves system the Fed cannot pin an exact rate; it establishes boundaries and steers within them. Transmission is immediate to money market rates, fast to the front end of the Treasury curve, and progressively slower and weaker further out. *Current: 3.50–3.75 percent, unchanged since December 2025.*

**A2. Interest on reserve balances (IORB).** The rate the Fed pays banks on reserves held at the Fed. In a floor system this is the primary steering instrument — no bank will lend in the market below what it can earn risk-free at the Fed, so IORB establishes the effective floor for money market rates. **The Board of Governors sets it administratively, not by FOMC vote**, which means it can be adjusted between meetings and independently of the target range. The Fed has used small IORB adjustments as technical corrections when the funds rate drifted within its range. This is a genuine and underappreciated degree of freedom.

**A3. The overnight reverse repo offering rate.** The rate paid to money market funds and other non-bank counterparties who park cash at the Fed overnight. It functions as a sub-floor for entities that cannot hold reserves. Adjusting it relative to bill yields changes the incentive for cash to sit at the Fed versus flow into Treasury bills — **a direct lever on where system cash resides.**

**A4. The primary credit rate.** The discount window's price. Historically set above the target range to make the window a penalty facility, reinforcing the stigma that has limited its usefulness. Post-2023 the Fed has worked to reduce that stigma. Lowering the spread would make the window a more usable backstop and would change the shape of stress episodes.

**A5. The Standing Repo Facility rate.** The fixed rate at which eligible counterparties can borrow cash against Treasury collateral. It functions as a ceiling on repo rates — no one should pay more in the market than the SRF charges. Its existence caps the September-2019 failure mode. *Current: available, usage minimal.*

**The relationship among these five is the corridor.** IORB and the RRP rate form the floor, the SRF forms the ceiling, and market rates trade between them. Watching where SOFR sits within that corridor is the single best real-time read on whether cash is abundant or scarce, and it is the subject of one of the most important tactical signals in Part V.

---

### Family B — The Federal Reserve's Quantity Levers

**B1. Asset purchases.** Outright buying of Treasuries or mortgage-backed securities, expanding the balance sheet and creating reserves. Transmission works through the duration channel — removing duration from private hands compresses term premium — and through the signaling channel, since purchases communicate policy intent. *Current: not active as stimulus, but see B5.*

**B2. Balance sheet runoff and redemption caps.** Allowing maturing securities to roll off without reinvestment, shrinking the balance sheet passively. The **cap** specifies the maximum monthly runoff; anything above the cap gets reinvested. Adjusting caps is a dial rather than a switch, which lets the Fed modulate the pace without announcing a policy change. *Current: quantitative tightening effectively concluded as of December 2025.*

**B3. Reinvestment composition.** When the Fed reinvests, it chooses maturities. Reinvesting maturing proceeds into bills rather than long bonds shortens the portfolio and pushes duration back to the private sector, which steepens the curve. Reinvesting into long bonds does the reverse. **This is a curve-shaping tool that requires no announcement and receives almost no attention.**

**B4. Asset mix between Treasuries and MBS.** The Fed has stated a long-run preference for a Treasury-only portfolio. The pace at which MBS runs off relative to Treasuries directly affects mortgage spreads and therefore housing, independent of any change in the policy rate.

**B5. Reserve-management purchases.** Buying short-dated Treasuries specifically to keep reserves ample as currency in circulation and other liabilities grow. **This is technically not stimulus and the Fed is careful to say so, but the balance sheet still expands and reserves still increase.** The December 2025 announcement initiated exactly this. The distinction between "reserve management" and "QE" is real in intent and genuinely blurry in effect, and how the market chooses to interpret it is itself a variable.

**B6. The Standing Repo Facility.** Discussed above as a rate; as a quantity lever it provides elastic cash against Treasury collateral on demand, converting a potential funding crisis into a facility drawdown. Usage is a stress signal.

**B7. The FIMA repo facility.** Allows foreign central banks to obtain dollars against their Treasury holdings without selling them into the market. Prevents forced foreign selling from becoming a Treasury market event.

**B8. FX swap lines.** Dollar liquidity provided to selected foreign central banks, who lend it onward to their domestic banks. This is the mechanism that arrests global dollar funding stress, and it was decisive in both 2008 and 2020. It is also, quietly, one of the most geopolitically significant instruments the Fed possesses, since access is discretionary.

**B9. Section 13(3) emergency facilities.** Broad authority to lend against a wide range of collateral in "unusual and exigent circumstances," requiring Treasury approval. This is the authority under which corporate credit facilities were created in 2020. **Its existence is priced into credit spreads even when unused.**

**B10. The discount window.** The original lender-of-last-resort function, constrained by stigma rather than by capacity.

### Family C — The Federal Reserve's Communication Levers

These are not soft. In a regime where the market prices a path rather than a point, communication moves more money than most rate decisions.

**C1. Statement language.** Every adjective is negotiated. The shift from "somewhat elevated" to "elevated" is a policy signal. **Warsh has deliberately shortened the statement**, which reduces the number of signals available and structurally raises uncertainty.

**C2. The Summary of Economic Projections and the dot plot.** Quarterly forecasts from each participant. The median dot has functioned as de facto guidance. **Warsh declined to submit his own projection**, which is a meaningful departure — it removes the chair's dot from the median and signals discomfort with the instrument itself.

**C3. The press conference.** Where the chair's framing is established and where most post-meeting repricing occurs.

**C4. Speeches and testimony.** Individual officials' remarks, especially from the chair, vice chair, and the New York Fed president. Jackson Hole in particular functions as a venue for framework-level signals; Warsh's August 28 remarks moved September hike pricing to roughly even.

**C5. Meeting minutes.** Released three weeks after each meeting, revealing the distribution of views behind a vote.

**C6. Meeting frequency.** **Warsh has floated reducing the number of FOMC meetings.** This is a structural lever disguised as an administrative one — fewer meetings means fewer scheduled decision points, longer intervals of uncertainty, and greater weight on each event.

**C7. Framework reviews.** Periodic reassessments of the policy strategy itself. The 2020 review introduced average inflation targeting; a subsequent revision could change the reaction function materially.

---

### Family D — The Treasury's Levers

**This family is systematically underweighted in macro analysis and has become more consequential than several Fed levers.**

**D1. Gross issuance volume.** How much debt Treasury sells. Determined by the deficit and by refinancing needs. Larger supply, all else equal, requires higher yields to clear.

**D2. Maturity composition — the bill share.** **This is the most powerful and least discussed lever in the entire inventory.** Treasury chooses whether to fund itself with short-dated bills or long-dated coupons. Bills are absorbed by money market funds using cash that would otherwise sit at the Fed's reverse repo facility, so heavy bill issuance draws down RRP and is relatively liquidity-neutral for markets. Coupon issuance requires duration buyers — pensions, insurers, foreign officials, leveraged funds — and competes directly with other duration assets, pushing long yields up. **Shifting the mix toward bills eases financial conditions at the long end without any Fed action.** The 2023 shift toward bill-heavy issuance was a significant market event that received a fraction of the attention a comparable Fed decision would have.

**D3. The Treasury General Account target.** Treasury chooses how much cash to hold at the Fed. Building the balance drains reserves from the banking system; spending it down adds them. The swings around tax dates and debt-ceiling episodes produce liquidity effects comparable in magnitude to asset purchase programs, driven by a cash-management decision.

**D4. Buybacks.** Treasury repurchasing off-the-run securities, reintroduced in recent years for liquidity support and cash management. **Treasury has expanded this program in 2026.** Buybacks can improve liquidity in less-traded issues and, at sufficient scale, function as duration removal — the same economic effect as central bank purchases, executed by the fiscal authority. The commentary linking the expanded program to debt-crisis and dollar concerns reflects exactly this ambiguity about intent.

**D5. The Quarterly Refunding Announcement.** Treasury's scheduled statement of how much it will issue and at what maturities, accompanied by recommendations from the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee. **This is a scheduled, market-moving event that most investors ignore.** It contains the forward path of D1 through D4.

**D6. TIPS issuance share.** How much inflation-protected debt to sell. Affects breakeven pricing and the liquidity premium embedded in TIPS.

**D7. Auction sizing and schedule.** Individual auction sizes are adjusted incrementally, and the pattern of adjustments telegraphs the direction of the funding strategy.

**D8. Cash management bills.** Ad hoc short-dated issuance to bridge cash needs, often around tax dates.

---

### Family E — Regulatory Levers

These change the *capacity* of the private system to intermediate, which determines how much stress a given shock produces. They are controlled by the Fed in its supervisory role, by other bank regulators, and by the SEC for money funds.

**E1. The supplementary leverage ratio.** A capital requirement calculated against total assets without risk-weighting, which means holding Treasuries consumes the same capital as holding risky assets. **This directly constrains dealer balance sheet capacity to intermediate the Treasury market.** Temporary SLR relief in 2020 was a significant intervention. Proposals to permanently exclude Treasuries and reserves from the calculation would materially increase market-making capacity — an easing of financial conditions accomplished entirely through regulation. **Warsh's stated preference for lighter bank regulation makes this a live lever.**

**E2. Liquidity coverage ratio and high-quality liquid asset definitions.** These determine how many reserves and Treasuries banks must hold, which sets the *demand* side of the reserve equation. Changing the definitions changes the ample-reserves threshold — meaning a regulatory decision can move the level at which a funding crisis begins.

**E3. The G-SIB surcharge.** Additional capital required of globally systemic banks, calculated in part from year-end balance sheet size, which produces the well-documented pattern of dealers shrinking balance sheets into year-end and repo markets tightening at turn-of-year.

**E4. Stress tests and the stress capital buffer.** Determine required capital and therefore lending capacity. The severity of the scenarios is a discretionary choice.

**E5. Money market fund regulation.** The 2023 reforms changed liquidity fee and redemption gate mechanics for institutional prime funds, pushing assets toward government funds, which in turn concentrated cash in the reverse repo facility and Treasury bills. **A regulatory decision reshaped where trillions of dollars of short-term cash sits.**

**E6. Basel endgame implementation.** Pending capital rules whose calibration affects credit availability and dealer capacity.

**E7. Reserve requirements.** Reduced to zero in 2020 and largely a historical artifact in an ample-reserves system, but the authority remains.

---

### Family F — Fiscal and Political Levers

**F1. The deficit.** Determines gross issuance and therefore duration supply. Sustained deficits at levels historically associated with war or recession, during an expansion, are the structural driver behind the long-end repricing described in the preamble.

**F2. The debt ceiling.** A binding ceiling forces Treasury to spend down the TGA, injecting liquidity, then rebuild it violently once resolved, draining liquidity. **This produces some of the largest short-horizon liquidity swings in the system and is driven entirely by a political process with no economic logic.**

**F3. Tax policy.** Changes both the deficit and the timing of receipts, which drives TGA dynamics around filing dates.

**F4. Spending composition.** Direct transfers to households transmit to demand and inflation far more powerfully than reserve creation, as the contrast between 2009–2019 and 2020–2022 demonstrates.

**F5. Appointments.** The chair, vice chair, vice chair for supervision, and governors are presidential appointments requiring Senate confirmation. **Over a full term, an administration can substantially reshape the committee.** The current chair was appointed by an administration with an explicit preference for lower rates.

**F6. Political pressure.** Public criticism, threats to authority, and pressure on individual officials. This operates through two channels — direct influence on decisions, and indirect influence on market perception of Fed independence, which affects term premium and the dollar regardless of whether decisions actually change. **The second channel is the more important one for asset prices.**

**F7. Legislative changes to the Fed's mandate or structure.** Low probability in any given year, non-trivial over a decade, and the tail that would matter most.

---

### Family G — Private and Global Levers

The Fed and Treasury set conditions; private actors determine what happens within them.

**G1. Bank credit creation.** Banks create deposits when they lend. This is the largest single source of money creation in the system and it is a private-sector decision driven by loan demand, credit standards, and profitability. The Senior Loan Officer survey is the window into it.

**G2. Money market fund allocation.** Several trillion dollars choosing among the reverse repo facility, Treasury bills, and private repo. The allocation is a mechanical yield-seeking decision made by a small number of very large managers, and it determines whether Treasury's bill issuance is absorbed painlessly or drains bank reserves.

**G3. Dealer balance sheet capacity.** Primary dealers intermediate the Treasury market, and their capacity is constrained by regulation (E1, E3) and by internal risk limits. When capacity is exhausted, Treasury market liquidity deteriorates sharply — the March 2020 mechanism.

**G4. Foreign official demand.** Central banks and sovereign funds buying or selling Treasuries. Structurally declining as a share of the market, and the subject of persistent narratives that have historically overstated its importance.

**G5. Stablecoin reserve holdings.** **A genuinely new lever.** Large dollar-stablecoin issuers hold substantial short-dated Treasury portfolios as reserves against their liabilities. Growth in stablecoin float creates incremental bill demand; contraction forces liquidation. This channel did not meaningfully exist five years ago, is not captured in any traditional liquidity framework, and connects crypto-market flows directly to front-end Treasury demand. It is the core of the monetary-architecture factor added to Disruptive Themes in August 2026.

**G6. Pension and insurance duration demand.** Liability-driven investors buy long duration when yields rise, providing a natural stabilizer at the long end. The 2022 UK gilt crisis demonstrated that when these investors use leverage, the stabilizer inverts into an accelerant.

**G7. Corporate issuance and buybacks.** Corporations are large borrowers and large buyers of their own equity. Issuance conditions in credit markets — whether deals can be placed at all — are a faster stress signal than spread levels.

**G8. Foreign central bank policy.** The Bank of Japan's normalization removes the anchor from global funding costs and pulls Japanese capital home from foreign bond markets. The ECB and PBOC similarly affect global duration demand and dollar dynamics.

### The Levers Currently in Motion

Of the roughly fifty instruments above, the ones actively moving as of August 2026 are worth naming, because they define the regime:

- **The policy rate is pinned** by a 9-3 committee split, with market pricing near even odds of a September hike and above seventy percent by December.
- **Quantitative tightening has ended** and reserve-management bill purchases have begun — a quiet expansion of the balance sheet that is not called stimulus.
- **Treasury is issuing heavily into a large deficit** and has **expanded buybacks**, with the long end repricing supply and term premium.
- **Communications have been deliberately reduced**, raising rate volatility structurally and shifting weight onto scheduled events.
- **Regulatory easing is a live possibility** under a chair who favors it, which would expand dealer capacity and ease conditions without any rate decision.
- **Political pressure on the committee is explicit and public**, operating primarily through the term-premium and dollar channels.
- **Stablecoin bill demand is growing** as a new and largely unmodeled source of front-end absorption.

**The single most important structural observation: the price lever is stuck while multiple quantity levers are moving in opposite directions.** Reserve-management purchases and potential regulatory easing are loosening; heavy coupon supply and term premium repricing are tightening. The net effect is a curve that steepens rather than shifts, which is a different regime from anything in the post-2008 experience and closer to the 1951–1965 period than to anything most current market participants have traded.

### The Five Levers That Matter Most

The inventory above is comprehensive; it is not weighted. Five instruments carry disproportionate influence in the current regime, and each deserves fuller treatment than a catalog entry allows.

---

#### Lever 1 — Treasury's bill-versus-coupon mix (D2)

**Why it dominates.** Every dollar the government borrows must be absorbed by someone. Who absorbs it, and what they give up to do so, is determined almost entirely by maturity choice.

A **bill** is a money-market instrument. Its natural buyers are money market funds, corporate treasurers, and foreign reserve managers holding operating cash. These buyers are choosing between a bill, a repo, and the Fed's reverse repo facility. They are not choosing between a bill and a corporate bond or an equity. **Bill issuance therefore competes for idle cash rather than for risk capital**, and when idle cash is abundant, bills are absorbed with almost no effect on other asset prices.

A **coupon** — a two-year, ten-year, or thirty-year note or bond — is a duration instrument. Its natural buyers are pensions, insurers, foreign officials, index funds, and leveraged relative-value accounts. These buyers are explicitly choosing between government duration and other duration: corporate bonds, mortgages, and by extension the discount rate applied to equities. **Coupon issuance competes directly for the capital that prices every long-duration asset.**

**The mechanical consequence.** Shifting a hundred billion dollars of financing from coupons to bills removes a hundred billion of duration supply from the market. That is the same economic operation as a central bank buying a hundred billion of duration — quantitative easing by another name, executed by the fiscal authority, requiring no vote, no announcement beyond a routine refunding statement, and attracting a fraction of the scrutiny.

**Why it is constrained now.** The maneuver works when there is idle cash to absorb the bills. The reverse repo facility provided roughly two trillion dollars of exactly that, and it has been substantially drawn down. **The easy absorption capacity has been consumed.** Incremental financing increasingly has to be done in duration, which is the mechanical core of the current long-end repricing.

**What to watch:** the bill share in each Quarterly Refunding Announcement, and the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee's recommendations, which frequently telegraph the direction a quarter early.

---

#### Lever 2 — The Treasury General Account (D3)

**Why it dominates.** It is the largest single swing factor in weekly system liquidity, it is driven by cash management and politics rather than by monetary policy, and its calendar is known in advance.

**The mechanics.** The TGA is the government's checking account at the Fed. When taxpayers pay taxes or investors buy newly issued debt, money moves from private bank accounts into the TGA. Bank reserves fall by the same amount. The cash has not been destroyed; it has been moved to a place where it does no work in the private financial system. When the government spends, the flow reverses and reserves rise.

**The magnitudes are large enough to matter.** Swings of several hundred billion dollars around tax dates and debt-ceiling resolutions are routine. That is comparable in size to meaningful phases of asset purchase programs, occurring on a quarterly cycle, driven by the tax calendar.

**The debt-ceiling amplification.** When the ceiling binds, Treasury cannot issue and must fund operations by drawing down the TGA — a large, sustained liquidity injection that has, in several episodes, coincided with strong risk-asset performance. When the ceiling is raised, Treasury rebuilds the balance rapidly through heavy issuance, draining liquidity fast. **The full cycle is a monetary event with no monetary cause.**

**What to watch:** the daily TGA balance in the Daily Treasury Statement, the four-week rate of change, the tax calendar, and any debt-ceiling timeline.

---

#### Lever 3 — Interest on reserve balances (A2)

**Why it dominates.** It is the actual steering instrument of monetary policy in the current framework, it is set administratively rather than by committee vote, and almost nobody outside funding desks watches it.

**The mechanics.** In an ample-reserves system, the Fed cannot control the funds rate by adjusting reserve quantity — there are too many reserves for marginal changes to matter. Instead it sets the rate it pays on reserves, which establishes an arbitrage floor. A bank will not lend in the market below what it earns risk-free at the Fed.

**Why the administrative distinction matters.** The FOMC votes on the target *range*. **The Board of Governors sets IORB within that range, and can adjust it between meetings.** The Fed has used small technical adjustments to correct drift in the effective funds rate. This is a genuine degree of freedom that operates outside the meeting calendar and outside the political attention that surrounds rate decisions.

**The spread to watch is IORB against SOFR.** When SOFR trades below IORB, cash is abundant. When it pushes above, the floor is failing, which means reserves are scarce relative to collateral. That spread is the highest-value single number in the plumbing complex.

---

#### Lever 4 — The supplementary leverage ratio (E1)

**Why it dominates.** It determines how much capacity the dealer system has to intermediate the Treasury market, and dealer capacity is the binding constraint in every market-structure failure of the past decade.

**The mechanics.** The SLR requires capital against total assets without adjusting for risk. Holding a Treasury — the safest asset in the system — consumes the same leverage capacity as holding a risky loan. **This makes market-making in Treasuries expensive precisely in proportion to the size of the position**, which means dealer willingness to absorb selling pressure declines exactly when the market most needs it.

**The 2020 demonstration.** Temporary SLR relief was granted specifically because dealer capacity had been overwhelmed. The relief expired, the constraint returned, and the Treasury market has grown substantially larger since while dealer balance sheets have not grown proportionately.

**Why it is live in 2026.** Proposals to permanently exclude Treasuries and reserves from the calculation have been under discussion. **Adopting them would be a significant easing of financial conditions accomplished entirely through regulation, with no rate decision and no balance-sheet expansion.** A chair on record favoring lighter bank regulation makes this a real possibility, and it is the kind of change that would be announced through a supervisory channel rather than an FOMC statement — meaning it could easily be underpriced.

**What to watch:** proposed rulemakings from the banking agencies, dealer balance sheet data in the Fed's primary dealer statistics, and Treasury market liquidity metrics.

---

#### Lever 5 — Communication itself (Family C)

**Why it dominates.** In a regime where markets price a path rather than a point, the description of future policy moves more capital than the current setting.

**The Warsh change is a genuine regime shift and is underappreciated.** Shortening the statement, declining to submit a dot, floating fewer meetings, and reducing forward guidance are not stylistic preferences. They systematically transfer policy work from the expectations channel to the realized-move channel.

**Three consequences follow mechanically.** **Rate volatility rises structurally**, because the market has less information with which to anticipate and must reprice more violently on each data release. **Scheduled events carry more weight** — each CPI print, each payroll report, each speech becomes a larger repricing risk. And **the market's implied policy path becomes noisier and less reliable** as a guide, which degrades the informational content of signals like the two-year-minus-funds spread.

**The strategic logic is real, whatever one thinks of the tradeoff.** A chair who does not commit to a path cannot be accused of breaking a commitment, and cannot be attacked politically for a promise unkept. Less guidance preserves optionality in an environment where the chair sits between a hawkish committee and a dovish administration. **It buys institutional flexibility at the cost of market stability**, and that trade is being made deliberately.

**What to watch:** statement length and language changes, dot plot participation, meeting-calendar announcements, and the realized volatility of rates around scheduled events — S12 (MOVE) is the direct measure of whether this is working as described.

## Part III — The Transmission Map

### The Canonical Chain

Textbook transmission runs in one direction:

> **Policy rate → money market rates → the Treasury curve → credit spreads → the cost of capital → real economic decisions → growth and inflation → back to the policy rate.**

Each arrow is a separate mechanism with its own speed, its own reliability, and its own failure mode. Understanding where a given link is strong or broken is more useful than knowing the chain exists.

**Link 1: Policy rate to money market rates.** Nearly instantaneous and highly reliable. The corridor described in Family A does its job; SOFR, effective fed funds, and bill yields all track the target range closely. **Failure mode: reserve scarcity.** When cash gets tight, repo rates break above the corridor ceiling regardless of where the Fed set the target — September 2019. This link is the one that fails without warning.

**Link 2: Money market rates to the Treasury curve.** Strong at the front end, progressively weaker with maturity. The two-year is essentially a forecast of the average policy rate over two years, so it tracks policy expectations tightly. The thirty-year contains policy expectations plus a term premium that reflects supply, inflation uncertainty, and the compensation demanded for duration risk. **Failure mode: term premium moving independently of policy.** This is precisely what is happening now — the two-year near the 57th percentile of its range while the ten-year sits near the 98th. Policy expectations and long yields have decoupled.

**Link 3: The Treasury curve to credit spreads.** Moderate and regime-dependent. Corporate borrowing costs equal the Treasury yield plus a spread, so higher Treasury yields raise the cost of capital mechanically even if spreads are unchanged. **Failure mode: spread compression offsetting yield increases.** Currently active — high yield at the fifth percentile of its five-year range means credit is absorbing the long-end move rather than transmitting it. Borrowers face a higher all-in cost than a year ago, but the spread component says the market perceives no additional risk.

**Link 4: Cost of capital to real decisions.** Slow, with lags of two to four quarters, and highly variable by sector. Housing responds first and most; capital-intensive industry next; asset-light services barely at all. **Failure mode: balance-sheet insulation.** Corporations that termed out debt at 2020–2021 rates do not care about today's rates until their maturity wall arrives. Households with three-percent mortgages do not care either. **The current cycle has more of this insulation than any prior one, which is the single best explanation for why tightening produced less economic damage than every model predicted.**

**Link 5: Real activity to inflation.** Weak, lagged, and the least reliable link in the chain. The Phillips curve has failed in both directions within fifteen years.

**Link 6: Inflation back to the policy rate.** Strong, mechanical, and the reason inflation data is the highest-signal macro release. This is the link that closes the loop.

### The Parallel Channels

The canonical chain is not the only route. Four others operate simultaneously and sometimes dominate.

**The wealth channel.** Higher asset prices make households wealthier, which raises consumption independent of any change in income or borrowing cost. This channel has strengthened as household equity ownership has grown, and it means **equity prices are now a genuine input to the real economy rather than merely a reflection of it.** A Fed that tightens and triggers an equity decline gets more restriction than the rate move alone implies. A Fed that tightens into an equity melt-up gets less.

**The exchange rate channel.** Higher domestic rates attract capital, strengthen the currency, cheapen imports, and tighten conditions for anyone borrowing in that currency abroad. For the United States this channel is amplified by the dollar's funding role — dollar strength tightens global conditions well beyond the domestic economy.

**The bank lending channel.** Policy affects bank profitability, capital, and willingness to lend. A flat or inverted curve compresses net interest margins and reduces lending appetite. This channel runs through the Senior Loan Officer survey and shows up in real activity two to three quarters later.

**The expectations channel.** Possibly the most powerful and least mechanical. If market participants believe the Fed will act, they reprice before it acts, and the repricing accomplishes the policy. **This is why communications are a genuine lever rather than commentary** — and why reducing forward guidance, as Warsh has done, structurally weakens this channel and forces more work onto the others.

### The Feedback Loops

Three loops make the system reflexive rather than linear.

**The financial conditions loop.** Policy tightens, conditions tighten, growth slows, the Fed eases. But conditions can also loosen *while* policy is restrictive — equity rallies, spreads compress, the dollar weakens — which offsets the policy stance and forces the Fed to be more restrictive than it planned. **The current market is a live example: policy is nominally restrictive, credit spreads sit at cycle tights, and equity valuations are extreme.** The Fed has less traction than the funds rate implies.

**The fiscal-monetary loop.** Higher rates raise interest expense, which widens the deficit, which requires more issuance, which raises term premium, which raises rates further. **This loop is stabilizing at low debt levels and destabilizing at high ones**, because the interest-expense feedback becomes large enough to dominate. It is the mechanism by which fiscal dominance arrives — not through a decision, but through arithmetic.

**The credibility loop.** If the market believes the central bank will control inflation, expectations stay anchored and the required tightening is modest. If belief erodes, expectations drift, and the required tightening grows. **Credibility is therefore self-fulfilling in both directions**, which is why perceived political interference matters even when it changes no actual decision.

### Where the Chain Is Broken Right Now

Four breaks are worth naming explicitly, because scenarios in Part IV turn on whether they persist.

**The front end and the long end have decoupled.** Policy expectations drive the two-year; supply and term premium drive the thirty-year. A hike would raise the front end and might well *flatten* rather than steepen the curve, which is the opposite of the intuitive response.

**Credit is not transmitting the long-end move.** High yield at 251 basis points against a thirty-year at 5.2 percent is an inconsistency. Either credit is mispricing refinancing risk for issuers approaching maturity walls, or the long end is overstating the cost of capital that borrowers actually face. **These cannot both be right, and the resolution is one of the more consequential near-term questions.**

**Rate sensitivity in the real economy is muted by locked-in liabilities.** Both household mortgages and corporate debt were substantially termed out at historically low rates. The transmission returns as maturities arrive, which makes the corporate maturity wall a dated, forecastable tightening event that does not require any Fed action.

**The expectations channel has been deliberately narrowed.** Less forward guidance means less policy accomplished through anticipation and more through realized moves, which raises rate volatility structurally and increases the chance of a disorderly repricing at any given data release.

## Part III-B — Five Case Studies: The Machine in Action

Theory describes what can happen. Case studies show what did, in what order, with which signals firing when. Each of the five below is chosen because its mechanism is live in 2026.

---

### Case 1 — September 2019: Reserve Scarcity Discovered by Breaching It

**What happened.** On September 16 and 17, 2019, overnight repo rates spiked from around two percent to as high as ten percent intraday. The effective fed funds rate broke above the top of the target range. The Fed had to conduct emergency overnight repo operations for the first time since the financial crisis, and subsequently resumed balance sheet growth.

**The levers involved.** Balance sheet runoff (B2) had been proceeding for two years, mechanically draining reserves. Simultaneously, a corporate tax date drained cash into the Treasury General Account (D3), and a large Treasury settlement required dealers to fund a substantial inventory. Regulatory constraints (E1, E3) limited how much dealers could expand balance sheets to intermediate. **Three separate levers — one Fed, one Treasury, one regulatory — combined on a single day.**

**The signals, in order.** Repo rates had been drifting higher for weeks in a pattern visible only to funding desks. The SOFR distribution's upper percentiles widened before the median moved — stress concentrated in specific borrowers first. Then the median broke. Then fed funds broke the range. Equity markets noticed on day two.

**What it teaches for 2026.** The ample-reserves threshold cannot be identified in advance, including by the Fed. **The warning arrives in the money-market spreads and nowhere else.** Note that reserve-management purchases began again in December 2025 specifically because this lesson was learned — the Fed is now defending the level proactively rather than discovering it. That reduces but does not eliminate the risk, and the Standing Repo Facility now exists as an additional backstop that did not in 2019.

**Signal mapping:** S1 (SOFR-IORB), S3 (reserves), S4 (TGA), S2 (SRF, which now exists as the release valve).

---

### Case 2 — March 2020: When the Safe Asset Stopped Being Liquid

**What happened.** In the second week of March 2020, the Treasury market — the deepest and most liquid market in the world — became difficult to transact in size. Bid-ask spreads widened dramatically. Off-the-run issues became nearly untradeable. **Treasury yields rose during an acute risk-off episode**, which is the opposite of the expected safe-haven behavior and the clearest possible evidence of a mechanical rather than fundamental problem.

**The mechanism.** Leveraged relative-value positions — principally the cash-futures basis trade, in which hedge funds hold Treasuries against short futures with substantial leverage — were forced to unwind simultaneously. Dealers, constrained by the supplementary leverage ratio (E1), could not expand balance sheets to absorb the selling. Foreign central banks facing dollar shortages sold Treasuries to raise cash. Every source of demand vanished at once while supply surged.

**The response.** The Fed used essentially every lever in Family B: unlimited purchases (B1), the FIMA repo facility so foreign officials could raise dollars without selling (B7), expanded FX swap lines (B8), and 13(3) facilities extending into corporate credit (B9). Regulators granted temporary SLR relief (E1) to free dealer capacity.

**What it teaches for 2026.** **Market structure can fail independently of fundamentals**, and when it does, the Fed's response is mechanical rather than macroeconomic — it is fixing plumbing, not adjusting policy. The basis trade exists today at scale comparable to or greater than 2020. Dealer capacity remains constrained by the same regulation, which is precisely why proposals to exclude Treasuries from the leverage ratio are a live and consequential lever under a chair who favors regulatory easing.

**Signal mapping:** S6 (cross-currency basis, which widened dramatically), S12 (MOVE, which spiked), S15–S18 (auction and dealer metrics), S1.

---

### Case 3 — September 2022: The UK Gilt Crisis and the Long-End Accident Template

**What happened.** An unfunded fiscal package announced on September 23, 2022 triggered a disorderly selloff in UK government bonds. Thirty-year gilt yields rose more than one hundred basis points in days. The Bank of England was forced to announce emergency purchases within a week, and the government fell shortly after.

**The mechanism, and why it matters.** UK pension funds ran liability-driven investment strategies using leveraged interest-rate derivatives. Rising yields generated collateral calls. Meeting the calls required selling gilts, which pushed yields higher, which generated more calls. **A structure designed to hedge interest-rate risk became a mechanical amplifier of it.** The stabilizing buyer inverted into the marginal seller.

**What it teaches for 2026.** This is the template for scenario 1E. Three features transfer. **First, the trigger was fiscal, not monetary** — no central bank action was required to start it. **Second, the amplification came from leverage embedded in a structure widely regarded as conservative.** **Third, the timeline was days, not months** — a sovereign bond market that had functioned for centuries became disorderly inside a week.

The differences that matter: the United States issues the reserve currency and has a deeper domestic buyer base, and there is no direct US analogue to the concentrated LDI structure. The similarity that matters: **the US has leveraged relative-value positioning in Treasuries at very large scale, and the compounding mechanism is structurally the same.**

**Signal mapping:** S13 (30Y level and velocity), S15 (auction tails), S12 (MOVE), S11 (term premium), S29 (currency falling alongside rising yields — the diagnostic combination).

---

### Case 4 — March 2023: A Risk Category Nobody Was Testing

**What happened.** Silicon Valley Bank failed in roughly forty-eight hours. Signature Bank and First Republic followed. The Fed created the Bank Term Funding Program, lending against securities valued at par rather than market price.

**The mechanism.** SVB held a large portfolio of long-duration securities purchased at low yields, carried as held-to-maturity and therefore not marked to market in reported capital. When rates rose, the economic value collapsed while the accounting did not reflect it. The bank's depositors were concentrated, sophisticated, and overwhelmingly uninsured. When they moved, the bank had to sell securities, realize the losses, and became insolvent in the process.

**The genuinely new element was speed.** The run was coordinated through social media and executed through mobile banking. Every prior model of deposit stickiness was calibrated on a world where withdrawal required a phone call or a branch visit.

**What it teaches for 2026.** **The risk that materializes is usually the one not being measured**, because measured risks get managed. Capital ratios and stress tests looked adequate because they were testing credit risk, not the interaction of duration risk with deposit concentration. The BTFP response also established a precedent worth noting: **the Fed neutralized a mark-to-market problem by declaring that marks would not apply.** That is an extraordinary power and its availability is now partly priced.

**Signal mapping:** Bank equity and credit performance (which moved days ahead of the failures), S10 (rate repricing velocity — the two-year moved more than fifty basis points in days, one of the largest such moves on record), deposit flow data.

---

### Case 5 — 2023: The Bill-Share Shift, or How Treasury Eased Without the Fed

**What happened.** Through 2023, with the Fed running quantitative tightening and the market braced for heavy coupon supply to pressure long yields, the Treasury shifted its financing mix substantially toward Treasury bills rather than coupons.

**Why it mattered.** Bills were absorbed by money market funds using cash that had been parked at the Fed's reverse repo facility. That cash was, in effect, idle — it was sitting on the Fed's balance sheet earning the RRP rate. Moving it into bills financed the government **without competing for duration and without draining bank reserves.** The reverse repo balance fell by well over a trillion dollars over the period, and the long end was spared pressure that a coupon-heavy strategy would have generated.

**The significance.** **This was a material easing of financial conditions, accomplished entirely by a Treasury debt-management decision, with no Fed involvement and almost no market commentary.** It is the clearest demonstration available that Family D contains levers as powerful as anything in Family B.

**What it teaches for 2026.** The reverse repo buffer that made this possible has been substantially consumed. The same maneuver is less available now, which means incremental deficit financing must increasingly be done in coupons — competing for duration and pressuring the long end. **This is a large part of the mechanical explanation for the bear steepener described throughout this paper.** The quarterly refunding announcement is where the forward path of this variable is disclosed, which is why signal S19 deserves far more attention than it typically receives.

**Signal mapping:** S5 (RRP balance), S19 (refunding bill share), S13 (30Y), S32 (net liquidity).

---

### What the Five Cases Share

**Four of the five originated outside the Federal Reserve.** Tax dates and settlement timing, leveraged fund positioning, a fiscal announcement, deposit concentration, and a debt-management decision. Only the response was monetary.

**In every case the money-market and dealer-capacity signals moved before the headline asset prices.** The sequence in the Part V cascade is not theoretical; it is the observed pattern.

**In every case the constraint that bound was a quantity, not a price.** Reserves, dealer balance sheet, collateral, deposits, duration absorption capacity. **The policy rate was irrelevant to all five episodes.** A framework that watches only the funds rate would have had no warning of any of them.

## Part IV — Forward Scenarios

### How These Are Built and How to Read Them

Four horizons: twelve months, thirty-six months, five years, ten years. Each contains named scenarios with assigned probabilities, the specific conditions that would trigger each, the market implications, and the political dynamics that shape it.

**The probabilities are judgments, not calculations.** They are stated numerically because vague language conceals disagreement and prevents scoring, not because the precision is real. Treat a thirty percent estimate as meaning "more likely than not to be wrong, but the most likely single outcome," and revisit them when the triggers fire.

**Political dynamics are treated as a primary variable, not a residual.** In 2026 the Fed operates under an administration with an explicit rate preference, a chair appointed by that administration, a committee that has dissented three times in a row against holding, and a public debate about the institution's independence. Modeling policy as a technocratic response function would be a modeling error.

Three structural facts constrain every scenario below.

**First, the fiscal position is the dominant medium-term force.** Deficits at current levels during an expansion mean duration supply keeps growing regardless of who chairs the Fed. Interest expense compounds. This shapes the long end independent of policy.

**Second, the transmission is muted by locked-in liabilities and will return on a schedule.** Corporate maturity walls and mortgage turnover are dated events. Tightening that has not bitten yet will bite later without any new decision.

**Third, the credibility question is live in a way it has not been since 1951.** Every scenario has a version where the market's assessment of Fed independence changes the term premium more than the policy decision does.

---

### Horizon 1 — Twelve Months (September 2026 to September 2027)

#### Scenario 1A — Hold and Drift: the bear steepener persists
**Probability: 30 percent**

The committee holds or delivers one token hike to satisfy the dissenters and then stops. Core inflation grinds sideways in the mid-two to low-three range on core PCE. Labor softens without breaking. The long end continues repricing fiscal supply and term premium.

*Path:* Thirty-year drifts toward 5.50–5.75 percent. Two-year stays near 4.00–4.25. The 2s10s spread widens toward plus seventy-five to one hundred basis points. Credit finally begins to widen in the second half of the window as the maturity wall arrives, with high yield moving from the fifth percentile toward the twentieth.

*Market implications:* Multiple compression concentrated in long-duration equities, with value and short-duration cash flows relatively protected. Homebuilders and rate-sensitive sectors under pressure as mortgage rates follow the long end. Gold firm on real-rate ambiguity and continued official demand. Dollar mixed — supported by rate differentials, undermined by fiscal narrative.

*Political dynamics:* Warsh preserves optionality by not committing. The administration tolerates a pinned short rate while complaining about it. The long end does the tightening the Fed declines to do, which is a politically convenient arrangement for everyone except borrowers.

*Confirming tells:* Thirty-year auction tails widening. Rising term premium estimates. Falling bill share in the quarterly refunding. Dealer takedown rising at long-tenor auctions.

*Falsifiers:* Core PCE breaking below 2.8 percent and continuing lower; a payroll print below fifty thousand; high yield widening more than fifty basis points in a month.

#### Scenario 1B — The Hike
**Probability: 25 percent**

Core inflation refuses to improve. Energy re-accelerates on Middle East escalation. The three dissenters are joined by others, and Warsh delivers one or two hikes to establish anti-inflation credibility early in his tenure — which is also the cheapest moment in a chair's term to spend political capital.

*Path:* Funds rate to 3.75–4.00 or 4.00–4.25 percent. Two-year to 4.50–4.75. **The curve flattens from the front rather than steepening**, which is the counterintuitive result and the one most likely to catch positioning offside. Long end rises less than the front, because a credible Fed reduces the inflation risk premium embedded in term premium.

*Market implications:* Broad multiple compression, worst in long-duration growth. Dollar strength, with emerging market stress following. Credit widens meaningfully — this is the scenario where the 251-basis-point high yield spread finally reprices. Gold initially sells off on higher real rates, then finds a bid if the tightening threatens growth.

*Political dynamics:* Direct conflict with the White House. Public criticism of Warsh personally. **The important second-order effect is that a chair who hikes against political pressure buys credibility, which lowers term premium** — an underappreciated offset to the direct tightening.

*Confirming tells:* Core CPI at or above 0.3 percent month-over-month. Oil above ninety-five dollars. Five-year, five-year forward breakeven above 2.55 percent. August and September payrolls firm. Hawkish dissents increasing to four or more.

*Falsifiers:* Two consecutive soft core prints; unemployment rising through 4.5 percent.

#### Scenario 1C — The AI-Productivity Pivot
**Probability: 20 percent**

Core inflation resolves lower faster than the committee expects. Warsh invokes the productivity thesis he has articulated for years — that AI-driven output gains are disinflationary — and begins cutting in late 2026 or the first half of 2027, justified by data rather than by politics.

*Path:* Funds rate to 3.00–3.25 percent by mid-2027. Two-year rallies hard toward 3.50. Curve bull-steepens.

*Market implications:* Equity rally led by long-duration growth. Dollar weakens. Gold rallies strongly on falling real rates.

*The critical risk inside this scenario, and the reason it is not simply bullish:* if the market reads the pivot as political accommodation rather than as a genuine data-driven judgment, **the front end rallies while the long end sells off.** Term premium rises to compensate for the perception that the inflation-fighting reaction function has weakened. That is a bear steepener wearing the costume of a dovish pivot, and it is materially worse for balanced portfolios than a straightforward hold. **The tell that distinguishes them is the thirty-year and the five-year, five-year forward breakeven: if both rise while the Fed cuts, the market is repricing credibility, not celebrating disinflation.**

*Confirming tells:* Core PCE below 2.8 percent and decelerating. Unit labor costs decelerating. Measured productivity growth accelerating. Warsh explicitly foregrounding the productivity frame in speeches.

*Falsifiers:* Long-end yields rising during the first cut; breakevens widening; dollar falling more than three percent in a month alongside rising gold — that combination is a credibility signal, not an easing signal.

#### Scenario 1D — Labor Break and Forced Easing
**Probability: 15 percent**

The July payroll weakness was the leading edge rather than noise. Claims rise, unemployment moves to 4.6–4.8 percent, the Sahm gap widens, and the Fed is forced to cut quickly regardless of where core inflation sits.

*Path:* Seventy-five to one hundred twenty-five basis points of cuts inside the window. Two-year collapses toward 3.25–3.50. Sharp bull steepening.

*Market implications:* Credit is where the damage concentrates. **High yield at the fifth percentile of its range has no cushion**, and a genuine growth scare repricing it toward the seventieth percentile implies spreads doubling. Equities fall first on earnings revisions, then stabilize as the cut cycle is priced. Long-duration Treasuries are the best-performing asset. Gold strong.

*Political dynamics:* The administration gets the cuts it wanted, for the reason it least wanted. Warsh's credibility is not damaged by easing into a genuine downturn, which makes this the politically cleanest path to lower rates.

*Confirming tells:* Four-week initial claims above two hundred fifty thousand. Unemployment at or above 4.6 percent. ISM services employment below forty-five. CCC-minus-BB spread widening — the earliest credit signal. Quits rate falling.

*Falsifiers:* Payrolls rebounding above one hundred fifty thousand for two consecutive months.

#### Scenario 1E — Long-End Accident
**Probability: 10 percent**

A poorly-received auction, a leveraged unwind in the basis or a liability-driven portfolio, or a fiscal announcement shock produces disorderly conditions at the long end.

*Path:* Thirty-year gaps toward 5.75–6.25 percent in days rather than weeks. MOVE spikes. Dealer capacity is overwhelmed. Cross-asset deleveraging follows.

*Market implications:* Everything sells simultaneously — the correlation structure that makes balanced portfolios work fails. The Fed is forced into some form of intervention: market-function purchases, supplementary leverage ratio relief, or explicit support that it takes care not to call yield curve control.

*Political dynamics:* This is the scenario that resolves the independence question fastest and least favorably. A Fed that intervenes to support Treasury prices under fiscal stress is operating in the 1942–1951 mode, whatever it calls the program.

*Confirming tells:* Thirty-year auction tail wider than three basis points. Primary dealer takedown share spiking. MOVE above one hundred thirty. Cross-currency basis widening. Repo specials pressure in on-the-run issues.

*Note:* This scenario can follow from 1A or from the credibility-repricing version of 1C. It is a conditional tail rather than an independent path, which is why its probability is meaningful despite the apparent extremity.

### Horizon 2 — Thirty-Six Months (through mid-2029)

This window contains the 2028 presidential election, the expiry of several Federal Reserve Board terms, and the arrival of the corporate maturity wall in size. Political variables carry more weight here than in any other horizon.

#### Scenario 2A — Fiscal-Dominance Drift
**Probability: 35 percent**

No single decision produces this outcome; arithmetic does. Deficits remain in the six to seven percent range. Interest expense compounds toward and past defense spending as a budget line. Each attempt to hold rates high enough to reach two percent inflation worsens the debt trajectory visibly enough to generate political resistance. The Fed becomes structurally more accommodative than its own inflation data warrants — not through capture but through the accumulated weight of the constraint.

*Characteristics:* Inflation floors near three percent rather than two. Nominal growth is permitted to run above nominal rates. Real yields are suppressed. The curve stays steep. This is **financial repression**, and it is the historically typical resolution for a heavily indebted sovereign that borrows in its own currency.

*Market implications:* Nominal assets underperform. Real assets, commodities, and gold outperform. Long-duration nominal bonds are the worst place to be — negative real returns held to maturity, which is the entire point of the mechanism. Equities do adequately in nominal terms and poorly in real terms.

*Political dynamics:* This scenario is politically stable, which is why it is the modal outcome. Nobody has to announce it. No legislation is required. It is simply what happens when the constraint binds and no one chooses austerity.

#### Scenario 2B — Successful Normalization
**Probability: 25 percent**

AI-driven productivity gains materialize in the measured data. Unit labor costs decelerate. Inflation genuinely returns toward two percent without a recession. The Fed cuts to a neutral rate near three percent, and the curve normalizes to a healthy positive slope.

*Market implications:* The best broad outcome. Equities perform, though extreme starting valuations cap the returns available. Credit stays tight legitimately. The dollar is stable. Gold underperforms.

*Why it deserves twenty-five percent rather than less:* the productivity thesis is not merely a political convenience. Capital expenditure on AI infrastructure has been enormous, and if any meaningful share converts to measured output gains in services — the sector where productivity has been most stubborn — the disinflationary effect lands exactly where inflation has been hardest to move.

*Why it deserves no more:* productivity gains from general-purpose technologies have historically taken longer to appear in the statistics than their advocates expect, and the energy demand of the infrastructure is itself inflationary in the interim.

#### Scenario 2C — Recession and Reset
**Probability: 20 percent**

The labor softening compounds through 2027. A conventional recession arrives. The Fed cuts to 1.50–2.50 percent. The deficit blows out toward nine or ten percent of GDP through automatic stabilizers and discretionary response. Credit reprices severely, and the private credit book — untested at current scale through a default cycle — becomes the locus of the stress.

*Market implications:* Long-duration Treasuries perform, at least initially. Credit and equities fall hard. Gold performs. The recovery arrives with a materially higher debt load and less policy room than the prior cycle, which increases the probability of scenario 2A afterward.

*Political dynamics:* A recession in 2027 or 2028 is an electoral event. Fiscal response is likely to be large and fast regardless of which party holds power, which accelerates the debt trajectory and makes the subsequent regime more repressive.

#### Scenario 2D — The Second Wave
**Probability: 12 percent**

Easing arrives too early — whether through the productivity thesis proving premature, political pressure succeeding, or a growth scare that resolves quickly. Inflation re-accelerates on the combination of renewed demand, energy, tariffs, and deglobalization cost pressure. **This is the 1970s stop-go pattern**, and its defining characteristic is that the second wave is more expensive to break than the first because expectations have learned that the central bank blinks.

*Market implications:* The worst configuration for balanced portfolios, because stocks and bonds fall together and there is no diversifying asset except commodities and gold. Real rates must rise substantially to restore credibility. Equity multiples compress severely.

*Confirming tells:* Five-year, five-year forward breakeven drifting above 2.75 percent. Wage growth re-accelerating. Survey measures of long-run inflation expectations moving.

#### Scenario 2E — Institutional Rupture
**Probability: 8 percent**

Fed independence is materially compromised — through the accumulation of appointments, legislative change, an explicit public capitulation, or a formal agreement to support Treasury financing. The 1951 Accord, in reverse.

*Market implications:* Term premium repricing on a scale that dwarfs any policy decision. Dollar decline. Gold surge. Foreign official demand for Treasuries falls further. **The front end could be pinned very low while the long end sells off violently**, which is the specific signature to watch for.

*Why eight percent rather than lower:* the historical precedent exists, the current political environment features explicit pressure, and the arithmetic in scenario 2A creates a standing motive. *Why not higher:* the institutional and market constraints are strong, the bond market's ability to punish is well understood by all participants, and the 2022 UK episode provided a recent and vivid demonstration.

### Horizon 3 — Five Years (through 2031)

At this horizon the specific policy path matters less than which of a few structural questions resolve favorably.

**The four swing factors:**

1. **Does measured productivity accelerate?** This is the single largest variable. It determines whether the debt math is survivable through growth or requires repression.
2. **Does the debt trajectory force an adjustment, and of what kind?** Interest expense compounds mechanically. The question is whether adjustment comes through growth, inflation, austerity, or crisis.
3. **Does the monetary architecture change materially?** Stablecoin scale, official gold accumulation, settlement fragmentation, and the possibility of a genuine reserve alternative.
4. **How does the energy transition net out?** Capital-intensive and inflationary during the build, potentially disinflationary afterward. Five years is squarely within the build.

| Five-year scenario | Prob | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Managed repression** | 35% | Inflation floors near 3%. Real rates suppressed. Steep curve. Real assets outperform, nominal bonds deliver negative real returns. No crisis, no resolution — just a grind. |
| **Productivity resolution** | 25% | AI gains appear in the data. Growth outruns the debt math. Rates normalize near 3%. The benign outcome, and the one the current equity market is priced for. |
| **Disorderly adjustment** | 20% | A funding event, auction failure, or credibility break forces rapid repricing. Intervention follows. Ends in repression, but arrives violently rather than gradually. |
| **Deflationary bust** | 10% | Debt burden plus a demand shock produces a genuine deflationary episode. Rates to zero, QE resumes at scale. Long duration is the best asset; credit is the worst. |
| **Architecture shift** | 10% | Stablecoins, official gold, and bilateral settlement change the plumbing enough that the dollar's funding role measurably erodes. Gradual, not dramatic — but it changes the term premium floor permanently. |

**The cross-cutting observation:** three of these five scenarios involve real yields being held below where an unconstrained market would set them. That is not a coincidence — it is what the arithmetic in Part II Family F implies. **A portfolio built for the next five years should assume some probability-weighted degree of financial repression as the base case rather than as a tail.**

---

### Horizon 4 — Ten Years (through 2036)

At a decade, specific rates are unforecastable and regime character is the only meaningful output.

| Ten-year scenario | Prob | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial repression grind** | 40% | The historical base case for a heavily indebted sovereign borrowing in its own currency. Nominal growth persistently above nominal rates. Inflation tolerated in the 2.5–4% band. Debt ratio stabilizes or falls slowly through erosion rather than repayment. Savers in nominal instruments bear the cost. Politically durable because it is diffuse and unannounced. |
| **Technological offset** | 20% | Productivity gains large enough that real growth carries the debt. This is the outcome where the AI capital cycle earns its return. Rates normalize, inflation is genuinely contained, and the fiscal constraint loosens without policy action. |
| **Crisis and restructuring-equivalent** | 15% | Not formal default — a currency-issuing sovereign does not default nominally. Instead a period of very high inflation, capital controls, or forced domestic purchase of government debt. Effectively a restructuring executed through the price level. |
| **Architecture reset** | 15% | The dollar's role changes materially — not replacement, but sufficient erosion that the structural bid for Treasuries weakens and term premium resets permanently higher. Driven by some combination of official diversification, digital settlement alternatives, and geopolitical fragmentation. |
| **Return to stability** | 10% | Fiscal consolidation, credible policy, inflation at target, normal curve. Requires political choices that have not been made in decades. |

**The dominant lesson from ten-year history:** heavily indebted sovereigns that control their own currency have almost never resolved the problem through austerity or default. They have resolved it through sustained periods in which the price level rises faster than nominal rates. **That is the base case, it is unpleasant for holders of nominal claims, and it argues for a permanent structural allocation to real assets rather than a tactical one.**

### Political Dynamics: The Cross-Horizon View

Four political variables run through every scenario above and deserve separate treatment.

**The chair's position.** Warsh sits between a committee that has dissented hawkishly three consecutive times and an administration that wants lower rates. The productivity thesis is his mechanism for eventually delivering what the administration wants while claiming data justification. **Whether the market accepts that justification is the pivotal question**, because acceptance means a normal easing cycle and rejection means a term-premium repricing.

**Board composition.** Governor terms expire on a schedule. Over a full presidential term, an administration can substantially reshape the committee. This is the slow, legal, and most likely route to a materially different reaction function — not a dramatic rupture but a gradual drift in the median voter.

**The 2028 election.** A recession arriving in 2027 or 2028 would produce a large fiscal response regardless of outcome. An election-year Fed faces heightened scrutiny of every decision. Historically the Fed has tried to avoid moving close to elections, which compresses the available policy windows.

**The debt ceiling.** Recurring, politically driven, and productive of the largest short-horizon liquidity swings in the system. Each episode drains and then refills the Treasury General Account in size, which is a monetary event caused by a legislative process.

## Part V — The Tactical Signal Map

### Purpose of This Section

Parts I through IV describe a machine and where it might go. This part connects that to what you actually look at every day, every week, and every month across the five reports.

Each signal below is treated the same way: **what it measures**, **where it lives in the reporting stack**, **threshold levels**, **which Part IV scenario it discriminates between**, **how much lead time it provides**, and **how it fails**.

The signals are grouped by speed, because speed determines which report should carry them. Plumbing signals belong in the Daily Cascade because they can change in a session. Supply signals belong in the Monthly Macro because they arrive on a scheduled calendar. Structural signals belong in Disruptive Themes because they move over quarters.

**One organizing principle before the catalog.** The scenarios in Part IV differ primarily along two axes: whether the front end moves and whether the long end moves. A signal is valuable to the extent it discriminates between those. Signals that tell you "conditions are tightening" without telling you *where on the curve* are much less useful in the current regime than they were in any post-2008 cycle, because the whole distinguishing feature of the present configuration is that the two ends have decoupled.

---

### Tier 1 — Plumbing Signals (lead time: days; home: Daily Cascade)

These are the fastest signals in the entire stack and the ones that fail without warning. They belong in the Liquidity & Funding Stress overlay of the Top & Bottom report and in the daily flow.

**S1. SOFR minus IORB spread.** The single best real-time read on whether cash is abundant or scarce. SOFR is the cost of borrowing overnight against Treasury collateral; IORB is the risk-free rate banks earn at the Fed. When SOFR trades persistently above IORB, collateral is plentiful relative to cash, which means reserves are getting tight.

| Reading | State | Action |
|---|---|---|
| SOFR below IORB | Cash abundant | Normal |
| 0 to +3bp | Neutral | Monitor |
| +3 to +8bp sustained | Reserves tightening | Watch — reduce gross |
| Above +10bp sustained | Scarcity | Trigger — expect intervention |

*Discriminates:* Scenario 1E (long-end accident) and any funding-driven tail. **This is the September 2019 early-warning instrument.**
*Lead time:* Days. Sometimes hours.
*Failure mode:* Month-end and quarter-end distortions from G-SIB balance sheet management produce spikes that mean nothing. Look at the pattern between turn dates, and watch the 99th percentile of the SOFR distribution rather than the median — stress concentrates in specific borrowers first.

**S2. Standing Repo Facility usage.** Any material usage means private repo is not clearing at acceptable rates. Zero is normal; nonzero is a signal; sustained usage is a trigger.
*Discriminates:* 1E.
*Failure mode:* Some usage is technical testing rather than stress. Look for persistence.

**S3. Bank reserves (WRESBAL) level and trajectory.** The banking system's operating liquidity. The ample threshold is unknown in advance and discovered by breaching it. What matters is the *trajectory* and the interaction with S1 — falling reserves with a stable SOFR-IORB spread is benign; falling reserves with a widening spread is not.
*Note for 2026:* The December 2025 shift to reserve-management purchases means the Fed is now actively defending this level. That reduces the probability of a September-2019 repeat but does not eliminate it.

**S4. Treasury General Account balance and four-week change.** Direct, mechanical liquidity effect. A rising TGA drains reserves; a falling TGA adds them. Tax dates in mid-April, mid-June, mid-September and mid-January produce predictable drains.
*Discriminates:* Short-horizon risk-asset conditions across all scenarios.
*Lead time:* Same week; the calendar is known in advance, which makes this one of the few genuinely forecastable liquidity variables.

**S5. Reverse repo facility balance.** The shock absorber. When it is large, bill issuance drains it harmlessly. When it is exhausted, bill issuance comes out of bank reserves instead, and the same fiscal action becomes a tightening.
*The critical question for the current regime:* how much buffer remains. Track it against Treasury's bill issuance plan from the refunding announcement.

**S6. Cross-currency basis.** The premium to swap foreign currency into dollars. Widening negative basis means global dollar funding stress. Moves before almost anything else in an international stress episode.
*Discriminates:* 1E and any global transmission event.
*Home:* Monthly Macro Pillar 7; should also appear in the Daily Cascade during stress.

---

### Tier 2 — Rate and Curve Signals (lead time: days to weeks; home: Daily Cascade and Top & Bottom overlays)

**S7. The 2s10s spread.** The classic curve measure. In the current regime its interpretation has changed and this matters: **steepening driven by the long end rising is a bear steepener and is risk-negative; steepening driven by the front end falling is a bull steepener and is a growth signal.** The spread alone does not tell you which. Always decompose.

| Configuration | Signature | Scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Bear steepener | 30Y up more than 2Y | 1A, or 1C's credibility-failure variant |
| Bull steepener | 2Y down more than 30Y | 1C benign, 1D |
| Bear flattener | 2Y up more than 30Y | 1B |
| Bull flattener | 30Y down more than 2Y | Deflationary / flight to quality |

*Failure mode:* The 2022 inversion signaled a recession that did not arrive on schedule. The curve is a strategic clock, not a trade trigger — it leads the economy reliably at twelve to eighteen months and leads equities poorly.

**S8. The 5s30s spread.** More sensitive to term premium and fiscal supply than 2s10s, because it strips out the near-term policy path almost entirely. **In the current regime this is arguably the more informative curve measure**, since the entire story is happening at the back end.

**S9. Two-year minus fed funds — cuts or hikes priced.** The cleanest free proxy for market policy expectations, available on FRED without a CME subscription. A two-year below the funds rate prices cuts; above prices hikes. Converts directly to a number of expected moves at twenty-five basis points each.
*Current reading:* Two-year near 4.21 against a 3.50–3.75 range prices meaningful hike risk, consistent with the FedWatch figures.

**S10. Rate repricing velocity.** Your existing overlay: the twenty-day change in the two-year-minus-funds spread, with thresholds at thirty-five, fifty, and seventy-five basis points for watch, trigger, and crisis.

**The directional asymmetry is the important part and worth restating.** *Hawkish* repricing — the market rapidly pricing more hikes — transmits through the valuation channel and compresses multiples. *Dovish* repricing — the market rapidly pricing cuts — is usually a flight-to-safety signal and means something has broken. **Both directions are risk-negative, for entirely different reasons, and confusing them leads to buying a dovish repricing as good news when it is the market pricing a recession.**

*Discriminates:* Fast repricing hawkish points to 1B; fast repricing dovish points to 1D or 1E.

**S11. Term premium estimates.** Model-derived decomposition of the long yield into expected-policy and risk-compensation components. Rising term premium with a stable expected-policy path is the signature of fiscal supply pressure and of credibility concerns.
*Failure mode:* Model-dependent. Different estimates disagree materially. Use the direction, not the level.

**S12. MOVE index.** Implied volatility on Treasury options. **The divergence between MOVE and VIX is more informative than either alone.** Elevated MOVE with compressed VIX means the bond market is pricing risk the equity market is not, and that divergence has historically resolved toward the bond market's view.
*Threshold:* Above one hundred thirty is a stress reading in the current era.

**S13. The thirty-year yield and its percentile.** The single cleanest expression of the fiscal-supply and term-premium story. Currently near the ninety-eighth percentile of its five-year range while the two-year sits near the fifty-seventh — **that asymmetry is the defining feature of the present regime and the reason a curve-level signal alone is inadequate.**

**S14. FedWatch implied probabilities.** Market pricing for each meeting. The value is not the level but the *change*: a twenty-point shift in a single session tells you the market's reaction function to a data print.

---

### Tier 3 — Supply and Auction Signals (lead time: scheduled; home: Monthly Macro Pillar 8)

**This tier is systematically underwatched and is where the current regime's central risk lives.**

**S15. Thirty-year auction tail.** The difference between the yield at auction and the prevailing market yield immediately before. A positive tail means the auction cleared cheap — demand was weaker than the screen implied.

| Tail | Reading |
|---|---|
| Negative (stops through) | Strong demand |
| 0 to +1bp | Normal |
| +1 to +3bp | Soft |
| Above +3bp | Genuine demand problem — trigger |

*Discriminates:* 1E directly. A sequence of tailing long-end auctions is the most concrete available evidence of financing stress.
*Note:* August 2026's thirty-year cleared at 5.22 percent, the highest awarded yield at that tenor since 2001. The level is not itself a stress signal; the tail and the bidding composition are.

**S16. Bid-to-cover ratio.** Total bids relative to size. Falling bid-to-cover across successive auctions at the same tenor indicates deteriorating demand.

**S17. Indirect bidder share.** Largely foreign official and institutional demand. Declining indirect participation with rising dealer takedown means the primary dealers are absorbing supply they do not want, which they subsequently distribute into the market — a mechanical source of pressure in the days after an auction.

**S18. Primary dealer takedown.** The residual. A rising share is the clearest sign that end demand is not clearing supply.

**S19. Quarterly Refunding Announcement — the bill share.** **This is the single most important scheduled event in this pillar and the most consistently ignored.** The proportion of financing done in bills versus coupons determines whether Treasury's borrowing drains the reverse repo facility harmlessly or competes for duration and pushes long yields higher.
*Discriminates:* Directly between 1A persisting and 1A resolving. A shift back toward bill-heavy issuance would relieve long-end pressure without any Fed action.

**S20. Treasury buyback size and composition.** Expanded in 2026. At sufficient scale, buybacks remove duration from the market — the same economic effect as central bank purchases, executed by the fiscal authority. Watch whether purchases concentrate in off-the-run liquidity support or extend to genuine duration removal, and watch the market's interpretation, since the commentary linking buybacks to fiscal stress is itself a term-premium input.

---

### Tier 4 — Inflation and Expectation Signals (lead time: weeks to months; home: Monthly Macro Pillar 4)

**S21. Ten-year breakeven.** Market-implied average inflation over ten years. Contains a risk premium and a liquidity premium, so treat the level loosely and the direction seriously.

**S22. Five-year, five-year forward breakeven.** **The most important single number for the credibility question.** It measures expected inflation over the five years beginning five years out, which strips away current conditions entirely. If this stays anchored, the Fed can tolerate overshoots. If it drifts, the reaction function must become much more aggressive.

| Reading | State |
|---|---|
| Below 2.20% | Well anchored, possibly too low |
| 2.20–2.45% | Normal |
| 2.45–2.55% | Watch |
| Above 2.55% | Anchoring at risk — trigger |
| Above 2.75% | Un-anchoring — scenario 2D |

*The critical cross-signal:* **five-year, five-year forward rising while the Fed cuts is the tell that distinguishes a benign productivity pivot from a credibility repricing.** That combination, alongside a falling dollar and rising gold, is the signature of scenario 1C's failure mode and of 2E.

**S23. Sticky CPI and core services excluding shelter.** The persistence measures. Flexible components falling while sticky components hold means the supply shock is resolving while the underlying problem is not.
*Discriminates:* 1B versus 1C. Warsh's productivity thesis requires the sticky measures to come down; if they do not, the hawks win the argument.

**S24. Oil.** The energy pass-through channel that drove the 2026 headline spike. Middle East escalation is the live risk, and the Red Sea remains an active theater.
*Threshold:* Sustained moves above ninety-five dollars re-open the headline inflation problem and shift probability from 1C toward 1B.

### Tier 5 — Credit Signals (lead time: weeks to months; home: Monthly Macro Pillar 6, Top & Bottom HY overlay)

**S25. High yield OAS and its percentile.** Currently near two hundred fifty-one basis points, approximately the fifth percentile of its five-year range. **This is the most conspicuous mispricing in the current market relative to what the long end is saying about the cost of capital.**
*Discriminates:* Every scenario. Credit is the asset class with the least cushion, which means it is where the repricing concentrates in 1B, 1D, and 1E.

**S26. CCC minus BB spread.** Quality dispersion within high yield. **Widening dispersion while aggregate spreads stay tight is the earliest reliable credit-cycle warning**, because the weakest issuers lose refinancing access first.
*Lead time:* Weeks to a few months ahead of aggregate spread widening.

**S27. HY spread acceleration overlay.** Your existing construction. Rate of change rather than level. Given how tight the level is, the acceleration measure is doing most of the work — from the fifth percentile, the first fifty basis points of widening is far more informative than the absolute level.

**S28. Primary market issuance conditions.** Whether deals can be placed at all. A shut primary market is a more acute signal than any secondary spread level, and it arrives first.
*Note:* August 2026 saw one of the heaviest investment grade calendars on record clearing without difficulty. That is a genuine risk-positive data point and cuts against the pessimistic reading of the long-end move.

### Tier 6 — Global and Currency Signals (home: Monthly Macro Pillar 7, Alternative Asset Report)

**S29. Dollar index.** Rate differentials support it; the fiscal narrative undermines it. **A falling dollar alongside rising long yields is the specific combination that indicates credibility repricing rather than ordinary rate dynamics** — normally higher yields attract capital and strengthen the currency, so the inverse relationship is diagnostic.

**S30. Japanese ten-year yield.** Japan's normalization removes the global funding anchor and pulls Japanese capital home from foreign bond markets. Rising JGB yields are a withdrawal of demand from US long duration.
*Discriminates:* Supports 1A and 1E through the demand channel.

**S31. Gold and central bank gold demand.** Gold is responding to something other than real yields, which is why traditional models have underexplained it. Official-sector accumulation following the 2022 demonstration of political risk in dollar reserves is a structural bid.
*Home:* Alternative Asset Report; also the core of Disruptive Themes Factor V.
*Discriminates:* Gold rising alongside rising real yields and a falling dollar is a debasement signal, and it is the cleanest single market-based read on scenarios 2A and 2E.

### Tier 7 — Aggregates and Structural Signals (home: Monthly Macro Pillar 3, Disruptive Themes)

**S32. Fed net liquidity.** Balance sheet minus TGA minus reverse repo. A heuristic, not an identity, with a documented history of decoupling from equity prices. Useful for direction and fragility; unreliable as a directional trade signal.
*Unit caution:* the balance sheet is reported in millions while the facility balances are in billions. Normalize before computing.

**S33. NFCI.** The broad financial conditions composite. **Its most valuable use is detecting divergence between policy stance and actual conditions.** Restrictive policy alongside loosening conditions means the Fed has less traction than the funds rate implies — which is the current situation and a reason to expect the committee to lean hawkish.

**S34. H.8 bank credit and C&I loan growth.** Weekly, and the only high-frequency read on private credit creation. Slowing C&I growth precedes economic weakness by roughly two to three quarters.

**S35. Senior Loan Officer survey.** Quarterly, qualitative, and one of the better leading indicators in the entire stack. Net percentage tightening leads loan growth, which leads activity.

**S36. Stablecoin float and reserve composition.** The newest signal and the least modeled. Growth in dollar-stablecoin float creates incremental Treasury bill demand; contraction forces liquidation into the front end. This connects crypto flows directly to money market conditions.
*Home:* Disruptive Themes Factor V; deserves a line in Monthly Macro Pillar 3.

### The Cascade: Which Signal Fires First

If the framework has one practical output, it is the order in which things break. The following sequence has held in most funding-driven episodes and is the ordering to internalize.

1. **Cross-currency basis and SOFR-IORB widen** — days before anything else. Nobody outside funding desks notices.
2. **MOVE rises while VIX stays compressed** — the bond market prices risk first.
3. **Long-end auction metrics deteriorate** — tails widen, dealer takedown rises.
4. **CCC-BB dispersion widens** while aggregate high yield stays tight.
5. **Aggregate high yield widens** — now everyone notices.
6. **Equity volatility rises and the index falls.**
7. **Economic data confirms**, one to two quarters later.

**Reading order matters more than reading everything.** A reader who checks steps one through four weekly and steps five through seven monthly is looking in roughly the right places at roughly the right frequency. A reader who watches only steps five and six is receiving the news after the informed participants have finished repositioning.

### Signal-to-Scenario Discrimination Matrix

The compressed version — which signals most cleanly separate the twelve-month scenarios.

| If you see | It points toward |
|---|---|
| 30Y rising, 2Y stable, term premium up, bill share falling | **1A** — bear steepener persists |
| Core CPI ≥0.3% MoM, oil >$95, 5y5y >2.55%, dissents growing | **1B** — the hike |
| Core PCE <2.8% falling, unit labor costs decelerating, 30Y *falling* with the front end | **1C benign** — productivity pivot |
| Fed cutting while 30Y rises, 5y5y rises, dollar falls, gold rips | **1C failure / 2E** — credibility repricing |
| Claims >250K, U3 ≥4.6%, quits falling, CCC-BB widening | **1D** — labor break |
| Auction tail >3bp, dealer takedown spiking, MOVE >130, basis widening | **1E** — long-end accident |

**The single most valuable discriminating observation in the current configuration:** watch what the thirty-year does when the Fed finally moves. If the Fed cuts and the long end rallies with it, the machine is working normally. **If the Fed cuts and the long end sells off, the market is repricing the institution rather than the policy**, and that is a different world requiring different positioning — short nominal duration, long real assets, long gold, and structurally lower equity multiples.

## Coda

Three closing observations.

**The regime has changed and most participants have not noticed.** For fifteen years, the operative question in rates was what the Fed would do to the front end, and the long end followed. The current configuration inverts that: the front end is pinned by a divided committee while the long end reprices on fiscal supply and term premium. **Frameworks built entirely around Fed-watching are looking at the smaller half of the problem.** The Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement now competes with the FOMC statement for market significance, and almost nobody treats it that way.

**The two most consequential numbers in this paper are the thirty-year yield and the high yield spread**, because their relationship contains a contradiction that must resolve. A thirty-year at 5.2 percent says the cost of capital has risen materially. High yield at 251 basis points, the fifth percentile of its five-year range, says the market perceives no additional risk in lending to leveraged borrowers. Both cannot be correct. Either credit is mispricing the refinancing arithmetic facing issuers approaching maturity walls, or the long end is overstating the marginal borrower's actual cost. **Which way that resolves is the most actionable single question in the current market, and the signals in Tier 5 are where it will show up first.**

**Everything in Part IV rests on assumptions about mechanism that could break.** The scenarios assume the Fed retains operational independence, that the Treasury market clears without official intervention, that the dollar retains its funding role, and that the political system tolerates the arithmetic without a rupture. Each of those has held for decades. None is guaranteed, and the historical section exists specifically to demonstrate that the current arrangement is a negotiated settlement from 1951 rather than a law of nature.

The framework's value is not that it predicts which scenario arrives. It is that it specifies, in advance, what each one would look like on the way in — so that when the signals start firing in the cascade order described above, the recognition is fast rather than retrospective.
