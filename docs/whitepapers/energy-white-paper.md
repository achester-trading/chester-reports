## Purpose and How to Use This Document

Energy is the input to everything else in the report system — the cost base of the metals in paper VII, the inflation impulse that moved the Fed and the ECB in paper VIII, the term structure signal in paper XIII, the geopolitical channel in Disruptive Themes. It is also the commodity complex with the worst forecasting record and the most confident forecasters, which is a combination worth understanding before trusting any number.

The paper carries one emphasis, agreed in advance: **power and the AI-load thesis.** Electricity is the energy commodity where the 2020s changed the structure rather than the cycle, and it is the one with the thinnest coverage in the existing library. Part IV is the longest.

**Two claims organise the paper.**

**Energy is a spare-capacity and inventory market, not a resource market.** Price is set by the size of the buffer — OPEC+ spare capacity, commercial and strategic inventories, refining slack, LNG liquefaction and shipping capacity, grid reserve margins — not by reserves in the ground. Every major energy price episode of the past fifty years is a buffer story: the buffer was drawn down and the price went vertical, or the buffer was rebuilt and the price collapsed. Reading energy means reading the buffer, and the 2026 tape is a clean demonstration — a Hormuz disruption that removed 7–8 million barrels a day from Gulf exports produced a \$40 monthly trading range in Brent because the buffer was thin, while a US gas market with storage at its highest level heading into winter since 2016 barely noticed a global LNG price that doubled.

**Electricity has become the scarce energy commodity.** For two decades, US electricity demand was flat and the analytical centre of energy was the barrel. Data-centre load growth has revived demand, and the binding constraints are now interconnection queues, transformer and turbine lead times, firm-generation adequacy, and transmission — not fuel. PJM's capacity price rose from \$29 to \$333 per megawatt-day in two years and the auction still cleared 6.5 gigawatts short of its reliability requirement. The analytical centre is shifting from barrels to megawatts, and the paper is built to reflect that.

**Three principles govern the writing.** The four energy commodities — oil, gas, power, uranium — are four different markets with different buffers, different geographies, and different price-formation mechanisms, and the paper treats them separately before connecting them. Forecast ranges are preceded by the track record, because the 2026 record is instructive: the largest bank in the space was forecasting a 2.3 million-barrel-a-day surplus and \$60 Brent in February; Brent touched \$105 in July. And the paper is explicit about the honest uncertainty in the AI-load projections that underpin its emphasis — PJM's own market monitor questions the credibility of the demand forecasts feeding PJM's auctions, and the range of published 2030 data-centre demand estimates spans more than two-to-one.

**Timing note.** Written on 31 August 2026, six months into the US–Iran conflict that began on 28 February. Brent trades near \$88 after a July in which it ranged nearly \$40 — spiking to \$105 on 23 July when the mid-June ceasefire collapsed and strikes on vessels resumed — and the curve has returned to clear backwardation. Gulf exports have recovered to roughly two-thirds of pre-war levels, 15–16 million barrels a day against a March trough of 5–6, with an Iran–Oman revenue-sharing framework for the strait agreed but no reopening. US commercial crude inventories are expected to remain below the five-year low through year-end. Henry Hub sits under \$3 with storage at 3,169 billion cubic feet, while TTF and JKM sit above \$21, having doubled since the conflict began — a seven-fold spread bottlenecked by liquefaction capacity. PJM has just filed with FERC to put new loads above 50 megawatts first in line for curtailment. Uranium's term price at \$90 a pound is the highest since 2008, and US–Iran enrichment talks have collapsed. The August EIA outlook expects most Gulf production back near pre-conflict levels in early 2027 with 0.6 million barrels a day of disruption persisting through the end of that year.

**Suggested use.** Part I states the argument. Parts II and III are oil and gas reference. Part IV is the emphasis and the section to read in full. Part VI is the operational core. Part VIII is the cadence.

# PART I — THE ANALYTICAL FRAME

## 1. Why Energy Resists Valuation

An energy commodity produces no cash flow and, unlike gold, is consumed. It can be stored, but expensively and for a limited time — oil in tanks and ships at a cost, gas in depleted reservoirs and salt caverns seasonally, electricity almost not at all. The supply curve does not slope; it steps. A new oil field, LNG train, or power plant takes five to ten years from decision to output and then produces at near-full capacity regardless of price for decades. The result is a market in which supply is fixed in the short run, demand is inelastic in the short run, and price does all of the adjusting — which is why energy prices are more volatile than the underlying physical balances would suggest, and why the *level* of price carries less information than the *buffer* between supply and demand.

Three frames are commonly used and each is useful within limits.

**Cost of production** — the marginal barrel's breakeven — sets a long-run floor in a market with a responsive supply source (US shale, at roughly \$45–60 WTI for the marginal well) and says nothing about the ceiling. Oil traded at three times the marginal cost in 2008 and 2022.

**Supply-demand balance** — the annual surplus or deficit — is genuinely informative because energy is consumed, but the balance forecasts are revised constantly and the 2026 experience is the caution: a forecast surplus of 2.3 million barrels a day became a realised deficit within four months because a supply disruption no one modelled arrived.

**The buffer** — the paper's frame. Spare capacity, inventories, and slack are the variables that determine how a given balance translates into price. A 1 million-barrel deficit against 6 million barrels of spare capacity and inventories above the five-year average is a non-event. The same deficit against 2 million barrels of spare capacity and inventories at the five-year low is a spike. The balance tells you the direction of pressure; the buffer tells you the amplification.

## 2. The Buffer Thesis

Every major energy price episode is a buffer story.

**1973 and 1979.** OPEC's embargo and the Iranian revolution each removed a few million barrels a day from a market with no strategic reserves and little spare capacity outside the countries doing the removing. Prices quadrupled and then doubled again.

**1986.** Saudi Arabia, having cut output to defend prices, abandoned the effort and restored production into a market with rebuilt inventories. Prices fell by two-thirds.

**2008.** Demand growth from China met flat non-OPEC supply and OPEC spare capacity near 1 million barrels a day. Brent reached \$147. Then demand collapsed and the buffer rebuilt in months.

**2014–2016.** Shale added 4 million barrels a day to a market where OPEC chose not to cut. Inventories built to records; prices fell from \$115 to \$28.

**2020.** Demand fell 20 million barrels a day in a month; storage filled; the WTI front-month contract settled negative because there was nowhere to put the oil.

**2022.** Russia's invasion removed a perceived 2–3 million barrels a day from a market with inventories drawn down through 2021's recovery. Brent reached \$128. Then the barrels found buyers, the strategic reserves were released, and the price halved.

**2026.** Hormuz disruption removed 7–8 million barrels a day of Gulf exports at the March trough. The buffer — OPEC+ spare capacity of perhaps 6 million barrels a day on paper, much of it *behind* the strait, and OECD inventories that had not rebuilt through 2025 — was thinner than the balance sheets suggested. Brent ranged \$40 in a month.

**The rule.** Price sensitivity to a supply or demand shock is inversely proportional to the buffer. Reading the buffer — spare capacity by location, inventories against seasonal norms, refining and export capacity utilisation, grid reserve margins — is the analytical task, and Part VI is built around it.

## 3. Four Commodities, Four Markets

**Crude oil** is global, seaborne, and fungible. A barrel in the Gulf and a barrel in Texas are close substitutes, connected by shipping, and price differences between them are shipping and quality differentials. OPEC+ manages roughly 40% of supply and holds essentially all of the spare capacity, which makes it a cartel-influenced market in a way no other commodity is. The buffer is OPEC+ spare capacity plus inventories, and both are observable.

**Natural gas** is regional. Pipelines connect producers to consumers within continents; LNG connects continents but requires liquefaction, shipping, and regasification capacity, each of which is a multi-year, multi-billion-dollar investment. The result is three loosely linked regional markets — Henry Hub in North America, TTF in Europe, JKM in Asia — whose prices can diverge by multiples when the connecting capacity is saturated, as now. The buffer is storage, and storage is seasonal.

**Electricity** is local and, for practical purposes, non-storable. It must be generated at the moment it is consumed, transmitted over a grid with physical limits, and balanced second by second. Price is set in regional wholesale markets by the marginal generator and, in capacity markets, by the cost of ensuring enough generation exists to meet peak demand three years out. The buffer is the reserve margin — the excess of available capacity over peak demand — and it is the buffer that has been eroding.

**Uranium** is a fuel-cycle commodity. The mined product must be converted, enriched, and fabricated before it becomes reactor fuel, and each stage is a separate market with separate bottlenecks. Demand is set by utility contracting years ahead; spot is a small and volatile residual. The buffer is inventory at utilities and the enrichment capacity available outside Russia, and the second of those is the binding constraint.

## 4. From Barrels to Megawatts

For twenty years — from roughly 2005 to 2022 — US electricity consumption was flat. Efficiency gains, deindustrialisation, and LED lighting offset population and economic growth, and the electricity sector's analytical questions were about fuel mix (coal to gas to renewables) rather than about volume. Energy analysis was oil analysis, with gas as the domestic supplement.

**The load-growth return.** Data-centre expansion — driven by AI training and inference workloads with power densities an order of magnitude above conventional computing — has revived demand. PJM, the grid operator for 67 million people across thirteen states, projects peak demand growing by 32 gigawatts between 2024 and 2030, with all but 2 gigawatts of that from data centres, and roughly 70 gigawatts of new large-load demand by 2038 against roughly 15 gigawatts of generation retired since 2022. Published estimates of US data-centre electricity demand in 2030 span 425 terawatt-hours (IEA) to 970 (BCG) — a range that is itself the honest measure of how uncertain the thesis is — with a central cluster near 550–650, or 10–12% of total US consumption.

**Why this moves the analytical centre.** Oil is a market with 6 million barrels a day of spare capacity somewhere, a strategic reserve, and a demand side that responds to price within months. Power in the regions where data centres cluster is a market with a projected reserve shortfall, an interconnection queue measured in years, equipment lead times measured in years, and a demand side that does not respond to price at all — a hyperscaler committing \$10 billion to a data centre does not defer it because capacity prices rose. The scarcity, the inelasticity, and the multi-year lead times that define a genuinely tight commodity market now describe electricity more than they describe oil. That is the paper's second claim, and Part IV develops it.

# PART II — CRUDE OIL

## 5. Supply Structure

**OPEC+.** The group manages roughly 40 million barrels a day of production and, more importantly, holds essentially all of the world's spare capacity — production that can be brought online within months without new investment. Pre-conflict estimates put spare capacity near 6 million barrels a day, concentrated in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Kuwait. The 2026 conflict revealed the buffer's weakness: most of that spare capacity sits *behind* the Strait of Hormuz, so a disruption to the strait removes both the flowing barrels and the ability to replace them. Spare capacity that cannot reach the market is not a buffer.

The group's cohesion is the other variable. OPEC+ discipline held through 2023–2025 as the group defended prices against shale growth and soft demand; Goldman's February 2026 expectation was that the group would begin *raising* output in the second quarter into a surplus. The conflict pre-empted the question, and the post-conflict decision — whether to restore production quickly and rebuild inventories, or slowly and defend the price — will determine the 2027 balance.

**US shale.** Roughly 13 million barrels a day of crude, the largest single source of non-OPEC supply, and the source that responds to price — but more slowly than in 2014–2019. Capital discipline imposed by investors after a decade of value destruction means producers hold output roughly flat and return cash rather than chase price. Decline rates on shale wells are steep — 60–70% in the first year — so flat output requires continuous drilling, and the inventory of drilled-but-uncompleted wells (DUCs) that provided a fast-response buffer in earlier cycles has been worked down. Shale can add perhaps 500,000 barrels a day in a year at \$85 WTI; it cannot replace a Gulf disruption.

**The non-OPEC growth cohort.** Guyana (from zero to over 600,000 barrels a day since 2019, heading to 1 million-plus), Brazil's pre-salt, Canadian oil sands, and Norwegian offshore. Together they add roughly 1–1.5 million barrels a day annually and are the reason the pre-conflict consensus expected a surplus.

**Sanctioned barrels.** Russia (roughly 9–10 million barrels a day, flowing to India and China at discounts under a price cap), Iran (whose exports were the conflict's original target), and Venezuela. Sanctions enforcement intensity is a supply variable: tighter enforcement removes barrels; looser enforcement adds them. The 2026 conflict has made Iranian exports a war variable rather than a sanctions variable.

## 6. Demand

**The peak-demand debate, stated honestly.** The IEA has projected global oil demand peaking around 2029–2030 near 105–106 million barrels a day, driven by electric-vehicle adoption in China, efficiency, and the substitution of gas and renewables in power generation. OPEC projects no peak before 2050. The gap reflects genuinely different assumptions about EV adoption pace, petrochemical growth, and developing-economy demand, and the paper's view is that the IEA's date has moved later in each successive report, which is evidence about the forecast's reliability rather than about the eventual answer.

**China** has been the driver of global demand growth for two decades and has plateaued. Chinese gasoline demand is declining as EVs — over half of new car sales — displace it. Chinese diesel demand has fallen with the property downturn. The remaining growth is petrochemical feedstock, which is real and resilient but is not transportation fuel.

**India** has replaced China as the marginal growth source, adding roughly 200,000–300,000 barrels a day annually, and its consumption is transportation-heavy and price-sensitive.

**EV displacement** is real, measurable, and slower than the bull case for EVs assumed. Roughly 1.5–2 million barrels a day of demand has been displaced cumulatively; the annual increment is a few hundred thousand barrels. It is a structural headwind that matters over a decade and does not matter in a year with a Hormuz disruption.

**The resilient tail.** Petrochemicals, aviation, and marine — roughly 30% of demand — have no viable substitute at scale and grow with global GDP. Peak demand, when it arrives, is a plateau with a long tail rather than a decline.

## 7. The Curve

The oil futures curve is the most informative single object in the oil market, and it is read more reliably than the flat price.

**Backwardation** — spot above futures — means the market is paying a premium for oil now over oil later. It reflects near-term scarcity: buyers cannot wait, so they pay up for prompt barrels. It rewards long positions with a positive roll yield (the position rolls into cheaper deferred contracts) and it is the structural signature of a tight physical market. The curve returned to clear backwardation in July 2026 as Hormuz constraints tightened prompt supply, and the IEA's August report identified the return of prompt backwardation in both WTI and Brent as the market's own tightness signal.

**Contango** — futures above spot — means storage is being paid for: the market is oversupplied now and expects to be less so later. It punishes long positions with negative roll yield, which is why futures-based oil products (USO, UNG) bleed value in contango and why a multi-year oil view should generally not be held in front-month futures. The 2020 super-contango, when the front month went negative, was the extreme.

**Why the front month lies.** The prompt contract is where the disruption premium, the positioning, and the delivery mechanics concentrate. A \$105 print on 23 July said less about the 2027 balance than the twelve-month contract did. Reading the curve's *shape* — the spread between the first and sixth month, the first and twelfth — separates the physical tightness signal from the headline noise.

**WTI–Brent.** The spread between the US and global benchmarks reflects US export capacity and Atlantic Basin logistics. Brent's \$5–7 premium in August 2026 is elevated because the disruption is to seaborne Gulf crude, which prices off Brent, while US production is unaffected.

## 8. The Geopolitical Premium

Oil carries a geopolitical premium in most years — a few dollars of expected disruption priced into the curve — and the premium's behaviour has a consistent pattern that 2026 both confirmed and complicated.

**The pattern.** Supply disruptions have repeatedly been smaller and shorter than the market feared at the outset. The 2019 Abqaiq attack removed 5 million barrels a day and was repaired in weeks. The 2022 Russian invasion was priced as a 3 million-barrel loss and delivered less than 1 million as barrels rerouted. Markets learned to fade geopolitical spikes, and the fading became a reliable trade.

**2026 complicated it.** The Hormuz disruption was larger — 7–8 million barrels a day at the trough — and has lasted six months with no resolution. The pattern's core observation held in part: Gulf exports have recovered to two-thirds of pre-war levels through rerouting and the partial reopening the Iran–Oman framework permits, and Brent has fallen from \$105 to \$88 while the strait remains constrained. But the assumption that disruptions resolve in weeks has been falsified, and the strategic-reserve buffer — drawn down heavily in 2022 and only partly refilled — was smaller than in prior episodes.

**Reading the premium.** The gap between spot and the twelve-month contract is a rough measure of the disruption premium's size. Its decay rate — how fast the curve flattens after a headline — measures the market's confidence in resolution. The EIA's August assumption of continued severe constraints through August and most production back by early 2027 is the base case the curve is pricing; the curve's backwardation says the market is less confident than the EIA.

**The strategic reserve as a policy tool.** The US SPR was drawn from 638 million barrels to 347 million in 2022 and partly refilled to roughly 400 million. It is the one buffer that policy controls directly, and its release is the one supply response that arrives in days. Its current level, against a Hormuz disruption that has lasted six months, is a live constraint on the policy response to any further escalation.

## 9. Crude Oil: Bull, Bear, and Range

### The bull case
- The buffer is thin: spare capacity behind Hormuz is not spare capacity; OECD inventories did not rebuild through 2025; the SPR is at 60% of its 2021 level
- Shale cannot fill a Gulf gap; DUCs are worked down and capital discipline holds
- The disruption has lasted six months and the market's fade-the-spike reflex has been wrong
- Underinvestment in long-cycle supply through 2015–2021 leaves the non-OPEC pipeline thin beyond Guyana and Brazil
- Peak demand keeps moving later; the resilient tail is 30% of the market

### The bear case
- Gulf exports have recovered to two-thirds of pre-war levels and are rising; the EIA expects most production back by early 2027
- The pre-conflict balance was a 2.3 million-barrel-a-day surplus and the underlying supply growth from Guyana, Brazil, and Canada has not gone away
- OPEC+ will want to restore output and market share once the strait reopens, which is the 1986 setup
- Chinese demand has plateaued and EV displacement compounds
- \$88 Brent is already destroying marginal demand and pulling forward every barrel that can move

### The range-bound case
- \$75–95 Brent as the disruption premium decays but the buffer stays thin; OPEC+ manages the restoration to hold a floor; shale and non-OPEC growth cap the top

### What would settle it
The pace of Gulf export recovery against the OPEC+ restoration decision. If exports return to pre-war levels by early 2027 *and* the group restores output into it, the surplus reasserts and \$70 is in play. If the strait remains constrained into 2027 or the group manages the restoration to defend price, the buffer stays thin and \$85–95 holds.

# PART III — NATURAL GAS AND LNG

Natural gas is the commodity where the buffer thesis is most visible, because the buffer — storage — is published weekly and the market trades it directly. It is also the commodity where regional segmentation produces the strangest configurations, and August 2026 is one: the US benchmark under \$3 while the European and Asian benchmarks sit above \$21, a seven-fold spread between markets connected by an export system running at capacity.

## 10. US Supply: The Associated-Gas Problem

US dry gas production runs near 105–107 billion cubic feet a day, the largest in the world, and its structure explains its price behaviour.

**Roughly a third of US gas is associated gas** — produced as a byproduct of oil drilling in the Permian and other liquids plays. Like byproduct silver in the metals paper, associated gas responds to the *oil* price, not the gas price: when WTI is above \$80, Permian drilling produces gas regardless of whether Henry Hub is at \$2 or \$5. This is a structural depressant on US gas prices in high-oil-price regimes — exactly the current configuration — and it is why the Hormuz conflict, bullish for every other energy price, has been indirectly *bearish* for Henry Hub: high oil prices sustain the drilling that supplies the gas.

**Appalachia** (Marcellus and Utica) is the largest dry-gas source, pipeline-constrained, and the swing supplier to the eastern US. Takeaway capacity, not geology, caps its growth; new pipelines are a permitting question that has been repeatedly answered "no."

**The demand side is growing into it.** LNG exports (16.5 billion cubic feet a day in the third quarter, rising as new trains commission), pipeline exports to Mexico (9.6 billion cubic feet a day and rising as the Costa Azul terminal, which shipped its first cargo on 8 July, pulls Permian gas to the Pacific), and — the structural addition — gas-fired power generation serving data-centre load, up 2% in the first half of 2026 with the EIA expecting further growth in 2027 as low prices displace coal.

## 11. Storage: The Published Buffer

The EIA's weekly storage report — released Thursdays at 10:30 — is the single most-traded data point in US energy. Working gas in storage stood at 3,169 billion cubic feet in mid-August 2026, the highest level heading into winter since 2016, with the South Central region 5% above its five-year average after terminal maintenance backed gas up into storage.

**How to read it.** The level against the five-year average and range is the buffer's size. The weekly injection or withdrawal against the seasonal norm is the balance's direction. And the *regional* composition matters: South Central storage serves the LNG export complex, so a build there during export maintenance is mechanical rather than fundamental — exactly the current case, and a reason the headline surplus overstates looseness once Freeport's 2 billion cubic feet a day returns.

**The asymmetry.** Storage is a seasonal buffer, filled April–October and drawn November–March. A high starting level insulates winter prices; a low one amplifies them. But the buffer is finite and the winter draw is weather-driven, which is why gas is the most volatile major commodity: the January 2026 cold snap took the weekly average Henry Hub price from \$3 to \$13.80 in three weeks, and back below \$3.30 within a month. That round trip — a 4x spike fully retraced — is normal gas behaviour, and it is why gas price *levels* are nearly meaningless without the storage and weather context.

## 12. The LNG Buildout and the Linkage Question

US LNG export capacity is in the middle of a near-doubling — from roughly 12 billion cubic feet a day in 2023 toward 20-plus by the late 2020s as Plaquemines, Corpus Christi Stage 3, Golden Pass, and successor trains commission. Every incremental train connects the US market more tightly to global prices.

**Feedgas is the demand signal.** Gas flowing to export terminals is published daily and is the cleanest read on export demand. The August 2026 dip — Freeport maintenance removing 2 billion cubic feet a day — is temporary; the trend is structurally up.

**The linkage question is the structural trade of the decade in gas.** Today, Henry Hub is bottlenecked away from world prices: US liquefaction runs at capacity, so the marginal molecule of US gas cannot reach the \$21 world price, and Henry Hub prices off the domestic balance. As export capacity grows relative to production growth, the bottleneck loosens and Henry Hub converges toward export netback pricing — world price minus liquefaction and shipping, roughly TTF minus \$4–6. Full convergence would imply a structurally higher and more volatile US gas price; the counterargument is that US supply growth (associated gas plus Appalachia if takeaway is ever built) outpaces export growth and preserves the discount. The 2030 outcome sits between, and Part VII-B scores it.

## 13. Europe: Structural Demand Destruction and a Thin Buffer

Europe replaced Russian pipeline gas with LNG after 2022 at the cost of demand destruction — industrial gas use fell roughly 20% and has not recovered — and its market now runs on a thinner buffer with a higher clearing price.

The 2026 configuration: EU storage 60.8% full in mid-August, *below the five-year minimum* for the date, after a cold 2025–26 winter and a summer in which Middle East conflict diverted cargoes and raised prices. TTF at \$21 has nearly doubled since the conflict began (from \$10.78), pricing both the supply risk — Qatari LNG transits Hormuz — and the storage deficit ahead of winter. Europe is the market where the buffer thesis currently reads tightest, and TTF, not Henry Hub, is the marginal global gas price.

## 14. Asia and the Global Balance

JKM at \$21.61 has slightly more than doubled since the conflict began, moving with TTF as the two regions compete for flexible Atlantic cargoes. Japanese and Korean demand is stable with nuclear restarts trimming LNG dependence at the margin; Chinese LNG imports are price-elastic — China buys spot cargoes below roughly \$12 and pivots to domestic coal and pipeline gas above it, making Chinese demand the global gas market's shock absorber; South and Southeast Asian demand (India, Thailand, Vietnam) grows structurally but is the first to be priced out in tight markets.

**The supply wave question.** Qatar's North Field expansion and the US buildout together add roughly 8 billion cubic feet a day of export capacity by 2028–2030 — the largest LNG supply wave in history, sanctioned before the conflict. The pre-war consensus was a 2027–2028 glut with TTF falling toward \$8–10. The conflict has deferred but not repealed that supply; if Hormuz normalises, the glut thesis returns with the new capacity arriving into it. Gas has the clearest boom-bust setup in the energy complex: acute tightness now, a supply wave visible on the horizon.

## 15. Natural Gas: Bull, Bear, and Range

### The bull case (US)
- Power-sector demand from data centres is the first structural domestic demand growth in two decades, and it is price-insensitive
- LNG and Mexican export growth is contracted and mechanical; every new train tightens the domestic balance
- Associated-gas growth stalls if oil falls or Permian activity plateaus; Appalachian takeaway remains blocked
- The linkage: each year moves Henry Hub closer to world netback pricing

### The bear case (US)
- Storage at a decade high into winter; the buffer is full
- Associated gas keeps coming as long as oil is high — and oil is high
- Producers restore curtailed volumes on any rally; the supply response above \$4 is fast
- The global supply wave lands 2027–2030 and drags world prices toward US netbacks rather than the reverse

### The range-bound case
- Henry Hub \$2.50–4.50 with weather spikes; TTF/JKM \$15–25 until Hormuz resolves, then \$8–12 as the supply wave arrives

### What would settle it
For the US: whether power-sector gas burn growth (data centres) outruns associated-gas supply growth — visible in the storage trajectory against degree-days. For the world: Hormuz, and then the pace at which the Qatari and US trains commission into whatever demand exists in 2028.

# PART IV — POWER

The emphasis of this paper. Electricity is where the 2020s changed the structure of energy rather than its cycle, and where the buffer — the reserve margin — is eroding in public, in auction results, at three-year forward horizons, with the grid operator itself filing emergency measures. This Part covers the demand shock, the supply-side constraints, the market structure that prices them, nuclear and its fuel cycle, and the honest uncertainties in the thesis.

## 16. The Load-Growth Return

**The numbers, and their provenance.** PJM — the largest US grid operator, serving 67 million people across thirteen states — forecasts peak demand rising 32 gigawatts from 2024 to 2030, with all but 2 gigawatts attributable to data centres. Its 2026/27 forecast peak rose more than 5,400 megawatts in a single year. Looking further out, PJM projects roughly 70 gigawatts of new large-load demand by 2038, against roughly 15 gigawatts of generation retired since 2022, and warned in February 2026 of a potential 60-gigawatt supply deficit over the next decade if current trends in demand growth, retirements, and interconnection persist. Nationally, published estimates of data-centre electricity consumption in 2030 range from the IEA's 425 terawatt-hours to BCG's 970, with a central cluster of 550–650 — 10–12% of projected US consumption, against roughly 4% today.

**Why the load is different in kind.** Three properties distinguish data-centre demand from every prior load source. It is *concentrated* — individual campuses of 500 megawatts to multiple gigawatts, the demand of a mid-sized city at a single interconnection point. It is *price-insensitive* — the electricity bill is a small share of an AI data centre's economics, dominated by the GPUs, so capacity prices rising tenfold changes siting decisions at the margin and cancels almost nothing. And it is *firm* — a training cluster wants 24/7 power at 99.99%-plus availability, which means it demands dispatchable capacity, not average energy. A gigawatt of solar nameplate is not a gigawatt of what a hyperscaler is buying.

**The honest uncertainty, stated where the emphasis lives.** PJM's own independent market monitor has questioned the credibility of the long-range data-centre projections feeding the auctions, and the monitor's scepticism deserves standing in this paper. The demand forecasts contain double-counting risk — developers file interconnection requests in multiple jurisdictions for the same project and build one; utilities forecast from requests. They contain extrapolation risk — the 2030 estimates assume AI capital expenditure sustains its current trajectory, and the range from 425 to 970 terawatt-hours is the honest confession that nobody knows. And they contain an efficiency counterargument — chip efficiency per unit of compute improves continuously, and inference workloads are more schedulable than the firm-load framing assumes. The paper's position: the *direction* is not in doubt (capacity auctions are clearing short today on load that already exists), the *magnitude beyond 2028* is genuinely uncertain, and Part VII-B treats the AI-capex bust as a live scenario rather than a footnote.

## 17. The Supply-Side Constraints

The demand shock would be manageable if supply could respond. Four constraints, stacked in series, prevent it — and because they are in series, relieving any one does not relieve the system.

**The interconnection queue.** US queues held roughly 2,600 gigawatts of proposed generation and storage as of early 2026 — more than double the entire installed US fleet — with waits of five years or more. PJM's generation queue alone holds about 140 gigawatts, and in the Dominion zone of Northern Virginia, the data-centre epicentre, waits stretch to seven years. ERCOT manages a 410-gigawatt large-*load* queue, 87% of it data centres. The queue is not a paperwork problem; each project requires network-impact studies against a grid whose every addition changes the physics for every other applicant. Reforms are underway — PJM's cluster-study model, a FERC-approved fast track (February 2026) letting replacement generation inherit retiring units' connection rights, and PJM's proposed Expedited Interconnection Track (March 2026) allowing up to ten projects a year to bypass the queue to serve large loads directly — and the immediate resort to ad-hoc bypasses is itself evidence the base process cannot clear at the required rate. Roughly 63 gigawatts of PJM projects hold signed or offered agreements; the binding question is how fast they energise.

**Equipment.** Large power transformers carry lead times of two to four-plus years; heavy-duty gas turbines are backlogged for years at GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi, with the manufacturers — burned by two decades of flat demand — expanding capacity cautiously. Electrical equipment is under 10% of a data centre's cost and has become, in the industry's phrase, 100% of the bottleneck. This is the sulfuric-acid lesson from the metals paper operating in power: a cheap consumable input binding harder than the headline resource.

**Firm-capacity scarcity and the retirement schedule.** The fleet that provides dispatchable capacity — coal and older gas — has been retiring on economics and policy, roughly 15 gigawatts in PJM since 2022, while the replacement pipeline is dominated by solar, wind, and batteries, which contribute far less firm capacity per nameplate megawatt. Seventeen units totalling about 1,100 megawatts have *withdrawn* planned retirements since 2024 — the price signal working at the margin — and PJM's Reliability Resource Initiative attracted over 11,000 megawatts of proposed new and upgraded capacity. The cleared capacity mix in the 2026/27 auction remains 45% gas, 22% coal, 21% nuclear: the "energy transition" grid still buys its reliability almost entirely from the fleet that predates it.

**Transmission.** The constraint nobody prices until it binds. Moving power from where it can be generated to where the data centres are requires lines that take a decade to permit and build. Transmission is why a national reserve margin is a fiction — capacity is only capacity if it can reach the load.

## 18. Market Structure: The Capacity Auction as the Price of the Buffer

Wholesale power markets price two things separately: *energy* (megawatt-hours, priced locationally in real time) and *capacity* (the commitment to be available at peak, priced years ahead). The capacity price is the market's direct quote on the buffer, which makes PJM's Base Residual Auction the single most informative price series in North American energy right now.

**The series.** The 2024/25 auction cleared at roughly \$29 per megawatt-day. The 2025/26 auction jumped to \$270 area. The 2026/27 auction (run July 2025) cleared near \$329, procuring 134,311 megawatts against a shortfall of 209 megawatts, with total capacity costs of \$16.1 billion. The 2027/28 auction (run December 2025) cleared *at the administrative price cap* of \$333.44 in key zones, procured 145,777 megawatts, and fell **6,517 megawatts short of the reliability requirement** — the auction, offering the maximum permitted price, could not buy enough capacity. Total cost: \$16.4 billion. The market monitor attributes \$23.1 billion of capacity-market revenue across the last three auctions to existing and forecast data-centre load, and \$13.8 billion of the resulting customer-bill impact — even after a Pennsylvania price-cap agreement limited the increase.

**What a shortfall at the cap means.** In a commodity market, price rises until supply appears or demand yields. The capacity auction hit its ceiling and supply did not appear, because the constraints of Section 17 are not price-responsive on a three-year horizon — the turbine backlog does not shorten because the auction cleared higher. The buffer is being rationed by administrative rule rather than by price, which is the signature of genuine physical scarcity.

**The political economy has arrived.** Retail electricity prices rose 6.3% year over year by January 2026 to 17.78 cents per kilowatt-hour, with Goldman projecting roughly 6% annual increases through 2027. Pennsylvania's governor filed a FERC complaint over auction outcomes; the price cap was the settlement. And on 31 July 2026, PJM filed with FERC to place all new loads of 50 megawatts or more — data centres, explicitly — *first in line for curtailment* during capacity shortages, ahead of households, effective June 2027, unless they secure their own generation under a "Bring Your Own New Capacity" mechanism. The grid operator has formalised what the auction implied: at the margin, there is not enough firm power for everyone, and the newest, largest loads must either bring supply or absorb the shortage.

**The investment translation.** A capacity price at the cap with a reliability shortfall is the strongest demand signal a generator can receive. It flows to owners of existing firm capacity (nuclear and gas fleets repricing their capacity revenue), to independent power producers, to gas-turbine manufacturers with multi-year backlogs, and to the behind-the-meter and co-location deals through which hyperscalers are contracting supply directly. Part V-B takes up the instruments.

## 19. Nuclear and the Fuel Cycle

Nuclear is the asset class the power squeeze re-rated, because it is the only large-scale source that is simultaneously firm, carbon-free, and — for existing plants — already built.

**Restarts and uprates: the cheapest megawatts in America.** The scarcity value of firm capacity resurrected retired plants: Palisades in Michigan returned to service under Holtec; Three Mile Island Unit 1 — renamed the Crane Clean Energy Center — is being restarted by Constellation under a 20-year Microsoft power-purchase agreement, targeted for 2027–28; Duane Arnold in Iowa is on a restart path backed by Google-linked demand. Alongside restarts, uprates and license extensions across the existing fleet add capacity at a fraction of new-build cost. Every one of these transactions shares a structure: a hyperscaler signing a long-dated, premium-priced PPA for firm carbon-free power — the clearest revealed-preference evidence for the firm-capacity thesis.

**SMRs, honestly.** Small modular reactors are the sector's long-dated option, not its near-term supply: the first Western grid-connected units (GE Hitachi's BWRX-300 at Darlington in Ontario and TVA's Clinch River project) target roughly 2029–2031, no US SMR delivers commercial power as of this writing, and the sector's one completed cost test — NuScale's cancelled Utah project — failed on economics. The honest position: SMRs matter for the 2030s balance, not the 2020s shortfall, and the 2020s shortfall is what the auctions are pricing.

**Uranium and the enrichment bottleneck.** The fuel cycle's binding constraint is not mined uranium but enrichment. Russia supplies roughly 27% of US enriched uranium and controls about 40% of global enrichment capacity; the US produces under 1% of global enrichment. The 2024 US ban on Russian imports, with utility waivers running to January 2028, set a countdown that the collapse of US–Iran enrichment talks and Russian counter-restriction threats have tightened. Prices carry the story: uranium's long-term contract price reached \$90 a pound by the end of the first quarter of 2026 — the highest since 2008 — with contracts touching \$93 after the talks collapsed; spot peaked at \$101.41 early in the year before easing. US mined production, meanwhile, fell 44% in the third quarter of 2025 from a base of six operating facilities. Western enrichment expansion (Urenco, Orano, and DOE-funded HALEU capacity for the advanced reactors) is underway and takes years; the 2026–2029 window is where the constraint binds.

## 20. Gas, Renewables, and the Firm-Capacity Arithmetic

**Gas is the bridge and the backbone.** The cleared capacity mix says it plainly: 45% of PJM's procured reliability is gas. Every credible path through the load-growth decade runs on more gas-fired capacity — which is why the turbine backlog is the single most binding constraint in the system, and why the gas-demand growth of Part III and the power squeeze of this Part are one story.

**Renewables plus storage: real energy, partial capacity.** Solar generation grew 21% in the first half of 2026, wind 6%, and the additions dominate the queue. But capacity accreditation — the fraction of nameplate the market counts as reliable at peak — runs roughly 10–20% for solar, 15–35% for wind, and rising-but-duration-limited figures for four-hour batteries. Renewables are cheap energy and expensive-to-firm capacity; the system needs both, and the auction shortfall is a shortfall of the second.

**Behind-the-meter and co-location.** The hyperscalers' workaround: siting data centres at existing plants (the co-location fights at nuclear stations), contracting dedicated new gas turbines, and deploying on-site generation and storage. PJM's Bring-Your-Own-New-Capacity mechanism formalises it. The consequence is a two-tier market forming — loads with dedicated supply, and loads exposed to the shared buffer — which is the power-market version of the two-tier copper market the tariffs created.

## 21. Transmission

The constraint that outlasts the others. Generation can be built in five years and turbines in three; a new interstate transmission line routinely takes ten to fifteen from proposal to energisation, crossing multiple state siting authorities with veto points at each. FERC's Order 1920 planning reforms push the process; the physics and the politics resist it. The practical consequences: locational price divergence widens (power is abundant where it cannot be delivered and scarce where it is needed); the reserve margin is regional, not national; and co-location — moving the load to the generation rather than the power to the load — is partly a transmission arbitrage. For monitoring purposes, congestion revenues and locational price spreads are the transmission constraint's price signal.

## 22. Power and Uranium: Bull, Bear, and Range

### The bull case
- The capacity auction cleared at its cap and still fell 6.5 gigawatts short; the scarcity is measured, not forecast
- Demand is concentrated, price-insensitive, and contracted years ahead; supply is queue-bound, equipment-bound, and transmission-bound — the constraints are in series
- 70 gigawatts of new large load by 2038 against 15 retired since 2022, with the monitor's own $23 billion revenue attribution confirming the driver
- Firm carbon-free capacity commands demonstrated premium pricing (the nuclear PPAs); uranium's term price at an 18-year high with the enrichment countdown to 2028
- Retail price escalation of ~6% a year has political momentum behind *more* supply, not less

### The bear case
- The demand forecasts are the weak link: the market monitor questions their credibility, double-counting is real, and the 425–970 terawatt-hour range is a confession
- An AI capital-expenditure slowdown would cancel the marginal campuses precisely because the load is concentrated in a handful of decision-makers
- Supply is responding: 11 gigawatts through the reliability initiative, withdrawn retirements, fast-track reforms, and behind-the-meter build-out all arrive 2027–2029
- Efficiency per unit of compute improves relentlessly; the load per model-query falls even as queries grow
- Political backlash — curtailment rules, price caps, state FERC complaints — can ration demand onto the loads themselves, capping the price signal investors are buying

### The range-bound case
- Capacity prices stay pinned at or near administrative caps through 2028/29 while reforms slowly clear the queue; the shortage is managed by curtailment rules and BYONC rather than resolved; retail prices grind higher; no blackout, no glut

### What would settle it
The next two capacity auctions. Clearing below the cap with the shortfall closed means supply is arriving and the squeeze peaked; clearing at the cap with the shortfall widening means the constraints still bind. Secondarily: hyperscaler capital-expenditure guidance, which is the demand side's single point of failure, and the turbine manufacturers' delivery schedules, which are the supply side's.

# PART V — CROSS-CUTTING DRIVERS

## 23. Energy and Inflation

Energy is the inflation input that moves policy fastest, because it is visible weekly at the pump and it propagates into everything transported, heated, or manufactured.

**The 2026 demonstration is complete.** The Hormuz conflict raised oil and European gas; headline inflation re-accelerated on both sides of the Atlantic; the ECB hiked in June for the first time since 2023 explicitly on the energy shock; the Fed's rate path shifted from expected cuts to a hiking debate; and the December-2025 dollar-bear consensus collapsed — the sequence the currency paper (VIII) documents. Energy → inflation → policy → the discount rate on every asset. That is the transmission, and in 2026 it ran end to end in four months.

**The power addendum.** The new wrinkle is electricity's growing weight in the inflation basket at exactly the moment its price trend turned structural. Retail power at +6.3% year over year with mid-single-digit escalation projected through 2027 is a *persistent* rather than spike-shaped contribution — a slow-burn inflation floor that does not mean-revert with a ceasefire, because its driver is the capacity shortfall, not the war. Monthly Macro's inflation pillar owns the transmission; this paper's contribution is the observation that the energy component of inflation is acquiring a structural floor for the first time in two decades.

## 24. Energy and the Dollar

Oil is priced in dollars, so dollar strength raises the local-currency oil price for every non-US consumer — the same rationing mechanism as in metals. The correlation is loosely inverse, and the exceptions carry the information: oil and the dollar rising together, the 2026 configuration, marks a supply shock with a haven bid, and it transmits maximum pain to energy-importing emerging markets, whose currencies absorb both blades.

The petrodollar channel belongs to the currency paper (VIII): Gulf export revenues recycled into dollar assets are a structural dollar support, and any move toward non-dollar oil settlement — the mBridge corridor — would show up in Gulf reserve behaviour first. The 2026 twist is that the conflict cut Gulf export *volumes*, which cuts the recycling flow itself — a small, temporary version of what de-dollarised settlement would do permanently.

**US energy independence** is the structural fact beneath both channels: the US is a net energy exporter, so an oil shock is no longer a US current-account shock. It is a transfer from US consumers to US producers, regionally redistributive and roughly neutral nationally — one reason the dollar now behaves as a petro-adjacent currency in supply shocks rather than the oil-importer currency it was in 1974 or 2008.

## 25. Energy and Equities

**The sector decoupled from the commodity.** Energy equities spent 2021–2026 out-earning their 2010s selves at every oil price, because the capital-discipline regime — flat production, dividends and buybacks over growth — converted the sector from a volume business into a cash-return business. The consequence for signal-reading: energy-equity performance no longer forecasts the commodity; it tracks the payout. The commodity signal lives in the curve and inventories, not in XLE.

**The power trade moved next door.** The 2024–2026 re-rating of independent power producers, nuclear fleet owners, and turbine manufacturers is the equity market pricing the capacity squeeze of Part IV — and it has run far enough that the equities now embed multi-year escalation. The discipline from the metals paper applies: the equities are a levered, expectation-laden claim on the thesis, not the thesis itself.

**Energy as the anti-tech hedge.** The 2026 correlation structure has energy equities rising on the same headlines that hurt long-duration tech (oil shocks → rates), making a modest energy sleeve one of the few intra-equity diversifiers that worked this year. The power complex complicates this: IPPs and turbine makers are now partly an AI-capex derivative, correlated with the hyperscalers that are their customers. Energy-the-hedge and energy-the-AI-play are different books and should be sized as such.

## 26. Energy and Metals

The bridge to paper VII, in both directions. Energy is the cost curve for all mining — diesel, electricity, and acid set the floor under copper, gold, and silver production costs, and the 2026 sulfuric-acid episode showed a cheap energy-adjacent input binding a metal's supply. In the other direction, the electrification build-out that drives copper demand is the same grid build-out that Part IV describes: transformers, lines, and substations are copper intensity, so the power squeeze and the copper thesis are one structural story read through two commodities. The metals paper owns copper; the cross-reference is the point.

# PART V-B — POSITION CONSTRUCTION

This Part is educational description of structures commonly used, not a recommendation. Energy instruments carry roll, basis, and operational risks that differ sharply by commodity; suitability and tax treatment vary; none of this is investment advice.

## 27. The Instrument Set — and the Roll Problem

**Futures.** CL (WTI, 1,000 barrels), BZ (Brent), NG (Henry Hub, 10,000 MMBtu), RB and HO (products), plus micro contracts for oil. Deep, liquid, capital-efficient, and governed by the curve: in backwardation a rolled long *earns* positive roll yield — the current oil configuration — while in contango it bleeds. Gas lives in persistent seasonal contango outside winter, which is why NG longs are a timing instrument, not a holding.

**The ETP pathologies, quantified.** USO (oil) and UNG (gas) hold front-month futures and roll mechanically. In contango regimes the roll cost has run 10–30% annually for UNG — the fund has lost over 95% of its value since inception across periods when spot gas was roughly flat. These products track *spot* over days and destroy capital over years, and the 2020 episode (USO forced to restructure as the front month went negative) is the permanent exhibit. Rule: futures-based energy ETPs are tactical instruments with a measured decay rate, never allocations.

**Equities as the practical long-term vehicle.** For multi-year energy exposure the equity complex is the least-bad instrument, accepting equity beta in exchange for escaping the roll: integrated majors and E&Ps (cash-return machines levered to price), midstream and MLPs (volume-linked toll collectors, the "own the buffer" expression for storage and transport), refiners (crack-spread exposure), and the power complex — regulated utilities in load-growth territories, independent power producers, turbine manufacturers. For uranium: the physical trusts (closed-end vehicles holding U3O8, the cleanest commodity expression, trading at NAV premia/discounts that are themselves a sentiment gauge) and the miners.

**Options.** Deep on oil futures and the major ETFs; oil implied vol is regime-priced off OVX (see XIII for the vol framework). Defined-risk structures around scheduled catalysts — OPEC+ meetings, EIA Wednesdays — and collars on equity positions with embedded gains.

## 28. Near-Term Construction

**Calendar spreads as the tightness trade.** Long front / short deferred expresses deepening backwardation — the buffer draining — with far less flat-price risk than outright length, and it is the professional's expression of the Part II curve read. The reverse spread expresses normalisation (Hormuz resolution) without shorting a war headline outright.

**Crack spreads.** Long products / short crude isolates refining margin — the play when product inventories are tight but crude is disrupted-and-recovering, and the cleaner expression of demand strength than flat-price crude.

**Event structure.** The weekly EIA prints (crude Wednesday, gas Thursday) are scheduled volatility; OPEC+ meetings and ceasefire headlines are the unscheduled kind. The 2026 lesson from the \$40 July range: defined-risk options structures around headline risk, because the gap risk in both directions exceeds any stop-loss discipline.

**The geopolitical-premium fade, updated.** Fading supply-disruption spikes was a decade-long winning trade that 2026 punished — the disruption persisted. The updated rule: fade the spike only when the buffer is adequate (spare capacity reachable, inventories near normal). With inventories below the five-year low and spare capacity trapped behind the strait, the fade has no cushion, and the trade is the spread expression, not the outright.

**Sizing.** Oil realised vol runs 30–45% in disrupted regimes, gas 50–80%, power and uranium equities 40%-plus. Equal-risk sizing against a gold book means energy positions at one-half to one-third the notional. The January gas spike — 4x in three weeks, fully retraced — is the sizing exhibit.

## 29. Long-Term Construction

**The "own the buffer" thesis.** If the organising claim is right — price is set by the buffer — then the durable long-term energy position is ownership of the buffer itself: storage and midstream assets that earn more as scarcity pricing steepens the curve; spare-capacity-adjacent cash-return producers; and, in power, the firm capacity that the auctions are repricing. This is the energy analogue of the metals paper's stock-versus-flow conclusion: own what is scarce across the cycle (deliverability), not the flow forecast.

**The power-scarcity allocation.** The emphasis translated into construction: regulated utilities in data-centre territories (rate-base growth with commission-capped but rising returns), IPPs with unhedged existing firm fleets (the purest capacity-price torque, and the most extended), turbine and grid-equipment manufacturers (the backlog is the moat), and the nuclear complex — fleet owners with PPA repricing ahead, uranium physical trusts against the 2028 enrichment countdown. Staged entries respect that much of the 2024–2026 re-rating already happened; the auction calendar provides the catalysts.

**What not to hold for the decade.** Front-month futures products (the roll), single-country gas exposure ahead of the 2027–2030 LNG supply wave, and refining pure-plays into a peak-gasoline world. And the oil view itself is better held small: the ten-year oil question (Part VII-B) is genuinely two-sided in a way the power question is not.

**The barbell.** A defensible structural book pairs the power-scarcity complex (the growth leg, correlated to AI capex) against cash-return hydrocarbon producers (the value leg, correlated to geopolitical disruption) — the two legs hedge each other's principal risk, since the AI-bust scenario that hurts the first tends to ease rates and the second's political risk, while the conflict scenarios that hurt equities broadly pay the second.

# PART VI — READING ENERGY SIGNALS

The operational core: the buffer, read commodity by commodity, with the false signals named.

## 30. The Signal Hierarchy

**Oil, in order:** (1) inventories — US commercial crude against the five-year band, Cushing, floating storage, and the SPR level; (2) the curve — front-to-sixth and front-to-twelfth month spreads, the backwardation depth; (3) spare capacity estimates *adjusted for reachability* — the 2026 amendment; (4) Gulf export volumes (tanker tracking) against the recovery path; (5) refinery utilisation and crack spreads; (6) shale activity — rigs, frac spreads, DUCs — as the medium-term supply response; (7) positioning (COT managed-money); (8) flat price, last.

**Gas, in order:** (1) storage against the five-year band, with the South Central/LNG-adjacency read; (2) weather models — the only short-term demand variable that matters; (3) LNG feedgas daily flows; (4) associated-gas trajectory via the oil rig count — the cross-commodity supply signal; (5) TTF/JKM and the arbitrage spread to Henry Hub netbacks; (6) European storage percentage against the five-year minimum; (7) positioning; (8) price.

**Power, in order:** (1) capacity auction results — clearing price against the cap, shortfall against the requirement; (2) hyperscaler capex guidance — the demand side's single point of failure, quarterly; (3) interconnection-agreement energisation rates — signed gigawatts actually connecting; (4) turbine and transformer backlog disclosures from the three manufacturers; (5) reserve-margin projections and retirement withdrawals; (6) locational price spreads and congestion revenue — the transmission constraint's price; (7) retail-price politics — curtailment rules, price caps, FERC complaints — as the demand-rationing signal.

**Uranium, in order:** (1) term price and contracting volume — the utility market, which is the market; (2) the enrichment/SWU price and Russian-waiver expirations against the January 2028 deadline; (3) restart and uprate announcements; (4) physical-trust NAV premium/discount as the financial-demand gauge; (5) spot, last — it is the residual, not the market.

## 31. The Physical Tells

The energy-specific dislocations that reflect metal actually moving, parallel to the metals paper's Section 28: **prompt spreads** blowing out (single-month backwardation spikes mark acute delivery scarcity); **freight rates** — VLCC rates surged with Hormuz war-risk premia, and tanker rates are a real-time strait-stress index published daily; **basis blowouts** — Waha (Permian) gas trading negative marks takeaway saturation, and locational power spreads mark transmission binding; **refining margins** decoupling from crude (product scarcity versus crude scarcity are different diseases); and **the SPR release decision itself** — a policy tell that the administration judges the buffer inadequate.

## 32. Positioning and Sentiment

COT managed-money net length in crude and gas, percentile-ranked, contrarian at the tails — the crude net length hit extremes on both sides within 2026, marking the March spike and the June ceasefire trough. Options skew: call skew in crude marks disruption-hedging demand (the current state); put skew marks glut fear. The physical-trust premia in uranium. And the OVX regime read, owned by paper XIII: oil vol leading equity vol has been the reliable 2026 sequence.

## 33. Cross-Asset Confirmation

Oil versus the dollar (rising together = supply shock with haven bid — the current state). Oil versus breakevens and the front-end rate path — the inflation transmission's live wire; a crude rally that five-year breakevens ignore is being read as temporary. Gas versus power forwards — power forwards rising faster than gas marks the capacity squeeze, not the fuel, as the driver. Uranium versus the IPP complex — both pricing the same firm-capacity thesis; divergence means one is wrong. And energy equities versus crude: the decoupling of Section 25 means confirmation now runs through the curve, not the sector.

## 34. False Signals

**Reserves-to-production ratios.** Decades of "reserves" say nothing about deliverability this year; the buffer is capacity, not geology.

**Peak-oil and peak-demand dates.** Both camps have been serially wrong; the IEA's peak-demand date has drifted later in successive reports. Use the debate as a capex-behaviour input, never as a price call.

**Headline OPEC+ quotas.** Quotas are announcements; tanker-tracked exports are facts. The gap between them is chronic and directional.

**Rig counts as a supply forecast.** Productivity per rig moved so much that the 2015–2019 rig-count models broke; frac spreads and completions data are the better series, and even they lag DUC drawdowns.

**Single-hub inventory data.** Cushing can be tight while the Gulf Coast is glutted; the tariff-era metals lesson (relocation versus drawdown) applies verbatim.

**Energy-equity performance as a commodity signal.** Decoupled by the capital-discipline regime; the sector now tracks payout policy and, in the power complex, AI-capex sentiment.

**Weather-normalized nothing.** Any gas analysis that does not start with degree-days is a narrative; the January 4x round-trip was weather, not regime.

**The capacity-price cap as a market price.** The 2027/28 auction cleared *at* its administrative ceiling — the printed price is a floor on scarcity's true value, not an equilibrium, and modeling revenue off the cap embeds a regulatory assumption, not a market one.

# PART VII — FORECAST RANGES

## 35. The Track Record

Energy forecasting has the worst record of any commodity discipline, and the 2026 vintage is its cleanest recent exhibit.

**The 2026 surplus that became a \$105 print.** In February 2026, Goldman Sachs — with the most-resourced commodity desk on the sell side — raised its Brent forecast to \$60 for the fourth quarter and \$64 for the year, and maintained a 2026 *surplus* forecast of 2.3 million barrels a day, assuming no Iran-related disruption. Kotak was carrying \$70. Four months later Brent traded through \$105. The analysis was not careless — it was explicit about its no-disruption assumption — and that is precisely the point: energy balances are conditional on the absence of the events that move energy prices, and the events are the forecast's excluded variable.

**The agencies revise monthly, by design.** The EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook cut its 2026 Henry Hub forecast from \$3.67 to \$3.44 in a single month — 6% — on terminal maintenance and storage; its Brent path has been revised each month of the conflict. The IEA's medium-term reports have moved the global peak-demand date later in successive editions. These are not failures of the agencies; they are the honest behaviour of forecasts whose inputs change faster than their publication cadence.

**The structural misses.** Shale was under-forecast by every major agency for most of 2011–2019 — the supply response that broke the 2014 "\$100 forever" consensus was invisible in the models until it arrived. Bank of America's 2024 call for \$135 uranium in 2026 met a spot peak of \$101 and a retreat. The pre-2022 European gas models had no scenario for the loss of Russian pipeline supply. Each miss is the same miss: a step-change in the buffer that a balance model, extrapolating flows, could not contain.

**What forecasts are for.** The *conditions* attached to them (Goldman's "assuming no disruption" is the most useful clause in its note), the *revisions* and their stated drivers, and the dispersion across houses as a measure of uncertainty. Not the levels.

# PART VII-B — THE TEN-YEAR VIEW

## 38. Oil: Peak Demand and What It Does to the Buffer

**Plateau with a long tail (~50%).** Demand crests near 105–107 million barrels a day around 2030 and declines slowly — EVs erode gasoline, petrochemicals and aviation hold the floor. The buffer paradox arrives: underinvestment ahead of an anticipated peak thins spare capacity *before* demand falls, producing a decade of episodic spikes on a flat demand trend. Price volatility rises even as the level's trend is flat.

**Later peak (~30%).** EV adoption slows, developing-economy demand outruns efficiency, the IEA's date drifts past 2035 as it has drifted before. Spare capacity is drawn down continuously; \$90–110 becomes the equilibrium that finally revives long-cycle investment.

**Earlier peak (~20%).** Chinese demand declines outright, EV penetration accelerates globally, and the 2027–28 restoration of Gulf supply meets a shrinking market. OPEC+ cohesion fails on the way down — the 1986 template at decade scale. \$50–60 with spare capacity abundant and unused.

**The discriminating observable:** long-cycle upstream sanctioning (deepwater FIDs, greenfield projects) against the demand trajectory. Under-sanctioning into flat demand is the volatile-plateau world; sanctioning into declining demand is the glut.

## 39. Gas: The Henry Hub Linkage

**Partial convergence (~45%).** US export capacity grows in step with supply; Henry Hub prices at a structural discount to world netbacks that narrows from today's extreme to \$3–6/MMBtu but never closes; domestic power demand absorbs the difference. The US retains its industrial gas advantage, diminished.

**Full linkage (~30%).** Export capacity outruns supply growth; Appalachian takeaway stays blocked; associated gas stalls with oil investment; Henry Hub converges on TTF-minus-shipping and imports world volatility. The US power squeeze inherits global gas risk — the scenario in which the barrels-to-megawatts shift becomes a gas-price shock to the grid.

**Discount widens (~25%).** The 2028–2030 supply wave (Qatar plus US) meets soft global demand; TTF/JKM collapse toward \$7–8; US capacity is stranded at the margin and Henry Hub decouples downward. The LNG developers' worst case and the domestic power sector's best.

**The observable:** feedgas as a share of dry gas production, trending; when exports exceed roughly 25% of production with takeaway still blocked, linkage is winning.

## 40. Power: The Firm-Capacity Settlement

**Sustained scarcity, gas-resolved (~40%).** AI load lands at the 550–650 TWh central range; turbine backlogs clear 2028–30; new gas capacity plus nuclear restarts and uprates close the shortfall by the early 2030s; capacity prices stay elevated but come off the cap; retail power runs 4–6% escalation for the decade; gas burn for power is the structural demand driver of the US gas market. The emphasis thesis in its base form.

**Nuclear renaissance, late (~25%).** The 2030s deliver what the 2020s priced: first SMRs grid-connected, HALEU and Western enrichment capacity built out past the 2028 countdown, fleet uprates and life extensions maximised, hyperscaler PPAs underwriting a new-build cycle. Firm carbon-free capacity earns a durable premium; uranium's term price holds a permanently higher floor. This layers on the first scenario rather than replacing it — the near-term gap is still filled by gas.

**Demand disappoints (~25%).** The AI-capex bust or the efficiency curve — load growth lands at the IEA's 425 TWh or below; the queue empties of withdrawn requests; the 2027–29 capacity build arrives into flat demand; capacity prices collapse back toward the double digits; IPPs and turbine makers de-rate; the curtailment rules never bind. The power squeeze is remembered as a two-auction phenomenon.

**The observable:** hyperscaler capex guidance against the interconnection-agreement energisation rate. If capex holds and energisations lag, scarcity persists; if capex breaks, the queue's withdrawal rate will show it within two quarters.

## Falsification Tests

**"Energy is a buffer market"** is falsified by a supply shock of 5 mb/d-plus producing a sub-\$15 price move with inventories at the five-year low — pricing insensitive to the buffer.

**"Spare capacity behind Hormuz is not spare capacity"** is falsified if OPEC+ demonstrably delivers 3 mb/d-plus of incremental supply to market during a constrained-strait period via bypass pipelines and alternative routing.

**"The power squeeze is structural, not a two-auction phenomenon"** is falsified by two consecutive PJM auctions clearing below the cap with the shortfall closed while hyperscaler capex guidance is unchanged.

**"Data-centre load growth is real"** is falsified by interconnection-agreement withdrawals exceeding energisations for four consecutive quarters, or by hyperscaler capex cuts of 30%-plus.

**"Henry Hub is bottlenecked from world prices"** is falsified by the TTF–Henry Hub spread compressing below \$5 while US export utilisation stays at capacity — meaning the link exists without the capacity.

**"The LNG supply wave produces a 2028–30 glut"** is falsified if TTF holds above \$15 after Qatar's North Field expansion and the US trains are fully commissioned.

**"Enrichment is the uranium constraint"** is falsified by SWU prices falling while term U3O8 rises — the bottleneck being mined supply after all.

# APPENDICES

## Appendix A: Glossary

**Associated gas** — Gas produced as a byproduct of oil drilling; responds to the oil price, not the gas price.

**Backwardation / contango** — Spot above / below futures; the curve's tightness / storage signal.

**Base Residual Auction (BRA)** — PJM's capacity auction, held roughly three years ahead of delivery; the market's price for the reserve buffer.

**BYONC** — "Bring Your Own New Capacity": PJM's proposed mechanism exempting large loads from curtailment if they secure dedicated generation.

**Capacity accreditation** — The share of a resource's nameplate counted as reliable at peak; low for solar and wind, high for gas and nuclear.

**Crack spread** — The refining margin: product prices minus crude.

**DUC** — Drilled but uncompleted well; a fast-response supply inventory.

**Feedgas** — Gas flowing to LNG export terminals; the daily export-demand signal.

**HALEU** — High-assay low-enriched uranium, required by most advanced reactor designs; a Western-enrichment bottleneck.

**Henry Hub / TTF / JKM** — The North American, European, and Asian gas benchmarks.

**Interconnection queue** — The backlog of generation and load projects awaiting grid-connection studies.

**Reserve margin** — Available capacity in excess of peak demand; the power buffer.

**Spare capacity** — Production that can be brought online within months without new investment; held almost entirely by OPEC+.

**SPR** — The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve.

**SWU** — Separative work unit; the enrichment service's unit and price.

## Closing Note

Energy prices are set by the buffer, and in August 2026 the four buffers read four different ways: oil thin, gas full, power in deficit, uranium constrained. A single "energy view" cannot hold all four — which is why the paper treats them as four markets and why the most important shift in the complex is the one the emphasis names. The scarcity, inelasticity, and multi-year lead times that defined a tight oil market now describe electricity: a grid operator offering its maximum permitted price and failing to buy enough capacity, then filing to ration the newest loads first. Barrels remain the cyclical story; megawatts are the structural one.

The practical consequence: own the buffer rather than the flow forecast, keep the oil view small and two-sided, hold the power-scarcity thesis with its single point of failure — AI capital expenditure — explicitly monitored, and read the next PJM auction as the most important scheduled number in the complex.
