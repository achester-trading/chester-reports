## Purpose and How to Use This Document

This paper sits underneath the others. Every asset in the Alternative Asset Report — gold, copper, Bitcoin, emerging-market equities, real estate — is priced in dollars, and half the theses in the companion papers on metals and digital assets are, at bottom, theses about the dollar. A view on those assets that does not include a view on the currency they are denominated in is incomplete in a way that eventually costs money.

The paper answers seven questions, in order:

1. How do currencies appreciate and depreciate, and what does that do to an economy?
2. How much of a currency's price is set by traders and how much by central banks?
3. Where do digital assets fit as a form of currency?
4. **Above all: is a dollar devaluation coming — what would drive it, how likely is it, is it inevitable on the debt-cycle logic, and how fast could it happen?**
5. What is the multi-year outlook for the dollar, euro, yen, pound, franc, yuan, and the other currencies that matter?
6. How does one hedge currency risk?
7. How does one trade currencies opportunistically?

**Three principles govern the writing.** The paper separates three things that "dollar devaluation" is used to mean and that are constantly conflated — cyclical depreciation against other currencies, loss of purchasing power against real assets, and loss of reserve status — because they have different drivers, different probabilities, and different speeds. It treats the Dalio debt-cycle framework seriously and states its strongest form before examining where it is unfalsifiable. And it is explicit that currency forecasting has the worst track record of any macro discipline, which shapes how the outlook sections are written.

**Timing note.** This is written on August 30, 2026, two days after Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole keynote as Federal Reserve Chair, in which he said the Fed still has "work to do" on inflation. The dollar index sits near 100 after a round trip: above 108 in early 2025, a 9.4% decline through 2025 — the largest first-half drop since 1973 — a four-year low near 95.9 on January 29, 2026, then a recovery to a 13-month high near 101.6 in late June as the Middle East conflict and a Fed pivot from expected cuts toward possible hikes reversed the consensus. Fed funds are at 3.50–3.75% with the market debating a hike rather than a cut. The ECB hiked in June for the first time since 2023. The yen touched 163.73 per dollar in late July — its weakest since 1986 — and drew the first coordinated US-Japan yen-buying intervention since 1998. The yuan is at a three-and-a-half-year high with the PBoC leaning against it. Gold, meanwhile, trades near \$4,400, and in 2025 gold surpassed US Treasuries as a share of global official reserves. That last fact is the most important datapoint in this paper, and Part IV explains why.

**Suggested use.** Part IV — the dollar — is the section this paper exists for; read it first if time is short. Parts I and II are foundations. Part V is the currency-by-currency outlook and will age fastest. Parts VI and VII are operational — signals, hedging, and trading. Part VIII is the monitoring cadence.

# PART I — HOW CURRENCIES WORK

## 1. A Currency Price Is Always a Pair

The first thing to internalise about currencies is that there is no such thing as the price of a currency. There is only the price of one currency *in terms of another*. The dollar cannot rise or fall in isolation; it can only rise or fall against the euro, the yen, gold, or a basket. This sounds trivial and is not, for three reasons.

**Every currency move is two stories.** When EUR/USD falls, it is either dollar strength, euro weakness, or both, and the distinction matters for everything downstream. The dollar index rallied roughly 2.6% in a month in mid-2026 — but the Fed's broad trade-weighted index barely moved, and its emerging-market counterpart was flat. The rally was financed by European currency weakness, not by a global dollar bid. An analyst reading DXY alone would have concluded global financial conditions were tightening; they were not.

**Indices are baskets with choices baked in.** DXY is 57.6% euro, 13.6% yen, 11.9% sterling, and small weights in the Canadian dollar, Swedish krona, and Swiss franc — a 1973 construction that contains no yuan, no Mexican peso, and no Korean won. The Fed's broad trade-weighted index reflects actual trade partners and includes China and Mexico heavily. The two frequently diverge, and when they do, the broad index describes the real economy better while DXY describes what most financial market participants are trading. Use both, and notice when they disagree.

**"Devaluation" needs a denominator.** A dollar that falls 10% against the euro while the euro falls 15% against gold has not devalued against the euro in any meaningful sense; both have devalued against gold. This is precisely the 2022–2026 configuration, and it is why Part IV separates depreciation (against other currencies) from debasement (against real assets). Most confused commentary on the dollar is confused because it does not specify the denominator.

## 2. The Four Channels

Currencies move through four identifiable channels. They operate simultaneously and on different timescales, and most forecasting error comes from weighting the wrong channel for the horizon in question.

### Channel 1: Interest rate differentials and carry

The dominant channel over weeks to a few years. Capital flows toward higher risk-adjusted yields, so a currency whose central bank raises rates relative to peers tends to appreciate, and expectations of future rate changes move the currency before the changes occur. The mechanism operates through two-year yield spreads more reliably than through policy rates themselves, because the spread captures the expected path.

The 2026 tape is the channel operating in real time. The consensus entering the year — Goldman, Deutsche Bank, and MUFG all targeting EUR/USD at 1.24–1.25 by year-end — rested on Fed cuts widening against a steady ECB. The Hormuz energy shock pushed inflation up on both sides, the ECB hiked in June, and the Fed signalled hikes rather than cuts at its June meeting. With both banks hawkish and neither providing a divergence signal, the pair fell from 1.20 to the mid-1.14s and has sat "stuck in the middle" since. The consensus was not wrong about the mechanism; it was wrong about the inputs.

**Carry** is the trade that expresses this channel directly: borrow the low-yielding currency, hold the high-yielding one, collect the differential. It works until it does not — carry trades are structurally short volatility, profitable in calm regimes and catastrophic in unwinds, which is why the August 2024 yen carry unwind produced one of the most violent cross-asset moves of the decade in a matter of days.

### Channel 2: Growth and capital flows

Over one to five years, relative growth and the investment flows it attracts dominate. Capital goes where returns are being generated, and the "US exceptionalism" bid of 2023–2025 — dollar strength driven by American equity outperformance and AI-related investment — was this channel operating with unusual force. It is also reversible: when relative growth narrows, the flows reverse, which is what the 9.4% DXY decline in 2025 partly reflected.

Portfolio flows and direct investment are observable with a lag through Treasury TIC data, balance-of-payments releases, and fund-flow trackers. They are slower-moving than rate expectations and more durable.

### Channel 3: Trade and the current account

The classical textbook channel, and the slowest. A country running persistent current-account deficits must finance them with capital inflows; if those inflows falter, the currency must fall until the deficit corrects. The United States has run current-account deficits for over four decades without the adjustment the textbook predicts, because the dollar's reserve role generates structural inflow demand — the Triffin dilemma, discussed in Part IV.

For non-reserve currencies this channel binds harder. China's July 2026 trade surplus of \$112 billion, with exporters converting roughly \$37 billion a month of proceeds into yuan, is the mechanical source of the yuan's 2026 strength against soft domestic demand — an external-accounts story overriding a domestic one.

### Channel 4: Risk, liquidity, and reserve status

The channel that overrides the other three in stress. In a genuine risk-off event, capital flees to the deepest, most liquid, most legally secure markets, and those are dollar markets. This is the "dollar smile": the dollar strengthens when the US economy is outperforming (Channel 2) *and* when the world is in crisis (Channel 4), and weakens in the benign middle when global growth is synchronised and risk appetite is high. The 2026 Middle East conflict has been a persistent Channel 4 bid, which is one reason the consensus dollar-bear call failed.

The Swiss franc and yen share the haven property in different degrees — the franc through Switzerland's institutional stability and current-account surplus, the yen through Japan's enormous net foreign asset position, which repatriates in stress. The yen's haven behaviour has been muted this cycle by the carry dynamics of Channel 1, which is itself informative.

### The long-run anchor: purchasing power parity

Over decades, exchange rates gravitate toward the level that equalises the price of tradeable goods across countries. PPP is useless for timing — currencies can deviate from it by 30% for years — and indispensable for identifying which currencies are structurally cheap or expensive. The yen at 160-plus per dollar is roughly 40% undervalued on most PPP measures, which is why the US Treasury's description of it as "substantially undervalued" is not controversial, and why the intervention had a fundamental rationale even if its mechanism was weak. The Swiss franc is persistently overvalued on PPP and persistently strong regardless — a reminder that PPP identifies the direction of long-run pressure, not the timing of its release.

## 3. What Appreciation and Depreciation Do to an Economy

A currency move is a price change that ripples through every cross-border relationship an economy has. The effects are mostly symmetric but arrive on different schedules, and the political economy of who wins and who loses determines how authorities respond.

### Depreciation

**Exports become cheaper for foreigners; imports become more expensive at home.** The trade balance should improve — but only after the J-curve: in the first months, contracts already signed and volumes already committed mean the country pays more for the same imports while export volumes have not yet responded, so the deficit initially *widens* before improving. The lag runs six to eighteen months.

**Imported inflation.** Every good priced in foreign currency costs more in local terms. For an energy importer like Japan, yen weakness feeds directly into household energy bills — which is why the 2026 yen slide became a domestic political issue, damaging real incomes and lifting inflation in an economy that spent thirty years wishing for inflation and now has more than it wants.

**Foreign-currency debt becomes heavier.** Borrowers who owe dollars but earn in local currency face a rising real burden. This is the mechanism behind every emerging-market currency crisis of the past forty years: depreciation raises the local-currency value of dollar debt, which forces selling of local assets, which drives further depreciation. Advanced economies borrowing in their own currency are largely immune; emerging markets with dollar liabilities are acutely exposed.

**Wealth effects run in both directions.** Domestic holders of foreign assets gain; foreign holders of domestic assets lose. Japan's net foreign asset position of over \$3 trillion means yen depreciation makes Japanese institutions wealthier in yen terms — which is part of why the political pressure to reverse it has been slower to build than the household-level pain would suggest.

**Competitive devaluation.** A depreciation that helps one exporter hurts its competitors, who face pressure to follow. The 1930s and the 2010s both featured rounds of "beggar-thy-neighbour" dynamics. The current 2026 configuration has a novel feature: the United States has publicly identified the yen as unfairly weak and has acted to strengthen it — a reserve-currency issuer intervening to weaken its own currency against a trading partner's, for the first time since 2011.

### Appreciation

Mirror image: exports lose competitiveness, imports cheapen, inflation falls, foreign-currency debt lightens, and domestic asset holders lose relative to foreigners. The costs concentrate in the tradeable sector — manufacturing and export agriculture — while the benefits diffuse across consumers, which makes appreciation politically harder to sustain than the aggregate arithmetic implies. The PBoC's August 2026 decision to set the yuan fixing 633 pips weaker than market estimates — the widest weak-side deviation in six months — was exactly this: a central bank braking an appreciation that its own external accounts were generating, because the export sector could not absorb it against soft domestic demand.

**Dutch disease** is the pathological case: a commodity boom appreciates the currency enough to hollow out the non-commodity tradeable sector, leaving the economy dependent on the commodity. Australia, Canada, and Norway have all managed versions of this with varying success; several emerging producers have not.

### The trilemma

An economy can have at most two of: a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and independent monetary policy. Every regime choice is a choice of which one to give up. The United States, eurozone, Japan, and United Kingdom float and keep the other two. Hong Kong and the Gulf states fix to the dollar and import US monetary policy. China manages its rate and keeps monetary autonomy by restricting capital movement — which is the single reason the yuan cannot be a reserve currency in the near term, regardless of China's economic size. Part V returns to this.

## 4. Exchange-Rate Regimes

**Free floats** — the dollar, euro, yen, sterling, franc, Australian and Canadian dollars. Central banks set rates and occasionally intervene but do not target a level.

**Managed floats** — the yuan (daily fixing with a 2% band), the Indian rupee (active RBI smoothing), the Thai baht, the Korean won, and most emerging-market currencies. Authorities lean against moves they dislike without defending a fixed level. The distinction matters for trading: managed currencies have lower realised volatility than their fundamentals imply, until a regime break, at which point volatility arrives all at once.

**Hard pegs and currency boards** — Hong Kong (7.75–7.85 to the dollar), the Gulf states, and a few others. The peg is only as credible as the reserves behind it and the political will to defend it. Speculative attacks on pegs — sterling in 1992, the Asian currencies in 1997, the Swiss franc floor in January 2015 when the SNB abandoned it and the franc gained 30% in minutes — are the most violent events in currency markets, and they happen when the trilemma's arithmetic overwhelms the authority's commitment.

**Currency unions** — the euro is the significant case. Members surrender monetary policy to a shared central bank while retaining fiscal sovereignty, which creates the structural tension that surfaced in 2010–2012 and remains latent: a currency can be too strong for Italy and too weak for Germany simultaneously, with no exchange rate to absorb the difference.

# PART II — WHO SETS THE PRICE

## 5. The Market

Foreign exchange is the largest financial market in existence, with daily turnover above \$7.5 trillion per the BIS triennial survey — roughly thirty times daily global equity turnover. The size matters for one reason above all: **no central bank can outspend the market for long.** Japan's July 2026 intervention deployed roughly \$59 billion in a single day, an enormous sum by any standard and less than one percent of daily global FX volume.

**Who trades.** Interdealer banks provide liquidity and warehouse risk. Corporates hedge trade and investment exposures — genuine end-users whose flows are large and information-poor. Real-money investors — pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, reserve managers — hedge or deliberately take currency exposure on portfolios; their flows are slow and structural. Hedge funds and CTAs take speculative positions; their flows are fast and momentum-driven. Retail is small in aggregate but concentrated in a few pairs. Official institutions — central banks and treasuries — intervene episodically and manage reserves continuously.

**The speculative share.** Roughly a third of turnover is speculative in the sense of not hedging an underlying commercial or investment exposure. That third sets the *path* — the day-to-day and week-to-week price action, the overshoots, the momentum. It does not set the *level* over anything longer than a few months, because speculative positions must eventually be closed, and closing them reverses their price impact.

## 6. The Central Bank Toolkit

Central banks affect currencies through five instruments of sharply differing power.

**Policy rates** are the dominant tool and work through Channel 1. This is not intervention in any conventional sense; it is simply that a central bank setting rates is setting the return on holding its currency, and capital responds. The Fed's pivot from expected 2026 cuts toward possible hikes moved the dollar more than any intervention could have.

**Forward guidance and communication** move currencies through expectations without any transaction. Warsh's "work to do" line at Jackson Hole moved EUR/USD to its lowest level in ten days on words alone.

**Balance-sheet policy** — quantitative easing and tightening — affects currencies through the supply of the currency and through its effect on term premia. The Fed's end of QT on December 1, 2025, holding its balance sheet near \$6.5 trillion and redirecting reinvestment into bills, was a modest dollar-negative through this channel and a more significant signal about fiscal accommodation, which Part IV takes up.

**Direct intervention** — buying or selling the currency in the open market — is the tool the public associates with central banks and the least powerful of the five. It works when it aligns with fundamentals and signals a policy shift, and it fails when it fights them. The historical record is unambiguous:

- Japan's solo interventions in 2022 and 2024 produced sharp but brief reversals; each time the yen resumed weakening within weeks because the rate differential that drove the weakness was unchanged.
- The Bank of England's defence of sterling in September 1992 failed within a day against a market that correctly judged the peg inconsistent with the UK's domestic needs.
- The Swiss National Bank's franc floor held for three years and then was abandoned in a single morning in January 2015, delivering a 30% move.
- The PBoC's daily fixing works because it is backed by capital controls, not because of the fixing itself.

The 2026 US-Japan operation is the most instructive recent case, examined in Section 8.

**Reserve management** is the slow, structural version of intervention. When reserve managers shift allocations — from dollars toward gold, as they have since 2022 — the flows are small relative to daily turnover but persistent for years, and persistence is what moves levels.

## 7. Traders Versus Central Banks: The Honest Apportionment

The question "how much of a currency is dictated by traders versus central banks" has a clean answer once the timescale is specified.

**Over days and weeks, traders dominate.** Positioning, momentum, and flow set the path. Central banks can interrupt it with intervention or surprise communication, but the interruption is temporary unless it changes the underlying differential.

**Over months to a few years, central banks dominate — through rates, not intervention.** Relative policy paths and the expectations around them set the trend. Traders position along that trend and amplify it; they do not create it. A currency does not sustain a multi-year move against its rate differential.

**Over the decade, neither dominates. Fiscal policy does.** Deficits, debt trajectories, and the political capacity to correct them determine the sustainable level of a currency's real value — the debasement question. Central banks are constrained by fiscal reality in the long run (this is what "fiscal dominance" means), and traders are price-takers of the resulting regime.

**Intervention sits outside this hierarchy.** It sets short-lived floors and ceilings and, when coordinated and credible, can reset positioning. It does not change levels for long unless the fundamentals happen to turn at the same time — in which case it gets credit it does not deserve.

**The practical rule.** For a trade with a horizon under three months, study positioning, flows, and the event calendar. For three months to three years, study relative central-bank paths and the two-year spread. For anything longer, study fiscal trajectories, current accounts, and reserve-manager behaviour. Applying the short-horizon toolkit to a long-horizon question — or the reverse — is the most common error in currency analysis.

## 8. Three 2026 Case Studies

### The yen: coordinated intervention meets an unchanged differential

USD/JPY reached 163.73 on a Thursday in late July 2026, its weakest since 1986. Japan's Ministry of Finance intervened alone that day with an estimated \$59 billion, then the following day conducted a coordinated operation with the US Treasury — the first joint yen-buying since 1998 and the first US-Japan coordination of any kind since the G7 weakened the yen after the 2011 earthquake. The pair fell to roughly 155–157. Treasury Secretary Bessent said the US "will not hesitate to participate in further joint intervention" and endorsed correcting the yen's "substantial undervaluation." The President described it as "a signal of friendship."

**The mechanics were unusual and revealing.** Reports indicate the US sold *euros* rather than dollars to fund its yen purchases. Coordinated intervention has traditionally been dollar-funded. The most plausible explanation — one that Robin Brooks at Brookings and several bank analysts advanced — is that Washington wanted to spare Japan from selling US Treasuries to finance its own intervention, which would have pushed US long-term yields higher at a moment when the ten-year already sat near 4.7%. The intervention protected the yen and the Treasury market simultaneously, and the second objective may have been the larger one.

**The fundamentals did not move.** Japan's policy rate is 1.0% against fed funds at 3.50–3.75%; the ten-year JGB yields 2.85% against 4.69% for Treasuries; the Bank of Japan owns half the JGB market; the Takaichi administration is expanding fiscal policy and has proposed cutting the consumption tax on food; and Japan's energy import dependence means the Iran conflict is a direct yen negative. Within two weeks analysts were describing the operation as "a success in slowing speculation but not yet a success in changing fundamentals." UBS's line was the cleanest: the yen "should continue to be supported more by intervention risk than by domestic monetary fundamentals."

**The lesson is the one Section 7 states.** Intervention reset positioning and bought time. It could not change the level for long, because the level is set by a differential that only the BoJ can close — and the BoJ has been reluctant to close it.

### The yuan: a central bank braking its own currency

The yuan strengthened roughly 4–5.5% against the dollar over the year to August 2026, moving from near 7.00 to the low 6.70s — a three-and-a-half-year high. The driver was external: a \$112 billion July trade surplus and sustained conversion of export proceeds. The PBoC responded with a run of weak-side fixings, culminating on August 25 in a midpoint 633 pips weaker than estimates, to slow an appreciation it considered inconsistent with an economy showing renewed domestic weakness.

**This is intervention working**, and the contrast with Japan is exact: the PBoC is leaning *with* the fundamentals' direction while controlling the pace, backed by capital controls that make the fixing binding. UBS expects the appreciation to continue — 6.60 by December 2026, 6.50 by mid-2027 — with the central bank controlling speed rather than destination. A managed currency's central bank can set the path; it cannot indefinitely set the level against its own balance of payments.

### The euro: a rate decision reverses a consensus

The ECB's June 11 hike — the first since 2023 — was a response to an energy shock, not to strength. But its effect on EUR/USD was to remove the divergence the bull case required. The consensus had been long euro on the expectation that the Fed would cut and the ECB would hold; instead both moved hawkish and the pair had no directional driver. Spanish inflation at 4.5%, its highest since 2023, and French at 2.7% keep further tightening in play. The euro's 2026 has been a demonstration of Channel 1 in its purest form: the pair went where the differential went, and the differential went nowhere.

# PART III — DIGITAL ASSETS AS CURRENCY

## 9. The Three Functions Test

Money does three things: it is a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. A currency is an asset that performs all three within some economy. The test is useful for digital assets because it separates what they *are* from what they are *marketed as*.

**Bitcoin** is a store of value by aspiration and by partial demonstration — its long-run appreciation is real, its volatility disqualifies it for most holders, and its correlation to risk assets contradicts the thesis in stress, as the companion paper documents in detail. As a medium of exchange it is used at the margins — cross-border transfers, sanctions evasion, a handful of merchant integrations — but the friction, finality lag, and volatility make it a poor payment rail for most purposes. As a unit of account it fails almost entirely: essentially nothing is priced in bitcoin, including bitcoin, whose price is quoted in dollars. **Bitcoin is a monetary asset, not a currency.** It competes with gold, not with the dollar.

**Stablecoins** are the reverse case. They are a medium of exchange in genuine and growing use — the settlement layer of crypto markets and an increasingly real payment rail outside them. They are a unit of account by construction, because they *are* the dollar. As a store of value they are exactly as good as the dollar plus issuer risk. **Stablecoins are currency. Specifically, they are dollars.**

**Central bank digital currencies** are sovereign currency in a new wrapper. They add nothing to the three-functions analysis — the digital yuan is the yuan — but change the plumbing in ways that matter for the reserve-currency question.

## 10. Stablecoins: The Dollar's Unexpected Ally

The most consequential and least-anticipated development in digital assets is that they have strengthened the dollar's international role rather than threatened it.

Stablecoin supply stands near \$300 billion, having set records through the 2026 crypto drawdown. Roughly 99% of it is dollar-denominated. Tether alone holds around \$188 billion, backed substantially by Treasury bills — making it one of the largest holders of short-term US government debt in the world. The GENIUS Act of July 2025 formalised this: payment stablecoins must be backed one-to-one by dollar reserves, which means every unit of stablecoin growth is a unit of structural demand for Treasuries.

**The geographic evidence is decisive.** In Brazil, 98% of first-quarter 2026 crypto purchases were stablecoins. In Argentina, over 70% of purchases on the largest local exchange were USDT or USDC. These are not trading flows. They are households and businesses in economies with unreliable currencies acquiring dollars through a channel that bypasses local banking restrictions and capital controls. Crypto rails have made dollarisation available to anyone with a phone.

**This is the opposite of what the de-dollarisation narrative predicted.** The technology that was supposed to route around the dollar has instead extended its reach into precisely the populations that were hardest for the dollar to serve. The US Treasury understands this — Bessent has described stablecoins as a mechanism for expanding dollar dominance and Treasury demand simultaneously — and it is a structural support for the dollar that did not exist five years ago. Part IV counts it among the countervailing forces.

**The caveat.** Stablecoins extend the dollar's *medium-of-exchange* role. They do nothing for its *store-of-value* role — a Brazilian holding USDT is exposed to dollar debasement exactly as a New Yorker holding a bank deposit is. Stablecoins strengthen the dollar against other currencies; they are irrelevant to the dollar against gold.

## 11. CBDCs and the Plumbing Question

The digital yuan is the most advanced major CBDC, with significant domestic pilot deployment and cross-border experimentation through the mBridge project with the UAE, Thailand, Hong Kong, and Saudi Arabia. The digital euro is in preparation. The United States has legislatively prohibited a retail Fed CBDC and chosen the private stablecoin route instead.

**What CBDCs change.** Cross-border settlement without correspondent banks, without SWIFT, and without dollar intermediation for bilateral trade. This is a real reduction in the dollar's *transactional* role for the specific flows that adopt it — China-Gulf oil settlement being the obvious candidate.

**What CBDCs do not change.** The reasons the yuan is not a reserve currency — capital controls, an illiquid bond market, and political risk to foreign holders — are unaffected by digitising it. A digital yuan is a yuan with better plumbing and the same trilemma constraint. The dollar's share of SWIFT payments has held near 45% for a decade, and mBridge volumes remain small. This is a channel to monitor for share loss in specific bilateral trade corridors, not a threat to the reserve system.

**Where Bitcoin fits, restated.** Bitcoin's relevance to the currency question is not as a currency but as a *reserve asset alternative* — the same role gold plays, with a fifteen-year track record instead of a five-thousand-year one, higher volatility, and no central-bank sponsorship yet. The Strategic Bitcoin Reserve, capitalised entirely from seizures and still structurally stalled eighteen months after its creation, is legitimisation without purchase. If a central bank ever buys bitcoin as reserves, that changes; until then, in the currency-system analysis, bitcoin is an option rather than a participant.

# PART IV — THE DOLLAR QUESTION

## 12. Three Things "Devaluation" Means

Nearly every argument about dollar devaluation fails because the participants are discussing different things. There are three, and they must be held separately.

**Devaluation-1: Cyclical depreciation against other currencies.** DXY falling from 108 to 96. This happens regularly, is driven by rate differentials and relative growth (Channels 1 and 2), reverses, and has occurred in 1985–87, 2002–08, 2017, and 2025. It is not structural and it is not what Dalio means.

**Devaluation-2: Loss of purchasing power against real assets — debasement.** The dollar buying fewer ounces of gold, fewer barrels of oil, less real estate, less of the CPI basket, over time. This is the fiscal-dominance outcome: a government with unsustainable debt allowing inflation to run above the return on its bonds so that the debt shrinks in real terms and bondholders bear the cost. It can occur *while the dollar is strong against other currencies*, if other currencies are being debased faster — which is exactly the 2022–2026 configuration.

**Devaluation-3: Loss of reserve-currency status.** The dollar ceasing to be the default denomination for reserves, trade invoicing, and global debt. This is the once-a-century event — sterling's transition to the dollar across 1914–1960 — and it is what the "empire" framing evokes.

**The three have different drivers, probabilities, and speeds.** Devaluation-1 is frequent, fast, and reversible. Devaluation-2 is highly probable on current fiscal trajectories, slow, and is already occurring. Devaluation-3 is low-probability on any decade horizon, would be slow and then sudden, and requires an alternative that does not currently exist. Conflating them produces the two characteristic errors: dismissing debasement because DXY is at 100 ("the dollar is strong"), and forecasting reserve-status loss because DXY fell 9% ("the dollar is collapsing"). Neither follows.

## 13. Where the Dollar Stands

**Against other currencies.** DXY near 100, roughly flat year-over-year after a round trip through 95.9 in January and 101.6 in June. The broad trade-weighted index near 120.5 and the emerging-market dollar index near 129 — both close to multi-year highs, indicating the dollar remains strong on a trade-weighted basis even where DXY is middling. On PPP measures the dollar remains overvalued against the yen by roughly 40% and against most emerging-market currencies by less, and roughly fairly valued against the euro and sterling.

**In official reserves.** The IMF's COFER data for the first quarter of 2026 put the dollar at 57.13% of allocated reserves, up from 56.42% the prior quarter, with the euro at 20.03%, yen 5.44%, sterling around 4.6%, and the renminbi at 1.99%. The dollar's share has declined from roughly 70% in 2000 — but the IMF itself notes that about half of recent quarterly changes are exchange-rate valuation effects, and constant-currency analysis shows central banks made minimal active changes to dollar allocations through 2025. The euro's apparent gains were entirely valuation; at constant rates central banks actually trimmed euro holdings.

**The single most important datapoint: in 2025, gold surpassed US Treasuries as a share of global official reserves.** The IMF attributes this almost entirely to gold-price valuation, which is true, and which does not diminish its significance — a valuation effect that large reflects a repricing that reserve managers chose not to rebalance away from, and it sits alongside four years of central bank gold purchases above 1,000 tonnes annually. The reserve-diversification that everyone has been looking for in the currency data is not there because it went into gold instead.

**In fiscal terms.** Federal debt has passed \$39 trillion. The deficit ran approximately \$1.83 trillion in fiscal 2024 and \$1.9 trillion in fiscal 2025 — roughly 6% of GDP outside recession, historically unprecedented. Interest costs run near \$11 billion per week, about 15% of federal spending. Roughly \$9.2 trillion of debt matured in fiscal 2025 and was refinanced at higher rates. Treasury has shifted issuance toward bills, the Fed has ended quantitative tightening and redirected reinvestment into bills, and Treasury has conducted bond buybacks. China's Treasury holdings have fallen to \$730.7 billion, the lowest since 2008, while Japan's have risen marginally to \$1.15 trillion and Japan has recently sold some. In January 2026, the dollar fell to a four-year low *while* long-term yields rose — the combination that Dalio identifies as the fiscal-dominance signature, and one that the 1970s produced repeatedly.

**In policy terms.** Fed funds at 3.50–3.75% under a new Chair whose first major speech was hawkish. US CPI at 3.4%. The market pricing a hike, not a cut. This is a configuration in which the Fed is *fighting* debasement rather than accommodating it — which matters enormously for the probabilities below, and which is fragile, because a hawkish Fed and a \$39 trillion debt are in direct conflict.

## 14. The Dalio Framework, Stated Fairly

Ray Dalio's "Big Debt Cycle" is the most influential structural argument for dollar devaluation, and it deserves to be stated in its strongest form before it is examined.

**The cycle.** Long-term debt cycles run roughly 75 to 100 years and pass through identifiable stages. Early on, debt finances productive investment and the economy grows faster than debt. Over decades, debt accumulates faster than the income needed to service it. Eventually — Dalio places the US in what he calls Phase 5 of the debt cycle and Stage 6 of the broader "Big Cycle" — debt service consumes so much income that the central bank must choose between defending the currency (holding rates high enough to attract creditors, at the cost of recession and fiscal spiral) and financing the government (holding rates low, at the cost of the currency). Historically, governments choose the currency. They do not default outright and they do not cut spending sufficiently; they "print and devalue" because that is the path of least political resistance. The result is what Dalio calls a "beautiful deleveraging" when managed well and a disorderly one when not.

**The five convergent forces.** Dalio's confidence that this is a genuine turning point rather than a rough patch rests on five factors reaching breaking points together: the debt dynamics above; widening wealth gaps and rising populism on both flanks, which make the political compromise that restructuring requires impossible; a rising power (China) challenging the incumbent; acts of nature; and technology-driven disruption. Any one is manageable. All five together, in his reading of five hundred years of cases, is the signature of a cycle ending.

**Why reserve status does not protect.** The strongest objection — that the US borrows in its own currency and its bonds are the world's collateral — is one Dalio addresses directly: the Dutch guilder and the British pound both held reserve status and both lost it, and they lost it because excessive debt and the devaluation used to manage it eventually undermined the currency's function as a store of wealth. Reserve status is a consequence of being a reliable store of value, not a substitute for it.

**The signals he cites as consistent with the template**, as of August 2026: Japan selling some US Treasury holdings; US long-term yields rising alongside dollar weakness; the Treasury buying its own bonds; creditors and central banks shifting from bonds to gold, exactly as in previous pre-war periods when creditors feared the debtor reserve country would devalue.

**The prescription.** Dalio and Treasury Secretary Bessent have both advocated reducing the deficit to 3% of GDP. Dalio's investor guidance is 5–15% in gold. His personal forecast is that his grandchildren "are going to be paying off this debt in devalued dollars" — devaluation as slow, distributed, and inescapable rather than sudden.

That last framing is the most important thing about the Dalio view for practical purposes, and it is routinely misread. **He is not primarily forecasting a crash. He is forecasting Devaluation-2 — slow debasement — as the base case**, with a disorderly episode as a tail risk if the political capacity to manage it fails.

## 15. The Critique

The framework is serious and the critique should be equally serious.

**The timing problem.** Dalio has been describing the US as late-cycle since at least 2019. The dollar index has been higher, lower, and flat since. Gold has done what the framework predicts; the dollar against other currencies has not. A framework that explains outcomes over decades but cannot say which decade is not useless — it identifies direction — but it is not a forecast, and it should not be sized as one.

**The comparison set.** The guilder and sterling lost reserve status to a successor that was larger, more productive, more open, and had deeper capital markets. There is no such successor today. The euro cannot fill the role without fiscal union; the yuan cannot without abandoning capital controls; no other currency is large enough; gold is a reserve asset but not a transactional currency; bitcoin is neither yet. Sterling's decline took forty-five years *with* a ready successor. The absence of one is not a minor detail — it is the reason Devaluation-3 is a different probability class from Devaluation-2.

**Japan.** The most powerful counterexample to debt-cycle determinism. Japan's government debt exceeds 250% of GDP, its central bank owns half the bond market, and it has been in "unsustainable" territory for twenty-five years. The yen has weakened substantially, which is Devaluation-2 operating — but Japan has had no crisis, no default, no disorderly episode, and remains a G7 economy with a functioning bond market. The debt-cycle framework predicted crisis for Japan in the 1990s, the 2000s, and the 2010s. What it delivered was slow debasement. That is evidence *for* Devaluation-2 and *against* the disorderly tail.

**The Fed is currently fighting, not accommodating.** Fiscal dominance means the central bank subordinates inflation control to debt service. In August 2026 the Fed is at 3.50–3.75% with a Chair signalling more tightening at 3.4% inflation. That is not fiscal dominance; it is the opposite. The framework's mechanism requires the central bank to capitulate, and the current central bank has not. Whether it *can* sustain that against \$39 trillion of debt is the live question — but the framework's base case has not yet been triggered.

**Stablecoins were not in the model.** A new structural source of Treasury demand — \$300 billion and growing, legally required to hold dollars — that extends dollarisation to populations previously outside it is a genuine countervailing force that the historical cases did not have.

**What survives the critique.** The direction. Debt at 120% of GDP with 6% deficits and no political constituency for consolidation does not resolve without some combination of inflation, financial repression, and real-asset repricing. Gold's behaviour since 2022 and the central-bank rotation from Treasuries into gold are the evidence that Devaluation-2 is not a forecast but a description. What does not survive is the implication of imminence or inevitability for the disorderly and reserve-loss outcomes.

## 16. Drivers of Devaluation

The forces that would produce or accelerate dollar devaluation, ordered roughly by weight.

**Fiscal trajectory.** The primary driver of Devaluation-2. Deficits at 6% of GDP in expansion, debt service at 15% of outlays and rising, no consolidation constituency. Every other driver is secondary to this one, because it is the reason the others would bite.

**Fed independence erosion.** If the central bank is pressured into accommodating fiscal needs — holding rates below what inflation warrants — Devaluation-2 accelerates and the disorderly tail thickens. The appointment of a new Chair in May 2026 under an administration that has publicly pressed for lower rates was watched for exactly this. The early evidence is the opposite of the fear: Warsh's first major speech was hawkish. That is a data point, not a settled question, and it is the single most important variable to monitor.

**Weaponisation and the sanctions precedent.** The 2022 freezing of Russian reserves demonstrated that dollar assets carry political risk for any holder that might find itself adverse to US policy. This does not drive de-dollarisation of *transactions*, where there is no alternative; it drives reserve diversification into gold, which is Devaluation-2 by a different route, and it is already visible in the data.

**Deliberate policy.** The intellectual case for engineered dollar weakness — articulated in Stephen Miran's 2024 paper on restructuring the global trading system, and popularised as a "Mar-a-Lago Accord" — holds that dollar overvaluation is a structural burden on US manufacturing and that a coordinated devaluation, on the 1985 Plaza model, would be in the US interest. The 2026 yen intervention — the US actively weakening its own currency against a trading partner's — is a small instance of this logic in practice. Tariffs are a related instrument. If this becomes policy at scale, Devaluation-1 arrives by design.

**The Triffin dilemma.** A reserve currency issuer must run persistent current-account deficits to supply the world with its currency, which structurally erodes the fiscal position that underpins confidence in the currency. This is a slow structural pressure rather than a trigger, but it explains why the fiscal trajectory is not simply a policy choice — it is partly a consequence of the reserve role itself.

**Loss of foreign Treasury demand.** China's holdings at 2008 lows and Japan selling at the margin. Foreign official holders have been net reducers; the marginal Treasury buyer has become domestic — money-market funds, banks, stablecoin issuers, and the Fed's reinvestment. That is sustainable as long as domestic demand is sufficient, and it is fragile because domestic buyers are more rate-sensitive than reserve managers.

## 17. Countervailing Forces

**There is no alternative.** The euro lacks fiscal union and a unified safe asset. The yuan has capital controls, a closed bond market, and political risk for foreign holders. The yen and franc are too small. Gold is a reserve asset, not a settlement currency. Bitcoin is neither at scale. Devaluation-3 requires a successor and there is none on the horizon.

**Network effects and market depth.** The dollar's share of SWIFT payments has held near 45% for a decade. Global trade invoicing, commodity pricing, and cross-border debt remain overwhelmingly dollar-denominated. Treasury market depth and the legal infrastructure around it have no peer. These are the stickiest forms of dominance and they change over generations.

**Stablecoins.** \$300 billion and growing, legally dollar-backed, extending dollarisation into emerging-market populations. A structural Treasury buyer and a structural dollar-transaction rail that did not exist in prior cycles.

**Relative fundamentals.** Every competing currency issuer has its own fiscal problem — Japan at 250% debt-to-GDP with a central bank owning half the bond market, the eurozone with structural growth failure and a periphery, the UK with gilt-market fragility, China with a property overhang and deflation. Devaluation is relative, and the dollar's competitors are not obviously better positioned.

**US growth and productivity.** The AI investment cycle and energy self-sufficiency are genuine comparative advantages that support Channel 2 flows. If US productivity growth outpaces peers, the dollar can be fiscally challenged and still relatively strong for years.

**A currently hawkish Fed.** As long as the central bank defends the currency, Devaluation-2 proceeds slowly and the disorderly tail stays thin. This is the most important countervailing force and the most reversible.

## 18. Probabilities and the Inevitability Question

Subjective, stated with the reasoning, on a five-to-ten-year horizon.

| Outcome | Probability | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Devaluation-2: continued slow debasement** — dollar loses purchasing power against gold, real assets, and the CPI basket at a pace above the return on cash and bonds, while remaining the reserve currency | **~65–70%** | Already underway and observable in gold's repricing and reserve-manager behaviour. Fiscal arithmetic has no consolidation path. Japan demonstrates this is the modal outcome for a reserve-currency issuer with excessive debt. |
| **Devaluation-1: material cyclical depreciation against other currencies** — DXY toward 85–90 | **~40%** (overlapping) | Consensus expected it for 2026 and was wrong. Requires Fed easing relative to peers or a deliberate policy shift. Plausible within the decade; not the current configuration. |
| **Fiscal consolidation** — deficit toward 3% of GDP, debasement pressure recedes | **~10–15%** | Dalio and Bessent both advocate it, and it commands rare bipartisan rhetorical support. No mechanism to deliver it is visible. |
| **Disorderly episode** — failed auction, yield spike with dollar decline, emergency Fed accommodation, gold and real assets gapping | **~10–15%** | The Dalio tail. Requires the Fed to capitulate or a confidence break in Treasury demand. Currently the Fed is fighting and domestic demand is absorbing issuance. The January 2026 yields-up/dollar-down episode is the pattern to watch for recurring. |
| **Devaluation-3: loss of reserve status** within ten years | **<5%** | No successor exists. Sterling's transition took decades with one. Even the disorderly scenario above does not produce this without an alternative. |

**Is it inevitable?** The honest answer has three parts.

Debasement against real assets — Devaluation-2 — is as close to inevitable as macro forecasting gets, because it is already happening and the fiscal path that produces it has no political off-ramp. The question is pace, not direction. Dalio is right about this, and gold's behaviour since 2022 is the proof.

A disorderly crisis is not inevitable and is not the base case. It requires a specific failure — Fed capitulation or a Treasury demand break — that has not occurred, and the current Fed is behaving in the way that prevents it. Japan is the evidence that reserve-currency debt problems can resolve through decades of slow debasement without a crisis.

Loss of reserve status is not inevitable on any horizon an investor should plan around. It requires a successor, and the technology that was supposed to provide one has instead extended the dollar's reach.

**The synthesis for positioning.** Own real assets — gold above all, because it is the one with demonstrated central-bank sponsorship — as the hedge against the high-probability outcome. Do not position for the dollar collapsing against other currencies as a base case; position for it *debasing against real assets while remaining strong against currencies whose issuers are debasing faster*. And watch the Fed's independence as the variable that separates the slow path from the fast one.

## 19. Speed: How Quickly Could It Happen

The historical episodes give a range of speeds, and the pattern across them is consistent: gradually, then suddenly.

**1971 — Nixon closes the gold window.** The decision was announced on a Sunday evening and was effective immediately. But the pressure had built over a decade — the London Gold Pool collapsed in 1968, French gold redemptions accelerated through 1970–71, and US gold reserves had fallen from 20,000 tonnes to under 9,000. The dollar then lost roughly a third of its value against the Deutsche Mark and yen over the following two years, and gold rose from \$35 to over \$180 by 1974. **Speed of the event: overnight. Speed of the build: ten years. Speed of the adjustment: three years.**

**1985 — Plaza Accord.** A coordinated, deliberate devaluation. DXY fell roughly 40% over the following two years, from above 160 to below 100. This is the fastest large dollar move in the floating era, and it was engineered by policy — the model for what a deliberate "Mar-a-Lago" devaluation would look like. **Two years for 40%, by design.**

**2002–2008 — the cyclical bear.** DXY fell from 120 to 72 over six years with no crisis and no coordination — Fed easing after the tech bust, twin deficits, and euro adoption. **Six years for 40%, organically.**

**Sterling, 1914–1960s — reserve-status loss.** Sterling remained a major reserve currency through two world wars, a 1931 gold-standard exit, a 1949 devaluation of 30%, and a 1967 devaluation of 14%, before the dollar fully displaced it. **Roughly forty-five years for Devaluation-3, with a ready successor.**

**The Japan template — 2012 onward.** USD/JPY from 76 to 163 over fourteen years, with intermittent interventions and no crisis. **Slow, persistent, and still running.**

**What this implies.** Devaluation-1 can happen fast — 40% in two years — if policy chooses it or if the Fed eases decisively against peers. Devaluation-2 runs at the pace of inflation minus the bond yield, which at current settings is slow but which can accelerate sharply if the Fed capitulates; the 1970s produced a 40% loss of purchasing power in a decade. Devaluation-3 takes decades and requires a successor.

**The leading indicators of acceleration**, in order of how early they would fire:

1. **Fed independence** — any explicit or implicit subordination of rate policy to fiscal needs, visible in rates held below inflation without a growth justification.
2. **The yields-up, dollar-down signature** — long-term yields rising while the currency falls, indicating creditors demanding compensation for currency risk rather than fleeing to safety. It occurred in January 2026. If it recurs and persists, the slow path is becoming the fast path.
3. **Auction stress** — weak bid-to-cover, rising tails, dealers absorbing more of the issuance.
4. **Central bank gold buying re-accelerating** — reserve managers voting with their balance sheets ahead of the currency market.
5. **Term premium expansion** — investors demanding more to hold long-dated Treasuries independent of the expected rate path.
6. **Domestic Treasury demand faltering** — money-market fund and bank absorption of bill issuance declining, since these are now the marginal buyers.

None of these is currently at an alarming level. Two — gold buying and the January episode — have fired once. The framework's value is that it says what to watch rather than pretending to know the date.

# PART V — THE MAJOR CURRENCIES: MULTI-YEAR OUTLOOK

A standing caveat for this Part: currency forecasting has the worst track record of any macro discipline. The 2026 consensus — a weaker dollar, EUR/USD at 1.24–1.25, USD/JPY at 146 — was published by the largest banks in December 2025 and was wrong within six months on every count, not because the analysis was poor but because the inputs changed. Treat every level below as a scenario, and the conditions attached to it as the substance.

### The Long View in Pictures

*The HTML edition embeds charts throughout this Part: for each major pair, a thirty-year panel (with the last five years shaded) above a five-year detail, plus the comparative figure below this note. Three lessons the pictures teach that the text cannot.

**First, the envelopes.** Major floating pairs traverse roughly 25–45% around their multi-decade midpoints, and full-range traverses take seven to fourteen years, built from trend legs of five to eight. Nothing in G10 goes to zero and nothing escapes its envelope; every pair on these charts has visited both of its extremes and returned. This is the visual case for Part I's claim that PPP anchors direction without timing — and the visual caution against extrapolating any five-year trend.

**Second, the violent exceptions are regime breaks, not trends.** The moves that stand outside the envelopes' pace — the baht's 55% collapse in six months in 1997–98, the franc's 30% morning in January 2015, sterling's overnight gap in June 2016 — all came from suppressed volatility releasing at once: pegs, floors, and political binaries. Managed calm is deferred volatility, which is the currency market's version of the volatility paper's central claim.

**Third, where "now" sits.** The yen is at the weak extreme of its thirty-year envelope — the largest gap between price and long-run range anywhere in G10, which is the chart form of Section 22's asymmetry argument. The franc sits at its strong extreme. The dollar index, sterling, and the yuan sit mid-range. The five-year panels show the post-2021 regime in detail: the dollar's round trip, the yen's stair-step to 163.7, the franc's 2025 surge, the yuan's managed drift.

## 20. The US Dollar

**Structural position.** Reserve currency without a successor; fiscally impaired; supported by market depth, growth, energy independence, stablecoin-driven demand, and a currently hawkish central bank. Overvalued against the yen and most EM currencies on PPP; roughly fair against the euro and sterling; strong on the broad trade-weighted index.

**The 2026 lesson.** The dollar-bear consensus failed because it assumed Fed easing, and the Fed did not ease. The dollar's path over the next several years is, first, a function of whether the Fed remains hawkish relative to peers — and that depends on inflation, which depends substantially on energy, which depends on the Middle East.

**Bull case (dollar strong, DXY 100–110).** Inflation stays sticky near 3–3.5%, the Fed holds or hikes, US growth outperforms on AI capex and energy, the Middle East conflict sustains the haven bid, and Europe and Japan remain fiscally and structurally weaker. This is a continuation of the current configuration and requires no change.

**Base case (DXY 92–100 over three years).** Gradual Fed easing as growth slows, rate differentials narrow, the haven bid fades with any Middle East resolution, and the deliberate-weakening policy impulse — tariffs, intervention, the Miran logic — operates at the margin. The dollar drifts lower against currencies while continuing to debase against gold.

**Bear case (DXY 80–90).** A Fed that capitulates to fiscal pressure under political stress, a policy-driven Plaza-style devaluation, or a disorderly Treasury episode. This is Devaluation-1 arriving fast, and it would be accompanied by Devaluation-2 accelerating.

**What to watch.** Fed independence above all; the two-year yield spread against DXY-weighted peers; the broad index versus DXY divergence; TIC data on foreign Treasury holdings; and the yields-up/dollar-down signature.

## 21. The Euro

**Structural position.** The second reserve currency at 20% of allocated reserves, and structurally capped there by the absence of fiscal union and a common safe asset. Persistent current-account surplus is supportive. Growth is structurally weak, energy dependence is a vulnerability (the Hormuz shock hit Europe harder than the US), and the periphery remains a latent fragility that surfaces when rates rise.

**Current configuration.** ECB at 2.25% after the June hike; eurozone inflation 2.9% with Spain at 4.5% and France at 2.7%; EUR/USD near 1.16, down from a 1.20 high, with 1.14 the critical support. The ECB has moved from the dovish partner to a hawkish one, which removed the euro's weakness but also the dollar's divergence.

**Bull case (EUR/USD 1.25–1.30).** Fed easing against a firm ECB, Middle East resolution lowering European energy costs, and German fiscal expansion sustaining growth. This was the December 2025 consensus and it remains the path if the Fed eases.

**Base case (1.12–1.22).** Both central banks near hold, no divergence, the pair range-trades on data. The "stuck in the middle" configuration persists.

**Bear case (1.02–1.10).** Energy shock deepens, ECB forced to hike into a slowdown, periphery spreads widen, or a European political shock. The euro has no haven property and sells off in stress.

**What to watch.** ECB-Fed two-year spread; BTP-Bund spread (periphery cohesion); TTF natural gas (the energy channel); German fiscal execution.

## 22. The Yen

**Structural position.** The most undervalued major currency on PPP — roughly 40% cheap against the dollar — and the one where the gap between fundamentals and price is largest and most persistent. Japan's net foreign assets exceed \$3 trillion, which should make the yen a haven; its 250% debt-to-GDP and a central bank owning half the bond market make normalisation slow; and its energy import dependence makes it a direct casualty of the Middle East conflict.

**Current configuration.** USD/JPY near 155–157 after touching 163.73 in July, the weakest since 1986. BoJ at 1.0% against Fed at 3.50–3.75%. The first coordinated US-Japan yen-buying since 1998 reset positioning but not fundamentals. Takaichi's fiscal expansion and the proposed consumption-tax cut on food push against the BoJ's normalisation. Analysts from UBS to State Street agree the yen is supported by intervention risk rather than by domestic monetary fundamentals.

**Bull case for the yen (USD/JPY 125–140).** BoJ normalises meaningfully — several hikes toward 2% — as domestic inflation and wage growth justify it; the Fed eases; the carry differential compresses; the 40% PPP gap begins to close. This is the multi-year direction the fundamentals point toward, and it has been pointing there for three years without arriving.

**Base case (140–160).** Gradual BoJ hikes, gradual Fed easing, intervention capping the top, the pair drifting lower slowly with violent episodes — the pattern since 2024. Intervention at or above 160 should be assumed.

**Bear case for the yen (160–175).** BoJ stalls, Fed hikes, Japanese fiscal expansion deepens, energy costs stay high. Intervention slows but does not stop it. The 2022–2026 trend continues.

**The asymmetry.** The yen is the major currency with the largest identifiable undervaluation, an official commitment (now bilateral) to correct it, and a central bank that has been too slow. When the BoJ eventually normalises, the move will be large — the August 2024 carry unwind demonstrated the speed. Position sizing in yen should reflect that its realised volatility understates its potential volatility.

**What to watch.** BoJ policy path and JGB ten-year yield; Ministry of Finance intervention (published monthly); Japanese life-insurer and pension hedge ratios; the CFTC JPY position, which marks carry crowding.

## 23. Sterling

**Structural position.** A mid-sized reserve currency at roughly 4.6% of allocations, with a chronic current-account deficit financed by capital inflows — the "kindness of strangers" dependency that makes gilt-market confidence the binding constraint. Rate-sensitive because the Bank of England has been relatively hawkish.

**Current configuration.** BoE at 3.75% with three MPC members already voting for 4.00%; UK CPI 2.9% with services inflation in the 3.4–3.7% range; GBP/USD near 1.355 and GBP/EUR near 1.17, both close to 2026 highs. Sterling's strength against the euro is explicitly a rate-differential story — 3.75% against 2.25% — not a growth story; Q2 GDP slowed to 0.4%. The political transition to Andy Burnham as Labour leader removed a risk premium, and the new Chancellor's autumn Budget is the next fiscal test, with gilt yields the pressure point.

**Bull case (GBP/USD 1.42–1.50).** BoE stays hawkish as the Fed eases, fiscal credibility holds through the Budget, and the rate advantage persists. Sterling has historically outperformed when carry favours it and risk is benign.

**Base case (1.28–1.40).** Rate differentials narrow slowly, the pair range-trades, GBP/EUR gives back some of its rate-driven gains as the ECB stays hawkish.

**Bear case (1.15–1.25).** A gilt-market episode — a fiscally expansive Budget that markets reject in the 2022 pattern — or a BoE forced to cut into weak growth. Sterling's deficit-financing dependency means confidence shocks hit it harder than its fundamentals alone would imply.

**What to watch.** Gilt yields around fiscal events; BoE-ECB and BoE-Fed spreads; UK services inflation; current-account financing data.

## 24. The Swiss Franc

**Structural position.** The purest haven currency: institutional stability, a persistent current-account surplus, and a central bank whose defining problem is a currency that is too strong. The franc is chronically overvalued on PPP and chronically strong regardless — the clearest demonstration that PPP identifies pressure, not timing.

**Current configuration.** USD/CHF near 0.80; SNB policy rate at or near zero, with the bank retaining the option of negative rates and intervention against franc strength. The franc appreciated sharply against the dollar in 2025 — nearly 10% in a single quarter — and has been a beneficiary of the Middle East haven bid in 2026.

**Multi-year view.** The franc's direction against the dollar is largely a dollar story inverted. Against the euro, the SNB will resist appreciation below roughly 0.90–0.92 EUR/CHF but has demonstrated (January 2015) that it will not defend a level against its own balance sheet indefinitely. The franc is the currency to hold for tail-risk protection and the wrong currency to hold for carry; it yields nothing and its appreciation is episodic and stress-driven.

**What to watch.** SNB sight-deposit data (the intervention footprint), EUR/CHF against SNB tolerance levels, and global risk conditions.

## 25. The Yuan

**Structural position.** The currency of the second-largest economy and the largest trading nation, at 1.99% of global reserves — a gap between economic weight and monetary role with no precedent, entirely explained by capital controls, a closed bond market, and political risk to foreign holders. The trilemma is the whole story: China has chosen monetary autonomy and a managed rate, and forgone free capital movement, which forecloses reserve status.

**Current configuration.** USD/CNY near 6.73, a three-and-a-half-year high for the yuan, up roughly 4–5.5% over the year from near 7.00. The driver is the external account — a \$112 billion July trade surplus and \$37 billion a month of export-proceed conversion — against soft domestic demand, a property overhang, and disinflation. The PBoC is braking with weak-side fixings. UBS projects 6.60 by December 2026 and 6.50 by mid-2027; consensus sits near 6.75 for late 2026 and 6.64 for late 2027.

**Base case (6.40–6.80).** Managed appreciation continues at the PBoC's chosen pace, driven by the trade surplus, with the central bank controlling speed. This is the fundamentals' direction and the authorities are leaning with it, not against it.

**Upside case for the yuan (6.20–6.40).** Chinese stimulus succeeds, domestic demand recovers, capital inflows resume, and the PBoC allows faster appreciation as a rebalancing tool and a trade-tension release valve — a view Goldman and Bank of America have advanced.

**Downside case (7.00–7.40).** Domestic weakness deepens into deflation, capital outflow pressure builds, tariff escalation resumes, and the PBoC tolerates managed depreciation to support exports. The CNH-CNY spread would widen first.

**The reserve-currency question, answered.** The yuan will not become a major reserve currency this decade. Digitising it does not change the trilemma. Bilateral settlement in yuan — with the Gulf, Russia, and mBridge participants — will grow and will marginally reduce the dollar's transactional share in specific corridors. That is share erosion at the edges, not displacement.

**What to watch.** The daily fixing versus model estimates (the PBoC's intent); the CNH-CNY spread (market pressure on the managed rate); trade balance and settlement data; PBoC easing steps; Chinese Treasury holdings.

## 26. Others of Relevance

**Australian dollar.** A commodity currency and China proxy with a well-run central bank. Trades with iron ore, copper, and Chinese activity, and as a high-beta risk-on expression. The copper thesis in the metals paper is, in FX terms, partly an AUD thesis.

**Canadian dollar.** Oil-linked, with a central bank that tends to lead the Fed in easing and an economy exposed to US trade policy — Section 232 copper tariffs and broader US-Canada trade friction are direct CAD negatives. Forecasts near 1.40 USD/CAD reflect that exposure.

**Indian rupee.** A managed float with an active RBI that smooths aggressively. India's structural growth story is the strongest in EM; the rupee's path is a slow depreciation trend against the dollar managed to a narrow band, with reserves used to prevent volatility. Not a carry or momentum currency — a policy-managed one.

**Mexican peso.** The highest-quality EM carry currency for most of the past decade, with a hawkish Banxico. Exposed to US trade policy and nearshoring flows in both directions.

**Brazilian real.** High carry, high volatility, fiscally fragile, and the epicentre of the stablecoin-dollarisation trend — 98% of Brazilian crypto purchases were stablecoins in early 2026, which is households voting on the real with their savings.

**Korean won.** A semiconductor-cycle currency and a high-beta expression of global tech and Chinese demand.

**Gulf pegs.** The riyal and dirham are fixed to the dollar and import Fed policy; their relevance is as the source of petrodollar recycling, and any move toward non-dollar oil settlement — the mBridge channel — would be visible here first.

**Thai baht.** A managed float with an interventionist central bank, sensitive to tourism, electronics exports, and Chinese demand. The Bank of Thailand smooths aggressively and has historically resisted both sharp appreciation (which hurts exporters) and sharp depreciation. Realised volatility is low; regime breaks are rare but the 1997 crisis began here. Relevant to Part VII.

**Gold, restated as a currency.** The metals paper covers gold as an asset. In the currency framework, gold is the anti-currency — the denominator against which all fiat is measured over long horizons — and in 2025 it surpassed Treasuries in official reserves. Every currency in this Part has debased against gold since 2022. That is the single most important cross-rate.

# PART VI — READING FX SIGNALS

## 28. The Signal Hierarchy

In order of weight, for a horizon of one to twelve months.

**1. Two-year yield spreads.** The single most reliable driver of G10 currencies over months. Compute the DXY-weighted two-year spread against the US; when the spot rate deviates from what the spread implies by more than a few percent, the deviation tends to close. MUFG's December 2025 model had the spread implying DXY near 95 when spot was 98 — the model was directionally right for January and then the spread itself moved.

**2. Central bank reaction functions.** Not the current rate but the *function* — what each bank will do in response to data. Warsh's Jackson Hole speech was a reaction-function statement: inflation must fall visibly before easing. The ECB's June hike was a reaction-function revelation: it will tighten into an energy shock. Reading these correctly is worth more than any model.

**3. Positioning.** The CFTC Commitments of Traders report for currency futures — the managed-money net position in EUR, JPY, GBP, CHF, AUD, CAD, and the dollar index — as a percentile rank over three to five years. Extremes precede reversals; the middle of the range is noise. The yen short at extremes has repeatedly preceded intervention.

**4. Cross-currency basis.** The premium paid to borrow dollars in the swap market beyond what interest differentials imply. Widening basis signals dollar funding stress — a leading indicator of Channel 4 dollar strength that fires before the spot market moves. Watch EUR/USD and USD/JPY three-month basis.

**5. Options market structure.** Risk reversals — the implied-volatility skew between calls and puts — show which direction the market is paying to protect against. Persistent yen-call skew in 2026 has reflected intervention risk. One-month implied volatility against realised shows whether the market is braced for an event.

**6. Official flow data.** Treasury TIC data (monthly, lagged) for foreign holdings; Japan's Ministry of Finance intervention disclosure (monthly); SNB sight deposits (weekly); PBoC fixing deviations (daily); COFER (quarterly). Slow, structural, and the only direct read on the reserve-manager channel.

**7. PPP and real effective exchange rate deviations.** Useless for timing, indispensable for direction over three-plus years. Identifies the yen as the major with the most fundamental pressure toward appreciation.

**8. Terms of trade.** For commodity currencies — AUD, CAD, NOK, BRL — the ratio of export to import prices is a fundamental driver that the currency tracks with a lag.

## 29. False Signals

**DXY as "the dollar."** It is 57.6% euro. It missed the 2026 broad-dollar picture entirely. Use the broad trade-weighted index alongside it.

**Reserve-share headlines.** Half the quarterly change is valuation. The de-dollarisation that matters is into gold and is invisible in COFER's currency shares.

**Intervention as a trend change.** It resets positioning. It does not change the differential. Every solo Japanese intervention since 2022 was faded within weeks.

**Trade balances for reserve currencies.** The US has run deficits for forty years. The textbook adjustment does not apply to the issuer of the reserve asset.

**Bank consensus.** The December 2025 consensus was unanimous and wrong within six months. Consensus in FX is a positioning indicator, and unanimous consensus is a contrarian one.

**PPP for timing.** The yen has been 30–40% undervalued for three years. The signal is real; the calendar is not.

**Short-term correlation to equities as a regime call.** The dollar's risk-on/risk-off behaviour flips with the driver of the risk move (Section 2, Channel 4). A rate-shock selloff and a growth-shock selloff produce opposite dollar responses.

**Debt-to-GDP as a currency forecast.** Japan at 250% has the weakest major currency; the US at 120% has the strongest on the broad index; Switzerland at 40% has a currency the SNB fights. Debt levels set the long-run debasement pressure, not the exchange rate against peers who are also indebted.

# PART VII — HEDGING AND OPPORTUNISTIC TRADING

This Part is educational description of structures commonly used, not a recommendation. Instrument suitability, tax treatment, and regulatory access vary by investor and jurisdiction, and none of this is investment or tax advice.

## 30. Hedging Currency Risk

### First, identify what is actually exposed

Currency risk hides. A US investor "with no foreign exposure" who holds an S&P 500 index fund owns companies earning roughly 40% of revenue abroad — a strong dollar reduces their reported earnings. A holder of gold is implicitly short the dollar against the anti-currency. A holder of emerging-market equities has two exposures — the equities and the currencies — and in many years the currency has been the larger. The first step in any hedging decision is an honest inventory: which cash flows, assets, and liabilities are denominated in what, and over what horizon.

### The instruments

**Forwards.** A contract to exchange currencies at a set rate on a set date. The workhorse for known future cash flows — a construction payment, a property purchase, a dividend stream. Available through banks and specialist brokers for retail sizes; over-the-counter, customisable, no upfront premium. The forward rate is not a forecast; it is the spot rate adjusted for the interest differential, which leads to the central fact of hedging.

**The cost of hedging is the interest differential.** Hedging a foreign-currency asset back to dollars *earns* the differential if US rates are higher than the foreign currency's, and *costs* it if lower. A US investor hedging yen exposure today is paid roughly 2.5–2.75% annualised to do so (US rates above Japanese); hedging Brazilian real exposure costs several percent (Brazilian rates above US). This is why hedged and unhedged versions of the same foreign asset can have very different returns over a decade — the hedge return is a carry position in its own right.

**Futures.** CME currency futures (6E euro, 6J yen, 6B sterling, 6S franc, 6A Australian dollar, 6C Canadian dollar, and micro contracts at one-tenth size) plus dollar index futures. Exchange-cleared, transparent, liquid in the majors, quarterly expiry with roll. Capital-efficient. Less flexible in size and date than forwards; ideal for portfolio-level overlay hedging rather than matching a specific cash flow.

**Options.** Puts and calls on currency futures or ETFs. Buy protection against an adverse move while retaining the upside if the move is favourable — the asymmetric hedge, for which one pays a premium. Appropriate when the exposure is uncertain in size or timing, or when the holder wants to keep participation. Collars — buying protection financed by selling upside — reduce or eliminate premium at the cost of capping the favourable outcome.

**Currency-hedged fund share classes.** For foreign-equity and foreign-bond holdings, hedged ETF variants (currency-hedged versions of international and Japanese equity indices, for example) roll one-month forwards internally. Convenient, cheap, and blunt — the hedge ratio is fixed at 100%.

**Natural hedges.** Matching liabilities to assets in the same currency. A foreign-currency mortgage against a foreign property is a natural hedge: if the currency falls, the asset and the liability fall together. This is usually the cheapest hedge available when it is available.

### Whether to hedge

Three principles from the institutional literature that survive scrutiny:

**Hedge liabilities and known cash flows; think carefully before hedging long-term assets.** A payment due in six months in a foreign currency is a pure risk with no compensating return — hedge it. A foreign equity holding held for twenty years has a currency component whose expected return is near zero and whose variance partly diversifies the equity risk; the case for full hedging is weaker and depends on the carry.

**Hedge ratios need not be 100%.** Institutional practice commonly runs 50% hedged as a neutral position for foreign equities, with deviations driven by carry and valuation. Half-hedged captures much of the variance reduction at half the carry cost or benefit.

**The carry decides the marginal case.** When hedging pays (high-rate home currency), hedge more. When it costs, hedge less. A US investor is currently paid to hedge yen, euro, and franc exposure and charged to hedge most EM exposure — which argues for full hedging of developed-market exposure and selective hedging of EM.

**Gold's special status.** Gold is the hedge against Devaluation-2, and it cannot itself be usefully hedged back to dollars — doing so removes the exposure that justified holding it. Gold is the one foreign-currency-like asset a dollar-based investor holds specifically *for* its currency exposure.

## 31. Applied: A Dollar-Based Investor with Baht Exposure

A worked case, because the principles are clearer in a specific situation, and because it matches a real one: a US-dollar earner with a property in northern Thailand and a house under construction there.

**The exposure inventory.** A baht-denominated liability stream — construction payments over the build period, in tranches, in baht — and a baht-denominated real asset, the property, held indefinitely. Income is in dollars.

**The liability is the hedgeable part.** Each construction tranche is a known or estimable baht cash flow on a rough schedule. If the baht strengthens against the dollar between now and payment, the dollar cost rises. This is exactly the exposure forwards exist for: a specialist FX broker will quote a forward to buy baht for delivery around each tranche date, locking the dollar cost now. The forward rate will reflect the US-Thai interest differential; with US rates above Thai rates, the forward baht is modestly cheaper than spot, which means the hedge is roughly free to slightly favourable. The alternative — a rate alert and a spot purchase when the level looks good — works if there is schedule flexibility and no deadline, and fails when a contractor's payment is due in a week.

**The asset is generally not worth hedging.** The property is a long-horizon real asset. Hedging it would mean maintaining a rolling short-baht position against an asset that is not being sold, paying transaction costs, and removing a diversifying exposure. The baht is a managed float with low realised volatility and an interventionist central bank that resists sharp moves in either direction — the risk is not a violent break but a slow drift, and slow drifts in a real asset's currency are usually accepted rather than hedged. The exception would be a specific intention to sell within a defined window, at which point the sale proceeds become a known future cash flow and the liability logic applies in reverse.

**The natural-hedge option.** A baht-denominated construction loan or mortgage, if available to a foreign owner, would match the liability to the asset and remove most of the currency question from the build. Whether it is available and at what rate is a local question; the structure is the cleanest hedge on the menu when it exists.

**The signal to watch.** USD/THB against the Bank of Thailand's intervention behaviour — the Thai authorities publish reserve data monthly and lean visibly against appreciation that hurts exporters. A period of baht strength that the central bank is resisting is a reasonable moment to advance forward purchases; a period of baht weakness the bank is tolerating is a reasonable moment to let spot purchases run.

## 32. Opportunistic Trading: The Strategy Families

Systematic currency returns come from four documented sources, each with a mechanism, a regime in which it works, and a regime in which it fails.

**Carry.** Long high-yielding currencies, short low-yielding ones, collecting the differential. The most-studied FX strategy and the most reliable in calm regimes. Its failure mode is total: carry is structurally short volatility, and unwinds — August 2024's yen episode being the recent canonical case — return years of accumulated carry in days. The discipline is sizing for the unwind rather than for the carry, and reducing exposure when volatility is unusually low, which is when crowding is greatest. Current carry configuration: long dollar against yen and franc pays; long Mexican peso and Brazilian real against dollar pays more with more risk.

**Momentum and trend.** Currencies trend more persistently than most assets because their drivers — central bank cycles — are slow-moving and persistent. Trend-following on one-to-twelve-month lookbacks has a long positive record in FX. Its failure mode is the reversal at regime turns, and its cost is whipsaw in ranges — the 2026 EUR/USD "stuck in the middle" configuration has been a trend-follower's graveyard.

**Value.** Long currencies undervalued on PPP or real effective exchange rate, short overvalued ones, on multi-year horizons. Works over three-plus years, loses for years at a time on the way. The yen is the current value long; the franc is the persistent value short that never pays.

**Event and policy.** Central bank meetings, intervention, and political events produce identifiable, dated volatility. Structures that express a view on the *reaction* — spreads around FOMC, the fade of solo intervention, positioning ahead of a BoJ meeting where a hike is under-priced — with defined risk. The 2026 calendar has an unusual density of these: the ECB September 10, the Fed September 15–16, the BoE September 17, the UK Budget in the autumn, every BoJ meeting with intervention risk attached.

**The dollar smile as a framework.** Long dollar when the US is outperforming *or* when the world is in crisis; short dollar in the benign, synchronised-growth middle. Most of the 2026 dollar tape resolves into this: the bear consensus was a bet on the middle, and the Middle East conflict plus a hawkish Fed put the dollar on both sides of the smile at once.

## 33. Instruments and Construction for Opportunistic Positions

**Futures** for directional G10 views — capital-efficient, liquid, clean two-way. The euro, yen, and dollar-index contracts are among the most liquid futures in any asset class. Micro contracts allow precise sizing. Quarterly roll cost is minimal in the majors.

**Options for defined-risk directional and event trades.** Currency implied volatility in the majors runs 6–10% annualised — low relative to equities or commodities — which makes outright option purchases relatively cheap and makes spreads less necessary than in metals or crypto. Buying yen calls ahead of a BoJ meeting where a hike is under-priced; buying dollar puts as a hedge against the Fed-capitulation scenario; risk reversals to express a directional view with vol financing. Long-dated options are the cleanest way to express the multi-year yen-appreciation view without paying the carry cost of a spot short-dollar position every day.

**ETFs** — dollar-index funds, single-currency trusts for the euro, yen, franc, and others — are accessible and liquid but carry expense ratios and, for the single-currency products, hold cash deposits whose yield may or may not be passed through. Fine for modest tactical exposure; futures are more efficient at size.

**Forwards via broker** for customised exposures and for hedging, as above.

**Cross-currency positions** — long one non-dollar currency against another (yen versus euro, sterling versus franc) — remove the dollar as a common factor and isolate a relative-policy view. EUR/JPY and GBP/CHF have been the cleanest 2026 expressions of BoJ-versus-others policy divergence.

**Sizing.** G10 realised volatility of 6–10% is low, which invites leverage, which is how currency trading destroys accounts. The relevant volatility is not the realised figure but the *event* figure — the yen moving 12% in three weeks in 2024, the franc moving 30% in a morning in 2015, sterling moving 10% overnight on the 2016 referendum. Size for the event, not the average, particularly in any currency with a peg, a managed float, or an intervention regime, where volatility is suppressed until it is not.

**The construction discipline for the dollar-debasement view specifically.** The high-probability outcome (Devaluation-2) is expressed through gold and real assets, not through short-dollar currency positions — because the dollar can debase against gold while strengthening against currencies debasing faster, as it has since 2022. A short-dollar currency position is a bet on Devaluation-1, which requires Fed easing relative to peers or a policy shift, neither of which is the current configuration. Conflating the two is the most common way to be right about the dollar's fate and lose money on it.

## Falsification Tests

**"Dollar debasement against real assets is underway"** is falsified by gold falling and holding below its 2024 levels while central banks turn net sellers — indicating the reserve rotation has reversed.

**"The Fed is fighting, not accommodating"** is falsified by the Fed easing while inflation remains above 3% without a clear growth deterioration to justify it. This is the single most important test in the paper.

**"A disorderly episode is a tail, not the base case"** is falsified by two consecutive quarters in which the yields-up/dollar-down signature dominates, auction metrics deteriorate, and term premium expands together.

**"The yuan cannot be a reserve currency this decade"** is falsified by material capital-account liberalisation — not digitisation, not bilateral settlement deals, but removal of controls on portfolio outflows.

**"The yen is structurally undervalued and will appreciate"** is falsified by the BoJ abandoning normalisation and the PPP gap persisting beyond 2028 with no policy response — at which point the framework has to accept that Japan's fiscal position has permanently changed the equilibrium.

**"Stablecoins strengthen the dollar"** is falsified by stablecoin growth shifting materially toward non-dollar denominations, or by supply contracting during a period of dollar stability.

## Cadence Summary

**Weekly:** two-year spreads, COT positioning, basis, risk reversals, broad-versus-DXY, stablecoin supply, and the yields-dollar relationship.

**Monthly:** TIC data, MoF intervention, current-account releases, real effective rates, auction review, Fed independence assessment.

**Quarterly:** COFER at constant currency, WGC central bank data, scenario probability rewrite for Part IV, explicit review of the falsification tests, and a written statement of which dollar outcome the quarter's evidence supported.

**As they occur:** central bank decisions, intervention, fiscal events, trade-policy actions.

# APPENDICES

## Appendix A: Glossary

**Carry** — Return from holding a higher-yielding currency funded in a lower-yielding one. Structurally short volatility.

**COFER** — IMF Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves; quarterly; roughly half of share changes are valuation effects.

**Cross-currency basis** — Deviation from covered interest parity in the swap market; negative basis indicates a dollar-funding premium.

**Devaluation-1 / -2 / -3** — This paper's taxonomy: cyclical depreciation against currencies; debasement against real assets; loss of reserve status.

**Dollar smile** — The dollar strengthens in US outperformance and in global crisis, and weakens in the benign middle.

**Fiscal dominance** — The condition in which monetary policy is constrained by the need to keep government financing costs manageable.

**Fixing** — The PBoC's daily USD/CNY midpoint, around which spot may trade within a 2% band.

**Forward** — An OTC contract to exchange currencies at a set rate on a future date; the forward rate equals spot adjusted for the interest differential.

**J-curve** — The tendency for a trade balance to worsen before improving after depreciation.

**PPP** — Purchasing power parity; the exchange rate that equalises tradeable-goods prices; a long-run anchor, not a timing tool.

**Real effective exchange rate (REER)** — Trade-weighted exchange rate adjusted for inflation differentials.

**Risk reversal** — The implied-volatility difference between out-of-the-money calls and puts; measures directional skew in the options market.

**Trilemma** — At most two of: fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, independent monetary policy.

**Triffin dilemma** — A reserve-currency issuer must supply the world with its currency via deficits, eroding the fiscal position that underpins confidence in it.

## Closing Note

The dollar question is three questions, and answering them separately dissolves most of the apparent disagreement about the dollar's future. Against real assets it is debasing, has been since 2022, and the fiscal arithmetic says it will continue — Dalio is right about this and gold is the proof. Against other currencies it is strong, because its competitors are debasing faster and because the Fed is currently defending it. As the reserve currency it is unchallenged, because nothing exists to challenge it, and the technology that was supposed to has instead extended its reach.

The practical consequence is a positioning that looks paradoxical and is not: long gold as the debasement hedge, neutral-to-long dollar against most currencies, and a standing watch on one variable — the central bank's independence — that determines whether the slow path stays slow.
