### Reader's note

One sentence carries this paper, and the rest of the library is entitled to cite it without arguing it again.

**Every relationship the library teaches was measured inside a regime.** The regime is set by where the economy stands in the short and the long debt cycle, and by what the policy authorities do about it. The reader's first job in any decision, before a signal is read or a size is chosen, is to locate the present in the cycle and to know which of their measured relationships that location invalidates.

That sentence is easier to agree with than to use. Credit leads equities; gold moves against real yields; long bonds hedge equities; an inverted curve precedes a recession by six to eighteen months. Each of these is true in a sample, and each sample is a regime. Gold's relationship to real yields held for most of two decades and then broke in a single year. The stock–bond hedge that every balanced portfolio of the last generation was built on was the property of a disinflationary regime, and it inverted when the regime did. A reader who knows the relationships without knowing the regime they were measured in holds a set of tools without the instructions for when each one stops working.

The paper has six parts. Part I is the cycles: the short debt cycle, the long one, the operating regimes a heavily indebted state adopts, and the reserve currency, with the internal-order cycle beside the debt cycle as a lens of equal standing. Part VI is the closing apparatus every markets paper carries.

# Part I — The cycles

The debt cycle is the frame this paper uses because it is the most operational one available, not because it is the only one. Four other bodies of work see the same events from different sides, and the paper's diagnostic is built from their union.

**Minsky** explains why calm produces fragility: in a long stable period, borrowers and lenders extend leverage and degrade the quality of financing, migrating from *hedge* finance (income services principal and interest) to *speculative* finance (income services interest only, and principal must be rolled) to *Ponzi* finance (income services neither, and the position depends on the asset rising). **Reinhart and Rogoff** supply the long, multi-country record: crises recur, banking crises cluster and precede sovereign ones, and every generation believes its own case is different. **Koo** names the balance-sheet recession: after an asset bust, the private sector minimizes debt rather than maximizing profit, so it does not borrow however cheap money becomes, and monetary policy pushes on a string until the state borrows in its place. **Kindleberger** gives the anatomy of a mania — displacement, boom, euphoria, distress, revulsion — and the role of a lender of last resort in deciding whether distress becomes collapse. And **Dalio** supplies the vocabulary the paper uses for the cycles themselves: the short and long debt cycles, the "beautiful" deleveraging, and the four levers.

The lenses disagree at the edges, and the disagreements are useful. Dalio's frame expects a long cycle to end in a resolution; Koo's says a balance-sheet recession can last a decade without resolving, and Japan agrees with Koo. Reinhart and Rogoff's record says thresholds exist; the replication of their best-known threshold says the number does not. Minsky says the fragility is visible in the financing structure before the bust; Kindleberger says it is rarely acted on until distress begins. The paper does not choose among them. It asks, at each point, which lens a given observable belongs to, and it treats agreement across lenses as evidence and agreement within one lens as a single vote.

## Chapter 1 — The short debt cycle

**The short debt cycle is credit expanding faster than income for a few years and then contracting, with the central bank's policy rate as its regulator.** It runs on a clock of roughly five to ten years *(conceptual; rounded; the post-war U.S. business cycles, NBER dating, 1945–2020; last verified October 2026)*. Its four phases are the ones every allocation framework recognizes, and the reason to restate them is that each phase does something specific and different to each asset class.

**Expansion.** Income is growing, credit is growing with it, and the central bank's rate sits at or below neutral. Spending financed by borrowing raises someone else's income, which supports more borrowing: the loop is self-reinforcing on the way up. Equities rise on earnings growth; credit spreads tighten as defaults fall; the curve is positively sloped; commodities rise with demand; the dollar's direction depends on whether growth abroad is faster or slower. This is the phase in which most of the library's relationships were measured, and the phase in which they work as described.

**Tightening.** Growth runs ahead of capacity, inflation rises, and the central bank raises its rate to slow credit. The effect arrives with long and variable lags, so the bank tightens into an economy that is still accelerating and keeps tightening after it has begun to slow. Short rates rise faster than long rates and the curve flattens, then inverts. Credit spreads stop tightening first — lenders see the rising cost of rolling debt before equity holders do — and equity leadership narrows to the names that can grow through higher rates. Commodities may still be rising. This is the phase in which a reader who looks only at the tape sees nothing wrong.

**Recession.** Debt service rises faster than income, borrowers cut spending to service debt, one participant's cut is another's lost income, and the loop now runs downward. Defaults rise; credit spreads widen; earnings fall; equities fall ahead of the earnings; long yields fall as the market prices the coming easing; commodities fall; the dollar usually rises, because dollar debt abroad must be serviced in dollars and the scramble to find them bids the currency.

**Easing.** The central bank cuts its rate and, if the rate reaches zero, buys assets. Debt service falls, the cheapest borrowers return first, and credit begins to expand again. Equities typically bottom before the economy, on the second derivative — the rate of deterioration slowing — rather than on improvement. The curve steepens as short rates fall faster than long ones. Credit spreads peak near the equity low in a credit-driven downturn and long after it in an inflation-driven one, because in the second case the central bank cannot ease until inflation has broken.

**The point the phases make together** is that the short cycle is regulated by one price, the policy rate, and that every asset's behavior in it is a consequence of what that rate is doing to the cost of rolling debt. When the regulator is free to move — when inflation is low enough to permit easing and debt is low enough that the rate is not itself the problem — the short cycle is the whole story. When the regulator is constrained, the short cycle is happening inside a longer one, and the longer one decides what the regulator can do.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** A firm carries debt equal to three times its annual operating income, at an average rate of 4%, so interest takes 12% of operating income. The central bank raises its rate by four percentage points across eighteen months, and the firm's debt reprices over three years as it rolls. At the end of the roll, interest takes 24% of operating income. If operating income grows 5% a year meanwhile, interest takes about 21%; if a recession cuts operating income by a fifth, it takes 30%. The firm has not changed what it does, and its ability to invest, hire and service its debt has halved because of a price set by someone else. Multiply across the economy and the short cycle's downturn is visible in advance in the maturity wall, the schedule on which debt must be rolled at the new rate — which is why Credit treats the lending-standards cycle and the refinancing calendar, not the spread, as the cycle's clock.

**A historical instance.** The 2004–07 expansion and the 2007–09 recession are the textbook sequence, with one difference: the debt that broke was household mortgage debt held through leveraged financial intermediaries, so the recession phase arrived as a banking crisis rather than as an ordinary rise in defaults. The policy rate rose seventeen times in quarter-point steps from mid-2004 to mid-2006, the curve inverted in 2006, credit spreads troughed in mid-2007 and the equity market peaked four months later *(Tops and Bottoms, the 2007–09 episode; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it looked like on the day.** In the summer of 2007 the equity index was making new highs, unemployment was low, and the central bank's statements described the housing correction as contained. What was visible on the day was a set of facts that each had an innocent reading: two hedge funds of one dealer had failed, a large European bank had frozen redemptions in three funds because it could not value their holdings, and the spread between interbank lending rates and the expected policy rate had widened sharply. The tightening phase had ended a year earlier; the recession phase was already running in the funding markets; the equity index was still in the expansion.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Tightening | Rising | Flattening, then inverting | Stop tightening first | Leadership narrows | Often firm |

## Chapter 2 — The long debt cycle

**The long debt cycle is the accumulation, across several short cycles, of debt faster than the income that services it, until the policy rate can no longer be used to restart credit.** Each short cycle ends with debt higher relative to income than the last, because each recession is met with easing and each easing is met with more borrowing rather than with repayment. The policy rate's peak in each cycle is lower than in the one before, because a given rate does more damage to a more indebted economy. Eventually the rate reaches zero, or the debt is in a currency the borrower cannot create, and the regulator that ran the short cycle stops working. Dalio places the clock at roughly fifty to seventy-five years *(conceptual; rounded; Base Rates, Chapter 14, citing the long-cycle literature; last verified October 2026)*, which is why the cycle is invisible inside a career and obvious inside a century.

### 2.1 The frame stated

The frame's claims are structural rather than predictive. Debt grows faster than income for decades because each debt-financed expansion is easier than the alternative. Debt service consumes a rising share of income. A point arrives at which new borrowing is needed to service old borrowing — Minsky's migration from speculative to Ponzi finance, now at the scale of an economy or a state. The imbalance is then resolved, and it is resolved through some combination of four levers.

**The four levers.** *Austerity* — spending less — reduces debt slowly and income quickly, so on its own it raises the debt burden while it is being applied. *Default and restructuring* reduce debt directly and destroy the creditors' wealth, which is someone's income, so on their own they deepen the contraction. *Transfers* from those who have to those who have not — taxes, guarantees, bailouts — move the burden without reducing it. *Monetization* — the central bank creating money to buy the debt — reduces the real burden by raising nominal income and the price level, and on its own it is inflationary. Each lever used alone makes the deleveraging worse. The frame's practical claim is about the mix.

**What "beautiful" means.** A *beautiful* deleveraging, in Dalio's term, is one in which the deflationary levers (austerity and default) and the inflationary ones (monetization and transfers) are balanced so that nominal income grows faster than the nominal interest rate on the debt, and the debt-to-income ratio falls without either a depression or an inflation. The mechanism is the one the Monthly manual's Pillar 8 states as arithmetic: if growth runs above the interest rate, the ratio falls even with a modest primary deficit. A beautiful deleveraging is the deliberate engineering of that condition.

**When it fails.** It fails in three ways. The mix can be wrong in the deflationary direction — too much austerity and default, too little monetization — and the contraction feeds on itself, as in the United States in 1930–33 and in Greece after 2010. The mix can be wrong in the inflationary direction — monetization outrunning the debt's reduction, inflation expectations un-anchoring, holders fleeing the currency — as in Germany in 1922–23 and repeatedly in Argentina. And it can fail politically: the mix requires someone to accept losses, and when no constituency will, nothing is chosen and the resolution is chosen by the market. The internal-order lens below is about that third failure.

### 2.2 The two deleveragings

**The decisive variable is the currency the debt is owed in.** Debt in a currency the borrower cannot create — gold under a gold standard, a foreign currency, a currency union's common money — can be reduced only by the deflationary levers, so its resolution is deflationary: default, contraction, falling prices, and a currency that strengthens because everyone needs it to pay debts. Debt in a currency the borrower controls can be monetized, so its resolution is usually inflationary, in its mild form as financial repression and in its extreme form as a currency collapse. The two deleveragings share their antecedents — high debt to income, a policy rate that has lost its effect — and want opposite protection, which is the fact that matters for a portfolio and the reason Part V asks how to tell them apart early *(Base Rates, Chapters 14–15; the architecture's long-cycle tail scenario, ruled with two branches; last verified October 2026)*.

**The deflationary deleveraging.** Incomes and prices fall, the real burden of fixed nominal debt rises as they fall — Fisher's debt deflation — and the attempt to repay makes the burden heavier. Real yields rise even as nominal yields fall, because inflation falls faster. Long government bonds of a solvent sovereign protect; equities, credit and real estate do not; cash gains purchasing power; gold protects once the authorities revalue it or abandon the constraint. It ends when the constraint is removed — the gold peg is cut, the central bank is permitted to monetize — and the deleveraging becomes inflationary or beautiful.

**The inflationary deleveraging.** Nominal income rises, the debt's real value falls, and the cost is paid by holders of the debt and of cash, whose purchasing power is transferred to debtors. Real yields are negative and stay negative. Long bonds are the worst asset; gold, commodities and real assets protect; equities protect partially and unevenly, depending on whether the businesses behind them can raise prices. It ends when the central bank is permitted, or forced, to raise real rates above zero — at the cost of the recession the inflation was postponing.

### 2.3 The internal-order cycle, a coequal lens

**The debt cycle says which lever will be needed; the internal-order cycle says whether a society can agree to pull it.** This lens, adopted as an equal of the debt cycle rather than a footnote to it, watches the political capacity for the compromise a resolution requires. Its observables are slow and measurable: the share of national income going to labor against capital, the dispersion of wealth across cohorts, the polarization of the legislature, the share of the electorate voting for anti-system parties, trust in institutions, and the independence the central bank is in practice permitted. Its literatures are several. Dalio's *internal order and disorder* cycle traces how wide wealth gaps and a bad economy together produce populism and conflict; Strauss and Howe's generational *turnings* propose a roughly eighty-year rhythm of institutional crisis and rebuilding; Turchin's structural-demographic work ties instability to elite overproduction and falling relative wages. The paper treats the rhythms these authors propose — eighty years, a hundred — as fitted to a short sample and not as clocks. It uses their observables, which are measurable, and their mechanism, which is the argument that matters: a resolution is a decision about who loses, and a polarized society defers the decision until a crisis makes it.

**Why the two cycles interact.** The deleveraging levers are each a transfer of loss — to creditors (default), to savers (monetization), to taxpayers or beneficiaries (austerity and transfers). In a cohesive society the transfer is negotiated and spread; in a polarized one each group blocks the lever that falls on it, and the residual — the lever nobody had to vote for — is monetization through inflation, which arrives without a vote. **The internal-order lens therefore biases the branch.** High debt in the state's own currency, plus a society that cannot agree on austerity or on taxes, leans the resolution toward the inflationary branch, because inflation is the default lever when no other is chosen.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** A state's debt is 120% of its output and its primary deficit is 3% of output; the interest rate on its debt is 4% and nominal growth is 4%. The ratio rises three points a year. A balanced plan closes the primary gap over five years — half spending, half revenue — and the ratio stabilizes. Suppose instead that neither half can pass. Then the arithmetic offers one other route: raise nominal growth relative to the interest rate. If the central bank holds the interest rate on new debt at 4% while inflation runs at 6% and real growth at 1%, nominal growth is 7%, three points above the rate, and the ratio falls about three and a half points a year *(conceptual; rounded)* — without a vote, paid by every holder of the debt and of cash. That is financial repression, and the internal-order lens is why it is the common case.

**A historical instance.** The United States in the 1930s used every lever, in sequence, with the political order under strain: default on the gold clause of its own bonds in 1933, devaluation of the dollar against gold, transfers on a new scale, and a central bank that later financed the war at capped yields. The rearrangement was large and it held, because the polity, polarized as it was, reached a settlement. The contrast case is Germany a decade earlier, where the order did not hold and the monetary resolution was the extreme one.

**What it looked like on the day.** In a polarized period the debt cycle's signs and the internal-order cycle's signs arrive in different sections of the newspaper. On a day when the long bond sells off on a weak auction, the same edition reports a budget negotiation that has failed for the third time and a poll showing trust in the central bank at a low. A reader of the debt cycle sees a supply problem; a reader of the internal order sees why it will not be solved by consolidation. The diagnostic needs both pages.

| Transfers | Taxpayers to debtors | Neutral in total | Moves the burden without reducing it |

## Chapter 3 — Financial repression, fiscal dominance, yield-curve control and capital controls

**A heavily indebted state with debt in its own currency has four operating regimes available, and each is a way of making someone hold its debt at a yield below what they would choose.** They are regimes rather than events because they last for years and change every relationship measured outside them. The library's other papers own the plumbing of each — The Rate and Liquidity Machine owns the levers, Currencies the capital-account consequences — and this chapter owns what each regime does to each asset class and to the holder who is captive in it.

**Financial repression** is the set of rules that channel savings into government debt at yields below inflation: caps on deposit rates, requirements that banks, insurers and pension funds hold government bonds, limits on foreign investment, and a central bank that keeps the policy rate below inflation. The mechanism is a transfer from savers to the state that does not appear in any budget. Reinhart and Sbrancia, studying the advanced economies after the Second World War, estimated that the *liquidation effect* — negative real yields on the outstanding debt — retired a substantial share of the post-war debt each year in the countries that ran it *(Reinhart and Sbrancia, the liquidation of government debt, advanced economies 1945–1980; last verified October 2026)*.

**Fiscal dominance** is the condition in which the central bank's rate is set by the needs of the state's debt rather than by inflation. It is not a rule anyone announces; it is what remains when a central bank calculates that the rate inflation warrants would cause a fiscal crisis, and declines to set it. Its tells are a real policy rate that stays negative while inflation is above target, a central bank that describes rising inflation as transitory beyond the point the data support, and a term premium that rises when growth expectations fall — the market pricing supply rather than growth.

**Yield-curve control** is the explicit version: the central bank announces a ceiling on a government bond yield at a stated maturity and buys whatever is offered at that yield. It is effective while the ceiling is credible, because holders do not sell into a bid they know is unlimited; it becomes expensive when inflation makes the ceiling a free option for anyone selling, and the central bank's balance sheet then expands without limit. The United States pegged its long yield from 1942 to 1951; Japan targeted its ten-year yield from 2016 and widened, then abandoned, the band as inflation returned.

**Capital controls** close the exits: limits on moving money abroad, on buying foreign assets, on holding foreign currency. They are the regime that makes the others enforceable, because a saver who can leave will not accept a negative real yield. They are common in emerging markets under stress and were standard in the advanced economies from the 1940s to the 1970s.

**What each does to each asset class.** Under repression and fiscal dominance, nominal bonds lose real value steadily and long duration is the worst holding; cash loses less than long bonds but still loses; equities hold nominal value and lose real value unevenly, doing best where earnings reprice with inflation; gold and real assets protect; the domestic currency weakens against real assets and may or may not weaken against other currencies, depending on whether the other issuers are doing the same. Under yield-curve control the bond's price is pinned and the adjustment is pushed into the currency, so the currency is where the regime's cost appears. Under capital controls the domestic asset prices become prices *inside* a closed room: they can rise in nominal terms because savings have nowhere else to go, and the holder's real outcome depends on a conversion rate they may not be permitted to use.

**The captive holder.** Every one of these regimes works by creating a holder who cannot leave: a bank required to hold the bond as liquid assets, an insurer required to match liabilities with it, a pension fund benchmarked to it, a saver whose alternatives have been closed. Price, Time, and Edge's Chapter 15 calls this regulatory forcing; here it is the regime's engine. The captive holder is the clearest forced counterparty in finance, and it is the counterparty a free holder is trading against whenever the free holder declines the repressed asset.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** Under a repression regime, a ten-year government bond yields 2% while inflation runs at 5% for a decade. A holder who buys at par and holds to maturity receives their money back with interest and has lost about a quarter of its purchasing power *(conceptual; rounded: 1.02 to the tenth against 1.05 to the tenth)*. A holder of a broad equity index in the same decade, with earnings repricing at the inflation rate and a valuation multiple unchanged, has roughly kept pace in real terms. A holder of gold, if gold kept pace with the price level, has kept it too. The bond holder lost and nobody saw a crash.

**A historical instance.** The United States from 1942 to 1951: the central bank capped the long Treasury yield at 2.5% and the bill rate lower, inflation ran above 15% in the year after price controls ended, and the long-bond holder lost roughly a third of their purchasing power across the decade while the equity market did tolerably in nominal terms *(Base Rates, Chapter 11.4 and Chapter 14.2; The Rate and Liquidity Machine, the 1929–1951 era; last verified October 2026)*. The United Kingdom ran a longer and more thorough version from 1945 into the 1970s, with exchange controls, and retired a far larger debt burden. Japan since the 1990s is the third instance and the one that changed the frame: its central bank came to hold about half of the government bond market, and the debt burden did not fall while yields stayed near zero for two decades. The mechanism here was not a liquidation through inflation but a stabilization at zero, which the frame did not predict *(Currencies, Chapter 15; last verified October 2026)*. The emerging-market instances — Argentina, Turkey, and others — show the regimes under weaker institutions, where controls are imposed, evaded, tightened and abandoned within a few years.

**What it looked like on the day.** On a day inside a repression regime, nothing happens. The bond auction is covered, because the buyers are required to buy; the yield is stable, because it is capped; the newspaper reports inflation running above target and the central bank explaining why it will fall. The holder's loss is a line in a real-return calculation that nobody publishes. What a reader sees on the day is the absence of the adjustment that would happen in a free market — a yield that does not rise when inflation does — and the absence is the signal.

## Chapter 4 — The reserve currency

**A reserve currency is a debt that the rest of the world wants to hold, and that wish is the most valuable asset its issuer owns.** It lets the issuer borrow in its own currency at lower rates than it would otherwise pay, run deficits that others finance, and pass the cost of its own adjustments to holders abroad. Currencies owns the mechanism of exchange rates and the dollar question in full — the three meanings of devaluation, the strongest statement of the long-cycle argument for dollar debasement, and the critique — and Metals owns the official-sector gold bid. This chapter takes their conclusions as given and adds the long-cycle view: what the reserve role does to the debt cycle, on what timescale it erodes, and what erosion looks like from inside.

**What the reserve role does to the debt cycle.** It lengthens it. A reserve issuer's debt has a buyer the ordinary sovereign lacks — the foreign central bank that must hold reserves and the global financial system that uses the issuer's bonds as collateral — so the point at which debt service becomes a constraint arrives later and at a higher ratio. It also changes the branch. A reserve issuer's debt is in its own currency by definition, so its resolution is inflationary rather than deflationary, and the inflationary branch is paid in part by foreign holders. **The reserve role is the strongest single reason the long-cycle frame's crisis predictions for the largest debtors have failed on schedule**, and it is a reason with a mechanism rather than an excuse.

**The timescales of erosion.** Reserve status is lost slowly and then suddenly, and the slow part is long. Currencies describes three meanings of devaluation — a fall against other currencies, a slow loss of purchasing power against real assets while remaining the reserve, and the loss of reserve status itself — and they run on different clocks. The second can run for decades without the first or the third occurring. The third has historically required a successor: sterling's decline as the principal reserve took roughly half a century, from the First World War to the 1960s, and it took place with a ready successor *(Currencies, Chapters 12 and 15; last verified October 2026)*. Where no successor exists, the incumbent's share of reserves can fall slowly toward a plurality rather than collapsing.

**What erosion looks like from inside.** It does not look like a crisis. It looks like a set of slow shifts, each with an innocent explanation. The share of the issuer's debt held by foreign official buyers falls, and domestic buyers — banks, money-market funds, the central bank — take it up, at the price of being more sensitive to the rate. Central banks add gold to their reserves at a pace not seen in decades. The issuer's long yield rises on a day its currency falls, which is the opposite of the reserve-currency pattern, in which bad news for the world bids the issuer's bonds and its currency together. The term premium rises when growth expectations fall. And the political use of the reserve system — sanctions, frozen reserves — makes reserve managers diversify not out of the currency for transactions, where there is no alternative, but out of the issuer's bonds as a store of value.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** A reserve issuer's foreign official holders own a third of its marketable debt and stop adding to their holdings. The issuer runs a deficit of 6% of output a year. The third that foreigners used to absorb must now be bought domestically, by participants who require a higher yield to extend duration. If each additional percentage point of output that domestic buyers must absorb raises the term premium by a fraction of a point *(conceptual; rounded; the magnitude is model-dependent and the sign is the claim)*, the issuer's long yield rises while its growth outlook is unchanged — the supply signature. Nothing has been sold; the marginal buyer has changed.

**A historical instance.** Sterling across the twentieth century: two wars financed with debt, a reserve role held through the 1920s and 1930s by inertia and the sterling area's controls, devaluations in 1949 and 1967, and the end of the sterling area in the 1970s. The dollar's own break in August 1971, when the gold window closed, is the instance of a monetary-system change inside a reserve role that survived it: the dollar ceased to be convertible and remained the reserve, because no successor existed.

**What it looked like on the day.** On a day of erosion, the tape shows the long bond and the currency falling together while equities are flat or up, and the commentary attributes it to the auction, the budget or a rating agency. The gold price rises on the same day against a rising real yield, which its old model says cannot happen. Each fact has its own explanation. The long-cycle read is that they are one fact.

| Shift | Innocent reading | Long-cycle reading |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign official share of the debt falls | Reserve managers rebalancing | The marginal buyer becoming domestic and rate-sensitive |
| Central-bank gold buying at a record pace | Diversification | Doubt about the issuer's bonds as a store of value |
| Long yield up on a currency-down day | A bad auction | The reserve pattern inverting |
| Term premium up as growth expectations fall | Volatility | The market pricing supply rather than growth |
| Sanctions on reserves | Foreign policy | A political risk premium on the store of value |

# Part II — Locating the present

## Chapter 5 — The twelve diagnostic questions

**Locating the present is a measurement problem before it is a judgment.** The questions below are the union of the lenses in Part I: each is measurable from a public series, each belongs to one or more lenses, and each has a reading that would push the location toward one branch or the other. None of them has a threshold that decides anything on its own — Base Rates' Chapter 14.4 is right that no debt-to-income level has been shown to be critical — and the diagnostic reads them together, as one mechanism, which is how the architecture rules the long-cycle state is to be read. The present values are in Appendix B, dated; the questions are timeless.

**1. How large is the debt, and how much of income does servicing it take?** Debt to output is the stock; net interest to revenue is the flow that bites. The stock moves slowly and has no proven critical level; the flow is where the constraint becomes concrete, because interest must be paid before anything else and crowds out the rest. The diagnostic reads the flow's *direction* and the point at which it passes major discretionary categories of spending. Lens: Dalio, the Monthly manual's Pillar 8. Stored as `calc.fed_debt_total_pct_gdp` and `calc.interest_to_receipts`.

**2. Who holds the debt?** A debt held by foreign official buyers, domestic captive holders, the central bank, or price-sensitive private buyers is four different debts. The share held by the central bank and by captive domestic holders measures how far repression has already gone; the foreign official share measures how much of the marginal demand depends on the reserve role. A falling foreign official share with a rising domestic, rate-sensitive share is the long-cycle frame's early tell. Lens: Dalio, Reinhart and Rogoff. Stored through the Treasury's debt statements and the TIC flows (`fiscaldata.mspd_*`, the `tic.flow_*` official lines).

**3. Where are real rates against growth?** The comparison of the real interest rate the state pays with the real growth rate of the economy — *r* against *g* — is the arithmetic of the debt ratio. When *g* exceeds *r*, a modest primary deficit is sustainable; when *r* exceeds *g*, the ratio compounds upward unless the primary balance is in surplus. Real yields well below zero for years are repression; real yields above growth with a primary deficit are the deflationary branch's pressure. Lens: Pillar 8, Reinhart and Sbrancia. Stored as `calc.r_minus_g`, its average and marginal forms, and `calc.r_real_10y_cpi`.

**4. How much policy space is left?** Space is the distance between the policy rate and the point at which cutting it stops working — zero, or a level below which the currency or the banks break — and the room the central bank's balance sheet and the state's budget have to act. An economy entering a recession with a policy rate of five points has a regulator; one entering with a rate near zero has only the balance sheet and the budget. Lens: Koo, The Rate and Liquidity Machine. Stored as `fred.fed_funds` and `calc.real_policy_rate`.

**5. How leveraged is the private sector, and how good is its financing?** Household and corporate debt to income are the stock; the share of borrowers whose income covers interest but not principal is Minsky's financing quality. A private sector that is deleveraging while the state borrows is Koo's balance-sheet recession; a private sector levering into a boom is Minsky's migration under way. Lens: Minsky, Koo, Credit. Stored as `calc.household_debt_pct_gdp` and `calc.nonfin_corp_debt_pct_gdp`.

**6. Where are valuations against income?** Asset prices relative to the incomes that must eventually service them — equity prices against earnings across a cycle, house prices against household income, credit spreads against default experience. Valuation never times a top (Tops and Bottoms is explicit), but it sets the asymmetry: how far prices fall when the financing that supports them is withdrawn. Lens: Kindleberger, Minsky. Stored as `shiller.cape`, `shiller.real_home_price` and `fred.hy_oas`.

**7. What is the credit impulse?** The change in new credit as a share of output — the second derivative of debt. Spending responds to the *flow* of new borrowing, not the stock, so the impulse turns before activity does, and a contracting impulse in an economy with high debt is the earliest sign that the short cycle is turning down inside a long one. Lens: Dalio, Credit. Read from the lending and consumer-credit series the Monthly carries.

**8. What is the fiscal impulse?** The change in the state's primary deficit as a share of output. A widening deficit at low unemployment is the structural signature the long-cycle frame watches; a sudden narrowing is austerity, and in a weak economy it is the deflationary lever. Lens: Dalio, Koo, Pillar 8. Stored as `calc.deficit_ttm_pct_gdp`.

**9. Which inflation regime is the economy in?** The level of inflation, its persistence, and above all whether expectations are anchored: whether long-dated breakevens stay near the target when the current rate moves. An anchored regime lets the central bank look through a shock; an un-anchoring regime forces it to tighten into weakness or to accept the inflationary branch. Lens: Reinhart and Rogoff, The Rate and Liquidity Machine. Stored as `fred.breakeven_10y`, `fred.tips_10y` and the CPI and PCE series.

**10. What is the external position?** The current account, the net international investment position, and the share of the debt owed to foreigners and in foreign currency. A debtor that owes in its own currency to its own residents is in a different position from one that owes in dollars to foreigners, and the second is the deflationary branch waiting to happen. Lens: Reinhart and Rogoff, Currencies. Read from the international accounts and, for the dollar, `fred.real_dollar_broad`.

**11. What political constraints bind the central bank?** Whether the central bank can, in practice, raise the real rate above zero when inflation requires it: the legal independence, the appointment calendar, the public pressure on the rate, and the internal-order lens's measures of polarization and trust. This is the question that separates fiscal dominance from a central bank that is merely slow. Lens: the internal-order cycle. Not a stored series; read from the narrative register and the voices as a set of dated events.

**12. How many of the top-of-cycle conditions are present?** The checklist Part III builds from five long-cycle tops: leverage at an extreme in the sector the boom is financed through; a valuation far above income; a narrowing of leadership and breadth into the final advance; a policy authority tightening into an economy that is already slowing, or unable to; financing migrating toward Ponzi; and a narrative that explains why this time the old constraints do not apply. Lens: Kindleberger, Minsky, Tops and Bottoms. Read from the Top & Bottom report's overlays and the Monthly's slow layers.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** Two economies each carry government debt of 110% of output. In the first, net interest takes 8% of revenue, a third of the debt is held by the central bank and captive domestic institutions, real rates sit two points below growth, the private sector is deleveraging, and inflation expectations are anchored. In the second, net interest takes 18% of revenue and rising, the foreign official share is falling, real rates are a point above growth with a primary deficit of 4%, private leverage is high, and long-dated breakevens are drifting up. The headline ratio is the same and the two economies are in different places: the first is in a stable repression with room to wait, the second in the zone where the regulator is losing its effect and the branch is about to be chosen. *(conceptual; rounded)*

**A historical instance.** Japan in 1990 and the United States in 2008 scored similarly on the private-leverage and valuation questions and very differently on the policy-space question: Japan's policy rate stood at six points and its central bank was slow to use it, while the United States cut to zero inside fifteen months and bought assets. Japan's balance-sheet recession ran for more than a decade; the American one was shorter, in part because the regulator was used faster *(Koo; Tops and Bottoms and The Rate and Liquidity Machine for the U.S. sequence; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it looked like on the day.** On any given day, the twelve answers do not arrive together. The debt figure is annual, the interest figure quarterly, the holder data monthly with a lag, the breakevens daily. A reader on the day sees one or two of them move and is tempted to read the location from those. The discipline is the opposite: the location is read from the whole set at its slowest common cadence, which is quarterly, and a single daily move is evidence about the short cycle, not the long one.

## Chapter 6 — What the last hundred years can and cannot tell us

**The U.S. record contains one long debt cycle, and a sample of one cannot calibrate anything.** The period from the 1920s to the present holds a single complete passage of the long cycle in the United States: the leverage of the 1920s, the deflationary resolution of 1929–33, the repression of 1942–51, the long expansion of private and then public debt from the 1950s, the inflationary episode of 1966–82, and the long disinflation that followed. Japan supplies a second, compressed, from the late 1980s, and its resolution — two decades of stabilization at zero rates — is one the frame did not predict. The international record of Reinhart and Rogoff and of the long-run returns literature supplies more instances of crises, but many fewer complete long cycles, and they come from economies with different currencies, institutions and reserve positions *(Base Rates, Chapters 10–13, and the long-run returns literature it cites; last verified October 2026)*.

**The honest inference** is therefore narrower than the frame's popular form. The record supports three claims. First, the mechanisms are real: debt service constrains policy, the currency of the debt decides the branch, and repression is the common resolution for a state that borrows in its own money. Second, the timing is not estimable: a frame that has been right about direction and wrong about the decade for most of its history has no timing content, and Currencies says so of its own strongest statement. Third, the distribution of outcomes is wider than the U.S. record shows, because the record is the record of the century's most successful market and is conditioned on its survival.

**What the record cannot do** is tell a reader what happens under conditions it has not contained. It contains no instance of a reserve issuer with debt near its present ratio, no instance of a reserve issuer whose central bank holds a large share of its debt while inflation runs above target, no instance of a fiat reserve system's end, and no instance of the present internal-order readings coinciding with the present debt readings in the reserve issuer. Each of these is an n of zero, and an analogy drawn across an n of zero is a story, not an inference.

**How to reason about conditions that have not occurred.** The method is the one The Twenty-Five uses for its listed tails: reason forward from the mechanism, state that the empirical content is absent, and refuse a number the sample cannot support. Concretely, for a condition with no precedent, the reader asks three questions. Which mechanisms in Part I would operate, and in which direction? Which lever is blocked, and therefore which is the default? And which of the twelve diagnostic readings would move first if the mechanism were operating — so that the absence of precedent is replaced by a watch list rather than by an analogy. This is first principles rather than pattern-matching, and it is slower and less satisfying, and it is the only form of reasoning the sample permits.

**A worked example.** A reader asks whether the present resembles 1946. The analogy is tempting: debt near the post-war peak, inflation above target, a central bank under pressure. The mechanism method asks instead: in 1946 the debt was held largely by domestic captive holders under capital controls and a yield peg; is it now? It was a reserve issuer with no rival; is it now? Its inflation came from the release of wartime price controls into pent-up demand; does the present inflation have that source? The analogy breaks on the first question, and the mechanism survives: repression is still the default lever, but the captive holder it depended on must now be created by regulation rather than inherited from a war.

**A historical instance.** The frame's predictions for Japan are the cleanest case of an analogy failing where the mechanism held. Through the 1990s, 2000s and 2010s, commentators applying the frame expected a Japanese debt crisis. The mechanism — debt in the state's own currency, held domestically — predicted that no crisis was necessary and that the resolution would be slow; the analogy to debtors that had crises predicted one. The mechanism was right *(Currencies, Chapter 15; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it looked like on the day.** On the day a new condition arrives, the commentary reaches for the nearest precedent and names it — "this is 1970s inflation," "this is 2008 again." The mechanism reader writes down instead which precedent's mechanism is operating and which is not, and treats the named precedent's later path as one scenario among several rather than as the base case.

| What the record supports | What it does not |
|---|---|
| The mechanisms: debt service constrains policy; the currency decides the branch | The timing of a resolution |
| Repression as the common resolution in one's own currency | Any threshold of debt that triggers a crisis |
| A distribution of outcomes wider than the U.S. record | Analogies across conditions it never contained |

# Part III — Tops of long cycles, from inside

**This Part is about locating, not about tails.** Each of the five instances below is a moment when a long cycle's top was forming, told as it read at the time rather than as it reads now. Each carries the same six things, at the same weight: the diagnostics as they read on the day and what the frameworks of the day said about them; who was forced to act and by what; how long the top took to form and what being early cost; what resolved it; what it implies for a reader's stance, as distinct from a forecast; and what it looked like on the day. The fifth instance is the present, and it is told as a diagnostic: the same six things, with the readings left to the dated appendix.

**The boundary with the turning-points paper.** Tops and Bottoms, which is to become Turning Points, Bubbles, and Crises, owns the equity-market turning points since 1970 on one template, the anatomy of manias, and the signal-by-signal calibration the Top & Bottom report uses. This Part does not re-narrate its eight bears. It takes the macro-structural view of four tops — two of which, 1929 and 1989, fall outside that paper's sample — and asks what the long cycle was doing underneath each, citing that paper for the equity mechanics and Base Rates for the early-positioning numbers.

**Why being early gets equal weight.** At every long-cycle top, the diagnostics were visible for years before the top, and a reader who acted on them when they first appeared lost money, credibility or both before being proved right. The cost of being early is therefore not a footnote to the diagnosis; it is half of what the diagnosis is worth. Base Rates and Tops and Bottoms give the general form: breadth divergence leads valuation tops by months to years, curve inversion leads recession bears by six to eighteen months, credit-spread acceleration led the 2007 top by four months, and valuation never times anything *(Tops and Bottoms, the synthesis chapter, eight bears and six near-misses, 1970–2024; last verified October 2026)*. Inside a decline, three to five rallies of 5% or more are ordinary, and the largest is typically double-digit *(Base Rates, Chapter 2.3; last verified October 2026)*.

## Chapter 8 — 1929

**The diagnostics at the time, and what the frameworks of the day said.** By 1928 the debt diagnostics read extreme in the private sector and benign in the public one. Brokers' loans to buyers of stock on margin had multiplied through the decade, and the call-money market in which they were financed drew funds from corporations and foreign banks at rates far above what productive lending paid. Equity valuations against income were at levels without precedent in the record of the time. Leadership was concentrated in the new industries — radio, utilities holding companies, automobiles — and in investment trusts that were themselves leveraged claims on other stocks. The policy authority was constrained by the gold standard: it raised its discount rate in 1928 and 1929 to restrain speculation and to defend gold, and could not ease without risking the peg. The dominant framework of the day held that a new era of stable prices and scientific management had raised the sustainable level of valuations, and the most respected economists of the time said so in public. The minority view — that margin credit was financing speculation the economy could not support — was held, and stated, by a few bankers and commentators who were treated as cranks.

**Who was forced, and by what.** When prices fell in October 1929, the margin borrowers were forced first: brokers called loans, and the calls forced sales into a falling market. The lenders in the call market — corporations and foreign banks — withdrew their funds, which forced further calls. Across 1930–33 the forcing moved from the market to the banks: depositors ran on banks whose assets had fallen, banks sold assets to meet the runs, and the gold standard forced the central bank to tighten rather than ease when gold left. Fisher's debt deflation was the forcing mechanism of the whole episode: falling prices raised the real burden of debt, and the attempt to repay deepened the fall.

**How long the top took to form, and what being early cost.** The diagnostics that later read as obvious were present from 1927. A reader who exited at the start of 1928 on the margin and valuation evidence gave up a gain of close to a doubling in the industrial average before the September 1929 peak *(Dow Jones Industrial Average, January 1928 to September 1929; last verified October 2026)*. A reader who sold at the peak and bought back after the first crash, in November 1929, rode the counter-trend rally of roughly 46% to April 1930 and then lost about four-fifths of their capital to the 1932 low *(Base Rates, Chapter 11.2; last verified October 2026)*. Both mistakes were made by intelligent people with the same data.

**What resolved it.** Not the market. The decline ended when the constraint ended: in 1933 the United States suspended gold convertibility, devalued the dollar against gold, insured deposits, and later abrogated the gold clause in its own bonds. The deleveraging moved from the deflationary branch to a mix, and nominal income began to grow. The index did not regain its 1929 peak in nominal terms for twenty-five years *(Base Rates, Chapter 11.2; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it implies for stance.** That the top's diagnostic was a financing structure, not a price, and that the financing structure was visible years ahead. That a deflationary resolution is the one in which long government bonds of a solvent sovereign protect, and that the first bear-market rally is the most dangerous moment for a reader who was right. And that the resolution came from a policy change that removed a constraint, which means the reader's watch, after the first fall, was on the constraint, not on the price.

**What it looked like on the day.** In the week of 21 October 1929 the newspapers reported a sharp fall attributed to profit-taking and margin calls, and statements from bankers that the decline was technical and that the fundamentals were sound. A consortium of bankers bought stock publicly the next day to steady the market, and it rallied. Nothing in the statements of the day said that the financing structure of the decade had begun to unwind.

## Chapter 9 — 1989 (Japan)

**The diagnostics at the time, and what the frameworks of the day said.** Japan in 1989 read extreme on nearly every private-sector question. Bank lending against land had grown far faster than income; land in the capital was priced, by a widely repeated estimate, above the value of all the land in the United States; equity valuations against earnings were several times those elsewhere, sustained by cross-shareholdings that kept much of the float off the market. Corporations borrowed against land to buy securities and earned more from financial engineering than from operations. The policy authority had cut its discount rate to 2.5% after the 1985 agreement to weaken the dollar and kept it there into 1989 while asset prices rose *(The Rate and Liquidity Machine and the standard histories; last verified October 2026)*. The dominant framework of the day held that Japan's economic model — long-term relationships, patient capital, industrial policy — justified its valuations, that land prices could not fall because land was scarce, and that conventional valuation measures did not apply. Foreign analysts who said otherwise had been wrong for years, and were treated accordingly.

**Who was forced, and by what.** The central bank raised its rate from May 1989 to 6% by August 1990, and the finance ministry capped the growth of bank lending to real estate in 1990. Borrowers who had bought land with borrowed money could no longer roll the debt at the old rate or borrow more against rising prices. The banks were then forced in a way peculiar to Japan: they held the land as collateral and the shares of their borrowers as cross-holdings, so falling prices impaired both sides of their balance sheet at once, and the regulatory forbearance that let them avoid recognizing the losses kept them alive and unable to lend. Koo's balance-sheet recession followed: corporations used every yen of income to repay debt, and no rate cut could make them borrow.

**How long the top took to form, and what being early cost.** The index roughly tripled from the 1985 agreement to its December 1989 peak, and a reader early by two years — selling at the start of 1988 on the valuation and land-lending evidence — gave up a gain of roughly four-fifths before the peak *(Nikkei 225, January 1988 to December 1989; last verified October 2026)*. The cost of being right was then the length of the decline: the index fell about 82% from its peak to its 2009 low and took thirty-four years, to February 2024, to regain its nominal high *(Base Rates, Chapter 13.2; last verified October 2026)*. Land prices kept falling for most of fifteen years. A reader who bought the first large decline in 1990, on the argument that the economy was sound, owned the instrument through a generation of losses.

**What resolved it.** Very slowly, and only partly. Bank losses were recognized across a decade, with public capital injections at the end of the 1990s and a cleanup in the early 2000s. The policy rate reached zero in the late 1990s and the central bank pioneered asset purchases in the 2000s; neither restarted private borrowing while balance sheets were being repaired. The state's borrowing replaced the private sector's, and the government debt ratio rose to among the highest in the world while yields fell to near zero — the stabilization the frame did not predict.

**What it implies for stance.** That a balance-sheet recession can last longer than any reader's patience, so the stance after a long-cycle top in a country that borrows in its own currency and saves domestically is to expect the deflationary branch to persist rather than to resolve. That long government bonds were the asset of the era, for two decades. And that the analogy-driven expectation of a Japanese debt crisis, repeated through three decades, was the mistake of reading the wrong branch.

**What it looked like on the day.** On the last trading day of 1989 the index closed at its high and the year-end commentary forecast further gains for 1990. The central bank's tightening was reported as a measured response to asset inflation. The first months of 1990 brought a decline of about a quarter, which was described as a correction in an otherwise sound market.

## Chapter 10 — 2000

**The diagnostics at the time, and what the frameworks of the day said.** By 1999 the equity market's valuation against income stood at the highest level in the record — a cyclically adjusted multiple in the forties *(Tops and Bottoms, the 2000–02 episode; Shiller's data; last verified October 2026)* — and the excess was concentrated in technology, telecommunications and the newly listed. The long-cycle debt diagnostics were benign in the public sector, which was running surpluses, and deteriorating in the corporate sector, where telecommunications carriers were borrowing heavily to build networks against forecast demand. Breadth had already failed: the average stock peaked in April 1998, nearly two years before the index *(Tops and Bottoms; last verified October 2026)*. The central bank raised its rate from 4.75% to 6.5% between mid-1999 and May 2000, and the curve inverted in early 2000. The dominant framework of the day held that the internet had raised the economy's productivity growth permanently and that earnings-based valuation was obsolete for companies whose value lay in future network effects. A central bank chair had warned of irrational exuberance in December 1996 and then, as the market kept rising, described the productivity case sympathetically.

**Who was forced, and by what.** The forcing at this top was capital exhaustion rather than margin. The marginal buyer — retail investors, momentum funds, the newly enlarged pool of technology-sector funds — was meeting a flood of new supply: initial offerings, secondary sales by insiders, and the expiry of lockups on shares issued months earlier. When the inflow slowed, the supply did not. Telecommunications carriers, which had financed their build-outs with debt, were forced into default as revenue failed to arrive, and their lenders and suppliers with them. The forcing on the equity holder was the slow one of redemption and mandate rather than the fast one of margin, which is why the decline was long rather than sudden.

**How long the top took to form, and what being early cost.** From the December 1996 warning to the March 2000 peak, the broad index roughly doubled and the technology-heavy index nearly quadrupled *(S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite, December 1996 to March 2000; last verified October 2026)*. A reader who acted on the valuation evidence in 1997 was early by three years and underperformed every benchmark in every one of them; several professional managers who did exactly that lost their clients or their firms before the top. A reader who acted on the breadth evidence in 1998 was early by two years. The decline, when it came, took thirty-one months and about half the broad index's value, in repeated legs separated by sharp rallies *(Tops and Bottoms; last verified October 2026)*.

**What resolved it.** The central bank cut its rate from 6.5% to 1% across 2001–03, the state moved from surplus to deficit, and household borrowing against housing replaced corporate borrowing as the economy's credit engine. The long cycle did not deleverage at this top; the debt moved from one sector to another, which is part of why the next top, seven years later, was a debt top.

**What it implies for stance.** That a valuation top without a debt problem resolves by repricing rather than by deleveraging, and that the repricing can take years and come in legs. That being early at a valuation top is the most expensive kind of early, because valuation never times anything. And that a resolution which moves debt rather than reducing it sets up the next top rather than ending the cycle.

**What it looked like on the day.** On 10 March 2000 the technology index closed at its high. The new-issue calendar was full, and the commentary of the day was about the new economy. The analysis that asked how long the newly listed companies' cash would last was published within the following fortnight, after the high, and by then the index had begun to fall.

## Chapter 11 — 2007

**The diagnostics at the time, and what the frameworks of the day said.** By 2006 the private-sector debt diagnostics were the most extreme in the post-war record: household debt to income had risen steeply across a decade, financed through mortgages of declining quality — loans with little documentation, little equity and rates that reset higher after two or three years — and the financing was held not by banks with deposits but by leveraged vehicles funded in short-term markets. Equity valuations were reasonable against earnings; the excess was in credit, not in equity multiples, which is why Tops and Bottoms calls 2007 "pure credit." The policy rate had risen seventeen times from mid-2004; the curve inverted in 2006. The dominant framework of the day held that the dispersion of risk through securitization had made the financial system more resilient, that national house prices had never fallen in the post-war record and therefore would not, and that the central bank could clean up after a bubble rather than prevent one.

**Who was forced, and by what.** Every forcing mechanism in Price, Time, and Edge's Chapter 15 operated at once. Holders of mortgage securities faced margin calls as prices fell. Structured vehicles that funded long-dated mortgage assets with short-dated commercial paper could not roll their funding. Money-market funds that held that paper faced redemptions, and one broke the buck. Dealers that funded inventories in the repurchase market faced rising haircuts. Banks faced the drawdown of credit lines they had extended to the vehicles. The forcing moved from the edge of the system to its core across fifteen months, through every institution whose funding was shorter than its assets.

**How long the top took to form, and what being early cost.** Warnings about the housing market were public from 2005, and a reader who acted on them then was early by two years. Being early at this top cost less than at 2000, because the equity index rose modestly — about a quarter — from late 2005 to its October 2007 peak, and the decline that followed took more than half of its peak value *(S&P 500, late 2005 to March 2009; Tops and Bottoms; last verified October 2026)*. The credit signal gave the clearest lead in the record: high-yield spreads troughed in June 2007 and had widened by more than half off that trough when the equity index made its final high four months later *(Tops and Bottoms; last verified October 2026)*. The reader who watched credit rather than equities had the top's timing within a season; the reader who watched house prices had its cause two years early.

**What resolved it.** A rescue of the financial system at a scale without precedent: capital injected into the banks, guarantees of their debt and of money-market funds, the policy rate at zero by December 2008, asset purchases announced in March 2009, nine days after the equity low, and a public stress test that told the market which banks were solvent *(Tops and Bottoms; The Rate and Liquidity Machine; last verified October 2026)*. Private debt was partly written down and partly transferred to the state, and the state's debt ratio roughly doubled across the following decade. This was the frame's beautiful deleveraging attempted in real time — the deflationary forces of default met with monetization and transfers — and it largely succeeded in avoiding a depression while moving the long cycle's debt from households to the state.

**What it implies for stance.** That when the excess is in credit, the credit market gives the timing and the equity market does not. That a deflationary deleveraging met quickly with monetization is shorter than one met slowly, which is the contrast with Japan. And that the resolution's transfer of debt to the state is what locates the next stage of the long cycle in the public balance sheet.

**What it looked like on the day.** On 9 October 2007 the equity index closed at its high, two months after the interbank market had seized and the central bank had cut its discount rate. The commentary credited the central bank with containing the problem. The funding markets did not agree, and their spreads had not returned to where they were before August.

## Chapter 12 — Now, as diagnostics

**This chapter is a method applied to the present, not a call about it.** It carries the same six things as the four before it. The readings are in Appendix B, dated; what follows is which readings matter, how each earlier instance's diagnostic maps onto the present, and what a reader would watch.

**The diagnostics, and what the frameworks of the day say.** The four earlier tops each had their excess in a particular sector: brokers' loans in 1929, bank lending against land in 1989, equity valuations and telecommunications debt in 2000, household mortgage credit through shadow banks in 2007. The first diagnostic question of the present is therefore *where the excess is*, and the twelve questions of Chapter 5 are the instrument. The candidate sectors, each with its own diagnostic, are the state's own balance sheet (questions 1 to 4 and 8), the valuation and concentration of the equity market (questions 6 and 12), the financing of the investment boom the themes report tracks as Factor I (question 5, in Minsky's terms), and the private-credit funds that have replaced banks as lenders to the middle market (question 5, as Credit's bifurcation thesis frames it). The frameworks of the day divide, as they did at each earlier top, into one that holds the old constraints no longer bind — a reserve currency's debt has no ceiling, a productivity boom justifies the valuations — and one that holds they do. The reader's job is not to pick a framework. It is to read which of the twelve answers each framework's case depends on, and to watch those.

**Who would be forced, and by what.** At each earlier top, the forced participants were the holders whose funding was shorter than their assets. The present version of the question is which classes carry that mismatch now: leveraged holders of government bonds funded in repurchase markets; funds that offer periodic liquidity against illiquid loans; vehicles whose collateral is the asset they finance; and the captive holders a repression regime would require. Price, Time, and Edge's Chapter 15 supplies the taxonomy; the present answer is a dated read.

**How long a top takes to form, and what being early costs.** The four instances span the range. In 1929 the diagnostics led by about two years; in 1989 by two to four; in 2000 by three, and the valuation reader underperformed throughout; in 2007 by two, but the credit reader had the timing within a season. The general base rates are those at the head of this Part: breadth leads by months to years, curve inversion by six to eighteen months, credit acceleration by a season, valuation not at all. The implication is that a long-cycle diagnostic read today, if it reads late-cycle, says almost nothing about the next year and a good deal about the distribution of the next decade — and that sizing a position as if it said something about the next year is the mistake each earlier instance punished.

**What would resolve it.** Each earlier top was resolved by the lever its currency and its politics permitted: in 1933 by removing the gold constraint, in Japan by a decade of balance-sheet repair and state borrowing, in 2001 by moving debt to households, in 2008 by moving it to the state. The present question is which levers remain: the state's balance sheet has absorbed the last resolution, so the next one's mix is the long-cycle question of Part I, decided between the deflationary and inflationary branches by the readings Chapter 17.4's proposed rule uses.

**What it implies for stance.** That the long-cycle read sets the distribution and not the timing; that a reader who finds the present late in the cycle expresses it through the composition of protection — which branch's hedges are held — rather than through a net short; and that the Doctrine's ruling that a tail thesis is a hedge budget, not a position, is the stance each earlier instance would have rewarded.

**What it would look like on the day.** If the present were a long-cycle top, the earlier instances say how the day would read: a market at or near its high, a policy authority describing a problem as contained, a dominant framework explaining why old constraints do not apply, and a funding market — whichever one carries the excess — that has stopped agreeing with the equity market. The last of these is the one a reader can watch.

### The five, read together

Read together, the five instances say three things this Part exists to say. **The excess moves.** It sat in a different sector at each top, so the diagnostic is a search across sectors, not a reading of one gauge. **Being early is the normal condition of being right**, and the cost of being early is set by how far the final advance runs, which valuation cannot tell and breadth and credit can only bound. **The resolution is chosen by the currency and the politics**, which is why Part I's two branches and the internal-order lens decide more about a reader's outcome than the top's date does.

# Part IV — The environments

**The eighteen environments are the library's common vocabulary for "the regime."** Every markets paper carries an Environments section that says how its subject behaves in each; this Part owns the master definitions and the cross-cutting matrices, and the other papers' sections are its rows. The environments are not mutually exclusive — a banking crisis happens inside a recession, financial repression inside an inflation — and they are not equally sampled. Several have no modern instance in a developed market, and those say so.

## Chapter 13 — The eighteen defined

The definitions are grouped by the mechanism that produces them, because the mechanism is what carries across instances. For each family the paper gives one worked example with numbers, one historical instance, and what it looked like on the day; each environment then carries its own mechanism and instances.

### 13.1 The cycle environments: expansion, recession, inflation, disinflation, deflation, stagnation

**Expansion** is credit and income growing together with the policy rate at or below neutral — Chapter 1's first phase. *Instances:* most of the post-war record; the long expansions of the 1960s, 1990s and 2010s. **Recession** is the loop running downward: debt service rising faster than income, spending cut to service it, one participant's cut another's lost income. *Instances:* the post-war recessions, NBER-dated. **Inflation** is a sustained rise in the price level beyond what the policy authority targets, produced by demand outrunning capacity, a supply shock, or monetization — and it becomes a regime when expectations follow it. *Instances:* 1966–82; 2021–23. **Disinflation** is inflation falling while remaining positive, usually after a tightening has broken it; it is the regime in which bonds and equities rise together. *Instances:* 1982–2020, the longest regime in the modern record. **Deflation** is a falling price level, which raises the real burden of nominal debt — Fisher's mechanism. *Instances:* 1929–33 in the United States; Japan for much of 1998–2012. **Stagnation** is low growth with low inflation and no policy traction, the regime of Koo's balance-sheet recession and of secular-stagnation arguments: savings exceed the investment demand at any attainable rate. *Instances:* Japan after 1990; the euro area after 2011.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** In a disinflation, inflation falls from 6% to 2% across five years and the ten-year yield falls with it from 8% to 4%. A long bond's price rises substantially as its yield falls, and an equity multiple expands as the discount rate falls. In the inflation that follows, inflation rises from 2% to 6% and the yield from 4% to 8%: the same bond loses about a quarter of its price *(conceptual; rounded; a duration of about seven years)*, and the equity multiple compresses at the same time. The regime, not the asset, decided the sign.

**A historical instance.** The turn from disinflation to inflation in 2021–22: inflation rose from below 2% to above 8% in eighteen months, the policy rate rose by more than four points in a year, and stocks and bonds fell together for the first time since the 1970s *(The Rate and Liquidity Machine, the 2021–2023 era; Tops and Bottoms, the 2022 episode; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it looked like on the day.** Through 2021 each high inflation print was described by the central bank as transitory, and the bond market largely agreed: the ten-year yield stayed below 2% while inflation passed 5%. The regime had changed in the data before it changed in the price.

### 13.2 The market environments: bubble, crash

**Bubble** is a price rising far above any value its income can support, sustained by a narrative that explains why old measures do not apply and by financing that depends on the price continuing to rise — Kindleberger's displacement, boom and euphoria, and Minsky's migration to Ponzi finance. *Instances:* 1929; Japan 1986–89; 1998–2000; housing 2004–06. **Crash** is a fall too fast for the participants who would ordinarily buy to absorb it, produced by forced selling into a withdrawn bid. *Instances:* October 1929; October 1987; March 2020.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** A stock financed half with margin falls 20%. The borrower's equity falls 40%, below the maintenance level, and the broker sells. If a tenth of the float is held on such terms and the book is thin, the forced sale moves the price another 10%, which triggers the next tier of calls *(conceptual; rounded)*. The crash is the loop, not the news.

**A historical instance.** 19 October 1987: the index fell about 20% in a day with no recession and no change in earnings, driven by portfolio-insurance selling into futures that dealers could not absorb *(Volatility, Chapter 16; Tops and Bottoms, the 1987 episode; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it looked like on the day.** The market had fallen for several days on rising rates and a trade figure; on the morning of the crash, quotes were stale, the futures traded at a discount the arbitrage could not close, and the specialists' books emptied. The cause was mechanical and the commentary searched for a fundamental one.

### 13.3 The crisis environments: liquidity crisis, banking crisis, fiscal crisis, currency crisis

**Liquidity crisis** is a sudden rise in the demand for cash and the safest collateral, in which even sound assets cannot be sold at a fair price because every holder is selling to raise cash. *Instances:* 1907; September 2008; March 2020, when even Treasuries could not be sold freely *(The Rate and Liquidity Machine, the March 2020 case; last verified October 2026)*. **Banking crisis** is a run on institutions whose assets are long and illiquid and whose funding is short — depositors, or wholesale lenders — and it becomes systemic when the run spreads to institutions that were sound. *Instances:* 1930–33; 2008; March 2023. **Fiscal crisis** is the state's debt market refusing to absorb its issuance at a yield the state can bear, usually set off by an auction, a budget or a rating action. *Instances:* Greece 2010–12; the United Kingdom's gilt market in September 2022, where a budget and leveraged pension hedges produced a spiral the central bank stopped by buying *(The Rate and Liquidity Machine, the September 2022 case; last verified October 2026)*. **Currency crisis** is a sudden fall in a currency when holders flee faster than the central bank's reserves or rates can defend it. *Instances:* sterling in 1992; Asia in 1997–98; Argentina and Turkey repeatedly.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** A pension scheme hedges its liabilities with leveraged long-bond swaps, posting collateral of a fifth of the exposure. Long yields rise one percentage point in three days. The swaps lose roughly a fifth of their notional value *(conceptual; rounded; a duration of about twenty years)*, the scheme must post collateral equal to its whole buffer, and it sells long bonds to raise it, which raises yields further. The fiscal crisis is a liquidity crisis in the state's own market.

**A historical instance.** Sterling on 16 September 1992: the Bank of England raised its rate twice in a day and spent reserves defending the currency's band in the European exchange-rate mechanism, and withdrew from the mechanism that evening. The currency fell sharply in the following weeks, and the economy recovered from the release.

**What it looked like on the day.** In each crisis family the day reads the same: an authority states that the position is sound, the market tests the statement at the point where the authority's resources are finite, and the statement is withdrawn within hours or days.

### 13.4 The structural environments: war, technological revolution, financial repression, capital controls, monetary-system change, structural break

**War** is the state becoming the marginal buyer, seller and rule-maker at once: production directed, prices controlled, debt issued at capped yields, capital controlled. *Instances:* 1914–18; 1939–45; for most modern developed markets, none since, so n≈0. **Technological revolution** is a general-purpose technology drawing capital ahead of the income it will produce — railways, electricity, the internet — with a bubble in the financing and a lasting rise in productivity that arrives later than the prices assumed. *Instances:* the 1840s railways; the 1920s; the late 1990s. **Financial repression** and **capital controls** are Chapter 3's regimes. *Instances:* 1942–51 and 1945–1970s; emerging markets since. **Monetary-system change** is the replacement of the rule that defines money: the gold standard's suspension in 1914 and abandonment in the 1930s, the end of convertibility in 1971, the euro's introduction. *Instances:* a handful in a century; in the present reserve system, n≈0. **Structural break** is a relationship that held across regimes ceasing to hold, not because the regime changed but because the mechanism behind it did. *Instances:* gold against real yields and the stock–bond correlation in 2022, treated in Chapter 19.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** Under repression with capital controls, a domestic saver holds a deposit at 1% while inflation runs at 6%, and is prevented from buying foreign assets. Across five years their deposit loses about a fifth of its purchasing power *(conceptual; rounded)*, and the domestic equity market rises in nominal terms because savings have nowhere else to go. A foreign holder who can convert at the official rate sees the same equity market fall in their own currency. The regime decided which price was real.

**A historical instance.** August 1971: the closing of the gold window ended dollar convertibility, and within two years the fixed exchange-rate system had given way to floating rates. Gold, fixed for a generation, rose many-fold across the decade that followed *(Metals; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it looked like on the day.** The announcement came on a Sunday evening with a wage-and-price freeze and an import surcharge. Markets rose the next day on the price freeze. The monetary system had changed, and the first day's reaction read it as a domestic policy package.

## Chapter 14 — Environments and the asset classes

**What each asset did is mechanism first and measurement second, and the measurement is thin.** For the cycle environments the modern record supplies enough instances to say direction with confidence and magnitude roughly; for the crisis environments it supplies a handful; for war, monetary-system change and capital controls in a developed market it supplies almost none. The paper therefore gives direction for every cell, magnitude only where a cited paper has measured it, and the number of instances behind each row.

**Equities** rise in expansion and disinflation, fall in recession — recession bears average roughly −40% over eighteen months and non-recession bears roughly −29% over six *(Tops and Bottoms, eight bears 1970–2022; last verified October 2026)* — and fall hardest in deflation and banking crises, the deepest families in the extended record *(Base Rates, Chapter 12; last verified October 2026)*. In inflation they fall in real terms and are dispersed by pricing power. In repression they hold nominal value and lose real value unevenly. In war and capital controls the domestic index is a price inside a closed room. Across the extended international record, the median bad market falls about 30% and recovers in about two years, the 95th percentile falls about 80% and takes twenty to thirty-five years, and the 99th is a total loss for the original holder *(Base Rates, Chapter 13.3; last verified October 2026)*.

**Long government bonds** of a solvent sovereign rise in recession, deflation, liquidity crises (after the first scramble) and banking crises, and they are the protection of the deflationary branch. They fall in inflation, and they are the worst asset of repression, fiscal crisis and monetary-system change, losing a third or more of purchasing power in the 1940s instance *(Base Rates, Chapter 11.4; last verified October 2026)*.

**Credit** tracks equities with a lead in the credit-driven environments and widens most in banking and liquidity crises; in inflation it is hurt by the rate and helped by the nominal growth that eases default.

**Gold** protects in inflation, repression, currency crises and monetary-system change, and in deflation only after the authorities revalue it; it falls in disinflation with rising real yields, and in the first stage of a liquidity crisis, when it is sold to raise cash.

**Commodities** rise in inflation, expansion and war, and fall in recession and deflation.

**The dollar**, as the reserve currency, rises in recessions, liquidity crises and banking crises abroad, because dollar debt must be serviced in dollars, and weakens in its own repression and fiscal stress, against real assets if not against other currencies (Currencies owns the detail). **Cash** protects in deflation and every crisis and loses steadily in inflation and repression.

## Chapter 15 — Environments and the library's signals

**A signal is a relationship, and each relationship was measured in a regime.** This chapter takes the seven the library leans on hardest and says, for each, the environments in which it holds, weakens or inverts.

**Credit leads equities.** It holds in credit-driven tops and recessions — the 2007 top, with a four-month lead — and in banking crises; it is correctly silent at mechanical and exogenous tops *(Tops and Bottoms, the synthesis chapter; last verified October 2026)*. It weakens in inflation-driven bears, where the equity fall is a discount-rate event and credit stays tight; and it fails under repression, where credit spreads are suppressed by the same policy that suppresses yields.

**Curve inversion precedes recession.** It holds in the short-cycle environments, with a lead of six to eighteen months *(Tops and Bottoms; last verified October 2026)*; it weakens when the term premium is distorted — by asset purchases, by yield-curve control, by a supply shock in long bonds — because the curve's slope then measures policy rather than expectations. Under yield-curve control it carries no information at all.

**Gold against real yields.** It held in the disinflation regime and broke in 2022, when the official-sector bid became the marginal buyer *(Metals, Chapter 5; last verified October 2026)*. In repression and monetary-system change the relationship is replaced by gold against the credibility of the currency, which is a different variable.

**The stock–bond correlation.** Negative in disinflation, when bad growth news lowers yields and equity prices together and the bond hedges the stock; positive in inflation, when the discount rate drives both. It inverted in 2022 and was positive across the inflation regime of 1966–82 *(Base Rates, Chapter 5; last verified October 2026)*. Every balanced-portfolio rule calibrated after 1998 is a disinflation rule.

**The variance risk premium.** Positive in most environments — insurance is sold at a premium — and briefly and violently negative in crashes and liquidity crises, when realized volatility outruns implied. Volatility's argument that the premium, crowding and unwinds are one fact governs it; the environment decides when the fact pays out.

**Momentum.** Holds in expansion, inflation regimes and trends of any sign; fails in sharp reversals — the turn from a crash to a recovery, the first months of a new regime — when the losers of the old regime lead the new one.

**The yen and volatility.** The yen strengthens when volatility rises, because yen-funded carry is unwound; the link holds when the carry is large and the yen's rate differential wide, and weakens when the differential narrows or when Japan's own policy is the shock *(Volatility, Chapter 21; last verified October 2026)*.

## Chapter 16 — Environments and the participants

**Each environment has a participant who becomes marginal and a participant who is forced.** Price, Time, and Edge's Chapter 26 gives the general form for the twelve edges; this chapter names the forced participant per family, because the forced participant is the one a reader is trading against.

**In the cycle environments** the forced participants are calendar and mandate holders: pension and insurance rebalancing, benchmark-relative managers, and in recession the redeeming fund and the de-risking allocator. **In inflation** the rate-sensitive holder — the long-duration fund, the liability-matched pension — repositions, and the real-asset buyer becomes marginal. **Under repression** the captive holder is the marginal buyer of the state's debt, buying because a rule requires it, and the free holder's edge is to decline the captive asset. **In a liquidity crisis** the leveraged holder is forced to sell whatever can be sold, which is the best assets first, and the only participant with an edge is one with unencumbered cash. **In a banking crisis** the depositor, who is not normally a market participant, becomes marginal, and the equity price of a bank becomes an input to the run on it. **In a fiscal crisis** the leveraged holder of the state's debt and the rating-linked mandate holder are forced together. **In a currency crisis** the sovereign and its central bank are forced, and the holders who can leave do. **In war and under capital controls** the state is marginal, and whoever is permitted to transact sets the price.

# Part V — Identification and transition

## Chapter 17 — Identification as a skill

**Identifying a regime and identifying its end are one skill, and it is a skill of reading slow evidence without being moved by fast evidence.** A regime is identified by a set of readings that agree, across lenses, at the cadence of the slowest of them; its end is identified when that agreement breaks. Both are hard for the same reason: the fast evidence — the day's price, the week's flow — arrives constantly and argues, and the slow evidence arrives rarely and is easy to discount.

### 17.1 The dials

The Doctrine operationalizes the regime as three dials rather than one composite, because the things they measure move on different clocks: the Macro dial monthly, governing Book A's band; the Volatility dial weekly to daily, governing size and instrument in every book; the Gamma dial daily, governing Book C's setups and Book B's timing (the Doctrine, Part V, 5.1). Volatility's two-regime frame is the source of the Volatility dial's states. This paper's contribution is the slowest layer beneath the Macro dial: the long-cycle location of Parts I and II, which changes on a clock of years and decides which of the Macro dial's states are even available. In a repression regime the Macro dial can read Expansion, and the expansion is happening inside a regime in which long bonds lose real value; the dial is right and the stance it implies for duration is wrong unless the long-cycle layer is read with it.

### 17.2 Leading and confirming

Each environment has indicators that lead its arrival and indicators that confirm it. Leading indicators are early and noisy; confirming indicators are reliable and late. For the short cycle, the lending-standards survey, the credit impulse and the curve lead, and unemployment and earnings confirm. For the inflation regime, breakevens and wage settlements lead, and the core price indices confirm. For the long cycle, the financing-quality and holder questions lead, and the debt-service ratio confirms. A reader who acts only on confirming indicators is late by construction and safe; a reader who acts on a single leading indicator is early and often wrong. The skill is to act in proportion: to reduce exposure to the old regime on leading evidence and to commit to the new one on confirming evidence — which is the Doctrine's transition rule, derived here rather than asserted.

### 17.3 The base rates of transitions, and the cost of early against late

Transitions are rarer than they feel. Tops and Bottoms counts eight bear markets since 1970 against six near-misses that looked like the start of one and were not *(Tops and Bottoms, 1970–2024; last verified October 2026)*: a reader who treated every correction of that size as a regime change would have been wrong nearly half the time. The market-state object's calibration tranche measures the same thing for the dials themselves — how often each dial state persists and how often it turns, with its sample size stated — and it prints "awaiting" until a row has enough independent starts to say anything. The base rates say the cost of being early and the cost of being late are not symmetric: being late into a crisis costs the depth of the first leg, and being early costs the remainder of the final advance, which Part III showed can be a doubling. The Doctrine's answer — reduce quickly to the lower half of the departing band, re-engage slowly — is the answer that bounds both costs rather than minimizing either.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** A Book A band of 60–80% equity sits at 75% when the Macro dial turns from Expansion to Transition. The rule moves the book to the lower half of the departing band, about 65%, hedged, rather than to the 20–40% band of a Contraction. If the transition is false and the market rises 10%, the book has given up about one point of return against staying at 75% *(conceptual; rounded)*. If the transition is real and the market falls 30%, the book has saved about three points against staying, and stays positioned to move to the new band on confirming evidence. The rule buys a small, bounded insurance against a large, unbounded error.

**A historical instance.** The autumn of 1998: a hedge-fund failure, a currency default and a sharp equity fall produced every leading sign of a crisis, and the central bank cut its rate three times. The market recovered to new highs within months, and the next real transition came eighteen months later *(Tops and Bottoms, the near-misses; last verified October 2026)*.

**What it looked like on the day.** On the worst day of a false transition the leading indicators all read the same as on the first day of a real one. What differs is the confirming set, which on the false day has not moved: credit is wider but not accelerating, earnings revisions are steady, and the policy authority is still able to act.

### 17.4 The two branches, told apart early

This section is written for the market-state object's `debt_cycle_branch` field, which prints `state: none` until a rule decides between the two branches of a long-cycle resolution and which the object's specification left to this paper. **What follows is a proposal for the operator's ruling, not a rule, and it grants nothing.** The field is report-only for every reader and stays so whatever is ruled; the sizing consequence of the branch — that Book A's duration sleeve is a deflation hedge and the wrong instrument for the inflationary branch — is the Doctrine's to write.

**The observables.** The object's specification names seven inputs, read together as one mechanism: federal debt to output and its trajectory, the structural deficit, net interest as a share of revenue, the foreign official share of the debt's absorption, the term premium's behaviour when growth expectations fall, real yields against inflation, and gold against real yields. Four of them are stored and printed as the field's observables block: the trailing twelve-month deficit as a share of output (`calc.deficit_ttm_pct_gdp`), the ten-year real yield (`fred.tips_10y`), the ten-year breakeven (`fred.breakeven_10y`) and the front gold future (`yfinance.mkt_gold_front`). The term premium is barred from the object by the rates-attribution ruling and can inform the reader but not the field. The real yield measured against trailing inflation (`calc.r_real_10y_cpi`) extends the real-yield arm before the inflation-linked bond existed.

**What each branch does to them.** The two branches share their antecedents — a high and rising debt-service burden, a structural deficit — and differ in the direction of three prices. In the deflationary branch, inflation and breakevens fall, real yields rise because inflation falls faster than nominal yields, the reserve currency strengthens, and gold does not rise against real yields until the authorities act. In the inflationary branch, breakevens rise, real yields are negative and stay negative because nominal yields are held or lag, the currency weakens against real assets, and gold rises against real yields — the divergence Metals calls the doubt about the numéraire.

| Observable | Shared antecedent | Deflationary branch | Inflationary branch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deficit and interest burden | High, rising | Rising, as revenue falls | Rising, then eased by nominal growth |
| Breakeven inflation | — | Falling | Rising |
| Real yield | — | Rising | Negative and persistent |
| Gold against real yields | — | No divergence until policy acts | Rising against flat or rising real yields |
| Reserve currency | — | Strengthening | Weakening against real assets |

**The proposed rule.** Stated as a proposal, with every threshold session-set and open to the ruling:

1. **Precondition.** The field reads `none` unless the long-cycle stress is present: net interest to revenue (`calc.interest_to_receipts`) above its own five-year 80th percentile, *or* the trailing deficit above its five-year median while the economy is not in recession. Without the precondition, a fall in breakevens is a short-cycle recession and not a long-cycle branch.
2. **The three tells, each read on a thirteen-week window.** *Inflation direction:* the breakeven's thirteen-week change. *Real-yield direction:* the real yield's thirteen-week change and its sign. *Gold against real yields:* gold's thirteen-week return when the real yield's change is zero or positive.
3. **Deflationary.** The breakeven falling, the real yield rising, and gold not diverging: all three.
4. **Inflationary.** The breakeven rising or the real yield below zero, *and* gold rising while the real yield is flat or rising: the divergence is required, because negative real yields alone are also the ordinary state of an easing cycle.
5. **Persistence.** A branch prints only after its condition holds at two consecutive month-end reads, as the object's dimensions require two sessions. A branch that has printed reverts to `none` after two consecutive month-ends without its condition.
6. **Confidence.** The count of tells in agreement, of three, printed beside the state; the field never prints a probability.

**The rule's assumptions.** That the branch is visible in prices before it is visible in the debt figures, which are annual and revised. That the reserve issuer's own breakeven and real yield are informative about its branch, which requires the inflation-linked market not to be distorted by the central bank's purchases. That gold's divergence from real yields is about the currency's credibility and not about a single buyer's demand — the assumption the 2022 break makes most fragile. And that thirteen weeks is long enough to remove the short cycle's noise and short enough to be early.

**The rule's falsifiers.** It is falsified if, read back over the history the store holds, it prints a branch that the following two years contradict — inflationary before a deflationary episode, or the reverse. It is falsified as noise if it changes state more than twice in a year. It is falsified in its gold arm if gold diverges from real yields for a reason a reserve-manager survey or the official-sector purchase data attributes to a single class of buyer. And it fails its own precondition if a branch's price signature appears without the stress — which would mean the signature is a short-cycle phenomenon and the rule has nothing to say about the long cycle. The history it can be scored on is short: the inflation-linked market's record begins in 1997, the real-yield proxy extends it, and the full long-cycle instances of Part III predate both.

**A worked example, with illustrative numbers.** Net interest has risen to the 90th percentile of its five-year range; the precondition holds. Over thirteen weeks the breakeven rises from 2.3% to 2.6%, the real yield falls from 1.5% to 1.2%, and gold rises 12% while the real yield is roughly unchanged across the last six of those weeks. Two of three tells agree on the inflationary branch at the first month-end and three of three at the second; the field prints `inflationary`, confidence three of three. A month later the breakeven falls back to 2.4% and gold stalls; the condition fails at one month-end and the field holds, because reversion also needs two *(conceptual; rounded)*.

**A historical instance.** The year 2008 is the instance the rule must get right on the deflationary side: breakevens collapsed in the autumn, real yields on inflation-linked bonds rose sharply as the market sold them for cash, the dollar strengthened, and gold fell before it rose. Every tell read deflationary — and the authorities' response turned the episode within months, which is the case for the persistence rule's two reads and for the field's being report-only.

**What it looked like on the day.** In the autumn of 2008 the ten-year breakeven fell toward zero while the real yield rose toward 3%, a move the commentary attributed to forced selling of inflation-linked bonds rather than to a change in expectations. Both readings were right: the price signature was deflationary and partly mechanical. A rule that read the day would have printed the branch; a rule that reads two month-ends would have printed it later and held it through the turn, which is the trade-off the persistence term makes.

## Chapter 18 — How regimes end

**Regimes end in sequences, and the sequences rhyme by mechanism rather than by date.** Tops and Bottoms owns the equity-market sequences; this chapter reads six transitions as regime changes, one paragraph each, and then the false transitions that looked the same.

**1972–74.** A disinflationary expansion with a two-tier equity market became an inflation regime: the dollar's link to gold ended in 1971, an oil embargo quadrupled the price of crude in 1973, the central bank tightened deep into the recession because inflation would not break, and stocks and bonds fell together. The breadth of the market had peaked about a year before the index *(Tops and Bottoms, the 1973–74 episode; last verified October 2026)*. The regime that ended was the post-war one of fixed exchange rates and low inflation.

**1980–82.** An inflation regime ended by a policy authority willing to raise real rates far above zero and hold them there through two recessions. The equity market was cheap at the top; the bear was made by policy. The transition that followed — disinflation — lasted four decades, and nearly every relationship the library teaches was measured inside it.

**2000.** A valuation regime ended by capital exhaustion and a tightening; the regime of rising multiples gave way to a long repricing, and the long cycle's debt moved from corporations to households.

**2007–09.** A credit regime ended by a run on the shadow banks; the transition was to zero rates, asset purchases and the state as the borrower of last resort — the regime of financial repression in its mild, post-crisis form.

**2020.** The fastest transition on record: from a calm, low-volatility expansion to a crash and a liquidity crisis in a month, and back to an expansion inside two months more, because the policy response was larger and faster than any before *(Volatility, Chapter 20; last verified October 2026)*. The regime that ended was not the long-cycle one; it was resumed, with more debt.

**2022.** The disinflation regime ended. Inflation rose above 8%, the policy rate rose faster than in forty years, the stock–bond correlation turned positive, and gold broke from real yields — a regime change and two structural breaks in a single year *(The Rate and Liquidity Machine, the 2021–2023 era; last verified October 2026)*.

**The false transitions.** 1994 (a bond-market rout without an equity bear), 1998 (a hedge-fund failure and a default), 2011 (a sovereign-debt scare and a downgrade), 2015–16 (a currency devaluation and a commodity collapse), 2018 (a volatility-product failure and a tightening) and 2024 (a carry unwind) each produced the leading signs of a regime change and none produced one *(Tops and Bottoms, the near-misses; last verified October 2026)*. What separated them from the real transitions was the confirming set: credit did not accelerate, earnings were not cut, and the policy authority could and did respond.

| Transition | Old regime | Ended by | New regime | Lead that worked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1972–74 | Fixed rates; low inflation | Gold link cut; oil; policy behind | Inflation | Breadth, about a year |
| 1980–82 | Inflation | Real rates far above zero | Disinflation (four decades) | Policy itself |
| 2000 | Rising multiples | Capital exhaustion; tightening | Repricing | Breadth, about two years |
| 2007–09 | Credit expansion | Run on shadow banks | Zero rates; mild repression | Credit, a season |
| 2020 | Calm expansion | An exogenous shock | Resumed, with more debt | None (untimeable) |
| 2022 | Disinflation | Inflation; fastest tightening in forty years | Inflation regime; two breaks | Breakevens and wages |

## Chapter 19 — Structural breaks

**A structural break is a relationship that stops holding because its mechanism changed, and it is told from a regime change by asking whether the mechanism still operates.** A regime change alters the conditions under which a mechanism produces its relationship; the relationship returns when the conditions do. A break alters the mechanism; the relationship does not return. Metals' chapter on the broken gold model is the template, and this chapter applies it to four relationships.

**Gold against real yields, 2022.** For most of two decades gold's price moved inversely with the ten-year real yield, because the marginal holder was a Western investor for whom gold's opportunity cost was the real yield. In 2022 real yields rose sharply and gold did not fall as the model required; the marginal buyer had become the official sector, buying for reasons the real yield does not enter *(Metals, Chapter 5; last verified October 2026)*. The mechanism test reads it as a break: the old mechanism still operates for the old buyer, and the old buyer is no longer marginal.

**The stock–bond correlation, 2022.** Negative for two decades, because growth shocks dominated and inflation was anchored; positive in 2022, because inflation shocks dominated. The mechanism test reads it as a regime change rather than a break: the mechanism — which shock dominates — is unchanged, and the correlation is expected to return if the disinflation regime does. The consequence for a portfolio is the same in the meantime, which is why the distinction matters only for what to expect next.

**The curve's lead time.** The curve inverted in 2022 and the recession it historically predicted did not arrive on the usual schedule. Two readings compete. A regime reading says the lead lengthens when household and corporate balance sheets are strong and debt was termed out at low rates. A break reading says asset purchases and the supply of long bonds have changed what the curve's slope measures. The test is whether the term premium, separately measured, explains the inversion; if it does, the curve measured policy and supply, and its lead is broken for as long as the distortion lasts.

**The Phillips curve.** The relationship between unemployment and inflation flattened across the disinflation regime, so that low unemployment produced little inflation. Whether the flattening was a break — globalization, anchored expectations, a changed labor market — or a regime property that would steepen again when expectations un-anchored, the 2021–23 inflation partly answered: the curve steepened, which is evidence for the regime reading.

**How to know a break from a regime.** Three questions, in order. Is there a named mechanism for the old relationship, and is it still operating for the participant it described? Has the marginal participant changed? And does the new relationship have a mechanism of its own? A relationship that fails the first question was a sample property and never a mechanism. One that passes the first and fails the second is a break. One that passes both and changes sign is a regime change. The answers decide whether a reader retires a tool or sets it aside.

| Relationship | 2022 behaviour | Reading | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold against real yields | Decoupled | Break | The marginal buyer changed |
| Stock–bond correlation | Turned positive | Regime change | The dominant shock changed; the mechanism did not |
| Curve's lead time | Lengthened | Contested | Depends on the term premium's share of the inversion |
| Phillips curve | Steepened | Regime property | Expectations un-anchored and it returned |

## Chapter 21 — Tactical applications, by horizon

This paper teaches location rather than an asset, so what is "tradeable" in it is the stance a location implies: which book's band is available, which branch's protection is held, and which measured relationships are suspended. What follows lays that against the seven rungs of Price, Time, and Edge's Chapter 6, in prose with the same nine fields for each rung, and then as a cheat sheet. Blank rungs are permitted and are used.

*Nothing in this chapter is investment advice. It describes how one operator, running one book under one set of rules, uses a regime read.*

**Intraday.** Nothing is tradeable at this rung from this paper; the regime read is too slow to bear on a session. The edge type is none. The counterparties at this rung — market makers and fast liquidity providers, transacting for the spread — are indifferent to the long cycle and right to be. Competition has nothing to remove, because no edge is claimed. Nothing would end the rung's blankness. There is no expression and no book, and the only report section is the close's Mechanics, read for whether a crisis environment's liquidity has withdrawn — in which case Chapter 16 says not to transact.

**Days.** What is tradeable is the reaction to an event read through the regime: a release whose surprise means different things in different environments, such as an inflation print in an inflation regime against one in a disinflation regime. The edge type is regime recognition. The counterparties are participants reacting to the sign of the news rather than to its meaning in the regime, transacting because their models were calibrated in another regime. Competition has not removed it because the regime-conditioning is slow knowledge that fast participants do not carry. It would end if the market priced the regime-conditional reaction reliably. The expression is defined-risk options sized to the packet's risk; the book is C; it is read in the close's The read and Plumbing & rates.

**Weeks.** What is tradeable is the persistence of a regime once it is identified, and the transition rule's reduction when one turns. The edge types are regime recognition and horizon. The counterparties are participants who treat a regime change as a correction and buy it, and benchmark-bound holders who cannot change exposure for a view of weeks. Competition has not removed it because acting on a slow read at a weekly cadence is contractually awkward for most capital. It would end if the transition rule's false-alarm cost rose above its insurance value on measurement. The expression is reduced size and the lower half of a band, and in Book B, trend trusted over mean reversion in a tightening; the book is B, with A's band; it is read in the Weekly's The read and What's priced.

**Months.** What is tradeable is the Macro dial's band, read with the long-cycle layer beneath it: which band is available, and whether the duration sleeve is the right instrument for the branch. The edge types are regime recognition and cross-asset. The counterparties are mandated holders who must hold duration regardless of the branch — the captive holder of Chapter 3 — and allocators whose rules were calibrated in disinflation. Competition has not removed it because the counterparties are prevented, not outbid. It would end at a structural break that invalidated the dials themselves. The expression is index and Treasury exposure inside the band, with the protection's composition chosen by the branch; the book is A; it is read in the Monthly's Plumbing & rates, the slow layers and Pillar 8.

**Cyclical.** What is usable is the long-cycle location of Parts I and II — an input to stance rather than a trade: the branch question, the protection composition, the regularities suspended. The edge type is horizon. There is almost no transacting counterparty; the counterparty is the absence of participants with a five-to-ten-year clock. Competition is excluded by contract rather than outbid. What would end it is the operator discovering they cannot hold a stance across the path. It is expressed only through the months-rung band and the protection mix, it feeds Book A as an input, and it is read in the Monthly's slow layers, Factor IV and the quarterly review.

**Secular.** What is usable is the long cycle and the internal-order lens as constraints: which branch the politics leans toward, which relationships are safe to rely on at all. The edge type is horizon and analytical. There is no transacting counterparty. Nothing competitive would end it; the risk is that the read is wrong and unfalsifiable for a decade. The expression is stance and the hedge budget only — a long-cycle thesis is a ring-fenced budget in Book D, renewed, never a net exposure, as the Doctrine rules for tail theses; it is read in the Disruptive Themes composite, the tail watch's long-cycle scenario and the annual structural review.

**Generational.** There is no edge for this book at this rung. The usable content is that the present monetary arrangement is not permanent, which changes what is assumed rather than what is held. There is no counterparty, no expression and no book. It is read once a year, in the annual structural review's adversarial questions.
