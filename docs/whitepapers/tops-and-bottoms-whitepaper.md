## 1. Introduction & Methodology

This paper studies every major U.S. equity market top and bottom since 1970, plus the significant corrections that did not become bear markets. It exists to serve a specific practical purpose: the Top & Bottom Report scores today's market against thresholds calibrated on these episodes, and a user of that report should be able to see the underlying evidence — what each historical turning point actually looked like, which indicators gave warning, which stayed silent, and what happened next.

Structure follows five principles agreed at the outset. First, episode chapters are strictly historical — no comparisons to the present are made inside them, because reading history through today's lens contaminates the evidence. All comparison to the current environment is quarantined in the final chapter. Second, every episode includes an explicit accounting of what signaled and what stayed silent, because a signal's value depends on knowing its failures. Third, the study includes the near-misses — corrections that looked like incipient bears and resolved without one — because a study of confirmed turning points alone has survivorship bias built in. Fourth, aftermath is standardized: price-only forward returns at one, three, five, and ten years from both the top and the bottom, so episodes are comparable rather than each receiving a narrative window chosen to flatter a thesis. Fifth, data gaps are shown, not filled.

### Episode selection

A bear market is defined conventionally: a decline of roughly 20% or more in the S&P 500 on a closing basis. Eight episodes qualify since 1970: 1973–74, 1980–82, 1987, 1990, 2000–02, 2007–09, 2020, and 2022. The 1990 decline reached −19.9% on a closing basis and is included as a bear by near-universal convention and because a recession accompanied it. Six near-misses receive a consolidated chapter: 1994, 1998, 2011, 2015–16, 2018, and 2024 — each a drawdown of roughly 9–20% that resolved without a bear market, and each informative about what separates a correction from a cycle turn.

### Data sources and honest gaps

Metrics are drawn from Robert Shiller's long-run dataset (CAPE, earnings, rates), FRED (policy rates, CPI, Treasury yields, credit spreads, unemployment), S&P index records, NBER recession dating, and CBOE volatility indices. Coverage is uneven by era, and this paper shows the gaps rather than filling them: no VIX exists before 1990 (the VXO series extends to 1986 and is used for 1987); no high-yield OAS series exists before late 1996 (earlier episodes reference Baa–Aaa spreads or qualitative credit conditions); reliable forward-earnings estimate archives begin in the mid-1990s (earlier episodes use trailing P/E and CAPE only); and modern positioning data (COT, fund-manager surveys) largely postdates 1990.

Figures are approximate where marked with a tilde (~). Historical index levels, drawdowns, and recession dates are precise; valuation and macro readings at specific dates are stated to the precision the underlying monthly or weekly source supports, and forward-return figures are rounded price-only approximations that exclude dividends — which flatters no era consistently but understates long-horizon compounding everywhere. Two recent-episode caveats: forward windows extending past May 2026 are marked open, and figures for 2024–26 reflect the market level as of this writing (S&P 500 near 7,500).

### A note on hindsight

Every 'what signaled' section benefits from knowing how the story ended. The discipline applied is to credit only signals that were observable in real time with data that existed then, and to note explicitly where a signal is visible only in retrospect. Even so, the reader should hold the whole exercise with appropriate humility: at every one of these tops, intelligent investors with the same data concluded the market was fine — and at every bottom, that it was going lower.

## 2. 1973–74: The Nifty Fifty Collapse and the Inflation Bear

> Top: January 11, 1973 (S&P 500 120.24) · Bottom: October 3, 1974 (S&P 500 62.28) · Drawdown: −48% over 21 months · Recession: Nov 1973 – Mar 1975 (NBER)

### Context

The market entered 1973 at the end of a two-tier era. The broad market had churned sideways for years while a cohort of roughly fifty large-growth stocks — the "Nifty Fifty" (McDonald's, Disney, Polaroid, Xerox, Avon, IBM) — commanded premium multiples on the belief that quality growth justified any entry price. The cohort's average P/E was roughly 42x against a market P/E near 19x. Institutional money had crowded into "one-decision stocks": buy and never sell.

Macro conditions were deteriorating beneath the surface. The Bretton Woods system had collapsed in stages through 1971–73, the dollar had been devalued twice, and wage-price controls imposed in 1971 were suppressing — not curing — inflation pressure. CPI was running 3.6% at the January top but accelerating. The Fed, under Arthur Burns, had eased aggressively into the 1972 election.

### Underlying Drivers

- Inflation regime change: the 1973 oil embargo (October) quadrupled crude prices, but CPI was already accelerating before the embargo — from 3.4% in mid-1972 to over 8% by late 1973, peaking above 12% in 1974. The embargo accelerated a fire already burning.

- Policy tightening into weakness: the Fed funds rate rose from roughly 5.5% in early 1973 to a peak near 13% in July 1974 — tightening continued deep into the recession because inflation would not break.

- Valuation unwind: the Nifty Fifty premium collapsed. Polaroid fell roughly 90%, Avon 86%, Xerox 71%. The one-decision thesis died with the multiple, not the businesses — many franchises remained excellent; the entry price did not.

- Currency and commodity instability: the end of Bretton Woods removed the monetary anchor; gold and commodities repriced violently.

### The Top — What Signaled

- Breadth divergence (long lead): the NYSE advance-decline line peaked in early 1972 — roughly a year before the index top — as the average stock rolled over while the Nifty Fifty carried the cap-weighted index higher. This remains one of the cleanest breadth-divergence tops on record.

- Two-tier valuation extreme: the gap between the favored cohort (~42x) and the median stock (~12–13x) was itself the signal — concentration of enthusiasm, not broad euphoria.

- Yield curve: inverted by mid-1973, roughly six months after the equity top but well before the worst of the decline.

- Inflation acceleration: CPI momentum turned decisively in 1972 while the market made highs.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- Absolute index valuation was unremarkable: trailing P/E near 19x and CAPE near 18.7 — elevated but nothing like 1929 or 2000. An investor screening only on index-level multiples saw no alarm.

- Credit spreads (Baa–Aaa) were well-behaved at the top; corporate credit stress arrived with the recession, not before it.

- No modern sentiment gauges existed; anecdotal sentiment (institutional "one-decision" orthodoxy) was extreme but unmeasured.

### The Descent

The decline unfolded in three legs across 21 months: a choppy 1973 first leg as inflation and Watergate eroded confidence; the embargo shock into year-end 1973; then the grinding 1974 collapse as earnings fell, multiples compressed, and forced selling from margined institutions fed on itself. There was no single crash day — 1973–74 was attrition. By the October 1974 low the index had halved and the real (inflation-adjusted) loss approached 60%.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- Valuation washout: trailing P/E reached roughly 7x and CAPE roughly 8.3 — the cheapest readings since the 1940s.

- Capitulation breadth: fewer than one in five NYSE issues traded above their 200-day averages at the low.

- Policy inflection: the Fed began easing in late 1974 as unemployment surged; the bottom formed as the tightening cycle ended, months before the recession officially ended in March 1975.

- Sentiment despair: 1974 remains a byword for institutional capitulation — equity mutual funds saw sustained outflows and "the death of equities" narrative began building (culminating in the famous 1979 BusinessWeek cover, five years into what became a generational buying opportunity).

Nominal recovery was deceptively quick — the index regained its 1973 high by 1980. Real recovery was brutal: adjusted for inflation, an investor at the January 1973 top did not break even until well into the 1980s. The episode's enduring lessons: inflation regimes destroy multiples across the board regardless of business quality; and concentration of institutional conviction in a "can't-lose" cohort is itself a late-cycle marker. The 1974 low, made in maximum despair at single-digit multiples, seeded the greatest structural bull market in history — but six years of sideways chop and a second inflation spike stood between the low and the 1982 launch.

## 3. 1980–82: The Volcker Bear

> Top: November 28, 1980 (S&P 500 140.52) · Bottom: August 12, 1982 (S&P 500 102.42) · Drawdown: −27% over 21 months · Recession: Jul 1981 – Nov 1982 (NBER); double-dip after Jan–Jul 1980

### Context

This is the anomaly among modern tops: the market peaked cheap. CAPE stood near 9.5 and trailing P/E near 9x at the November 1980 high — valuations most eras would call generational buying levels. The bear that followed was made entirely by policy: Paul Volcker's Federal Reserve had resolved to break a decade of entrenched inflation regardless of the real-economy cost.

Inflation had peaked at 14.8% in March 1980. A brief credit-controls recession in early 1980 had given way to a rebound, and the market rallied to its November high on post-election optimism. But the funds rate was heading back toward 20% — the second, deliberate leg of the Volcker squeeze was beginning.

### Underlying Drivers

- Monetary policy as wrecking ball: the funds rate reached 20% in mid-1981; the prime rate hit 21.5%. Volcker targeted money supply and accepted whatever rate resulted.

- Double-dip recession: the 1981–82 contraction pushed unemployment to 10.8% by late 1982 — the worst since the Depression, and worse than anything until 2020.

- Real-rate shock: with inflation falling from 14.8% toward 6% while nominal rates stayed extreme, real rates reached historic highs — devastating for rate-sensitive sectors, farm credit, thrifts, and emerging-market dollar debtors (the LDC debt crisis broke in August 1982, the same month the market bottomed).

- Valuation was a bystander: multiples compressed modestly from already-low levels; this bear was earnings-and-policy, not multiple unwind.

### The Top — What Signaled

- The curve and policy stance: the funds rate above every point on the Treasury curve was an unambiguous restriction signal; the deep 1980–81 inversions correctly preceded the 1981–82 recession.

- Inflation-policy collision: with the Fed explicitly targeting money supply, the equity top coincided almost exactly with the resumption of tightening — the rare top you could time off the central bank's own stated reaction function.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- Valuation gave no warning whatsoever — the market was cheap at the top and got cheaper. Any framework weighted heavily toward valuation would have scored November 1980 a buying opportunity. This episode is the standing rebuttal to valuation-only market timing, in the bearish direction: cheap can get cheaper when policy is breaking something on purpose.

- Breadth was unremarkable at the top; no meaningful divergence preceded it.

### The Descent

A long grind rather than a crash: 21 months of lower highs as the recession deepened, punctuated by failed rallies whenever markets hoped the Fed would relent. It did not relent until inflation was visibly broken. The August 1982 low arrived at CAPE 6.6 — the cheapest reading of the entire post-war era.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- The policy pivot, almost to the week: Volcker began easing in July–August 1982 as the Mexican default threatened the banking system. The market bottomed on August 12 and rose 58% in the following twelve months — the sharpest launch of the modern era.

- Extreme cheapness meeting falling rates: CAPE 6.6 with disinflation underway and bond yields beginning a two-decade decline — the single best risk/reward alignment in post-war history, visible in real time to anyone watching policy.

- Capitulation sentiment: the 1979 "Death of Equities" thesis was fully consensus by 1982; equity allocations at pensions and households were at generational lows.

August 1982 launched the great bull market: eighteen years, roughly fifteen-fold, interrupted only by 1987 and 1990. The structural drivers all inflected together — inflation broken, rates beginning a secular decline, equity allocations starting from generational lows, and a coming demographic and productivity tailwind. The episode's lessons run opposite to 1973: valuation told you nothing about the top and everything about the magnitude of the opportunity at the bottom; and the single most reliable bottom signal in the whole study is a forced central-bank pivot arriving while assets are cheap.

## 4. 1987: The Crash Without a Recession

> Top: August 25, 1987 (S&P 500 336.77) · Bottom: December 4, 1987 (S&P 500 223.92) · Drawdown: −34% over 3.5 months (−22.6% on October 19 alone) · Recession: None

### Context

The market entered August 1987 up 44% year-to-date, the fifth year of the great bull. The rally had gone vertical while the macro backdrop quietly tightened: the 10-year Treasury yield rose from about 7% in January to nearly 9% by August (and above 10% by mid-October), the dollar was under chronic pressure despite the Louvre Accord, and the Fed under newly-appointed Alan Greenspan raised the discount rate in September.

Beneath the surface sat a structural accident waiting to happen: portfolio insurance. An estimated \$60–90 billion of institutional assets were covered by dynamic-hedging programs that mechanically sold index futures as the market fell — synthetic put replication that worked for any single seller but was destabilizing in aggregate.

### Underlying Drivers

- Rate competition: by October, long Treasuries yielded over 10% against an equity market at roughly 21x trailing earnings — the bond alternative was suddenly compelling, and the equity risk premium had compressed sharply.

- Mechanical selling loop: on October 19, portfolio-insurance programs and index arbitrage interacted with overwhelmed market structure to produce a −22.6% single-day decline — still the largest ever. The selling was largely positioning, not fundamentals.

- Momentum extreme: the +44% year-to-date run had pulled the index roughly 20% above its 200-day average by August — stretched by any historical measure.

- Policy/currency friction: public disagreement between the U.S. and Germany over rates in mid-October undermined the Louvre framework and hit confidence at the worst moment.

### The Top — What Signaled

- Rates, loudly: the bond-market selloff through 1987 was the cleanest macro warning — equity multiples expanding while the discount rate rose 300bp is an unstable combination.

- Momentum/overbought extremes: weekly RSI readings and distance-above-trend metrics were at multi-decade extremes in August.

- Breadth: deteriorated meaningfully between the August peak and mid-October — the average stock was falling for weeks before the crash.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- The economy: GDP growth was solid, earnings were growing double digits, no recession followed. Every recession-oriented indicator (claims, ISM-equivalents, credit) was quiet — correctly, as it turned out.

- Credit spreads: no meaningful widening before the crash; corporate credit barely blinked even during it.

- Valuation was elevated but not historic: CAPE ~18, trailing ~21x — the crash was positioning and rates, not a bubble deflating.

### The Descent

−34% top to bottom, of which two-thirds came in three trading sessions (October 14–19). The Fed's response was the template for every crisis since: an unequivocal liquidity commitment the morning of October 20, discount-window lending, and moral suasion on banks to support dealers. The December retest held above the October intraday low.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- Volatility extreme: the old VXO index printed above 150 on October 19 — a level never approached since. Realized vol at the bottom was the highest ever recorded.

- Policy response: immediate and credible Fed liquidity — the first modern "Fed put."

- Fundamental disconnect: earnings kept growing through Q4 1987 and 1988 while the market traded a third cheaper — the valuation case resolved quickly.

New highs by July 1989 — twenty months from the crash low. No recession, no earnings decline, no credit event. 1987 established the category this study calls the mechanical top: a drawdown produced by positioning and market structure rather than the economic cycle, which resolves quickly once the mechanical selling exhausts and policy provides liquidity. Its descendants are 1998, 2018, and 2024. The durable lesson: when a violent decline is unaccompanied by recession or credit stress, it has been a buying opportunity every single time in this study — and distinguishing mechanical from cyclical in real time is precisely what credit and leading-indicator data are for.

## 5. 1990: The Gulf War Bear and the Credit Crunch

> Top: July 16, 1990 (S&P 500 368.95) · Bottom: October 11, 1990 (S&P 500 295.46) · Drawdown: −20% over 3 months · Recession: Jul 1990 – Mar 1991 (NBER)

### Context

The economy entered mid-1990 late-cycle and fragile. The yield curve had inverted in early 1989 — the classic lead — and the savings-and-loan crisis was in full resolution: hundreds of thrifts failing, the RTC liquidating assets, and surviving banks tightening commercial real estate and business lending into what contemporaries called the "credit crunch." Growth was slowing before any external shock.

The market nonetheless made a new high in mid-July 1990, supported by falling inflation expectations and hopes of Fed easing.

### Underlying Drivers

- Exogenous shock onto a weak base: Iraq invaded Kuwait on August 2. Oil doubled from roughly \$17 to \$36 within weeks — a textbook supply shock hitting an economy already rolling into recession.

- Credit contraction: bank lending standards tightened through 1989–90 (the S&L workout, new capital rules); small-business and CRE credit contracted — a slow-motion credit event without a Lehman moment.

- Consumer shock: confidence collapsed on war risk and gasoline prices; the recession began the very month of the market top.

### The Top — What Signaled

- The 1989 curve inversion: led the July 1990 recession start by roughly 12–14 months — squarely in the historical window.

- Credit conditions: loan-officer tightening and thrift failures were well-telegraphed through 1989–90 for anyone watching bank behavior rather than bond spreads alone.

- Cyclical data: housing and autos had rolled over well before the top.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- The invasion itself: no market indicator anticipates a geopolitical event. The top preceded the invasion by two weeks — coincidence of timing, not foresight; the market was vulnerable, and the shock selected the date.

- Valuation: unremarkable at roughly 16.5x trailing and CAPE ~17 — mid-range. Sentiment measures were not extreme.

### The Descent

Fast and shallow by bear standards: −20% in three months, bottoming on October 11, 1990 — three months before the air war began in January 1991. The market bottomed on maximum uncertainty, not on resolution: by the time the war's outcome was clear, the index was already up double digits.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- Peak fear pricing: oil at \$36, war imminent, recession underway — the bottom formed when the bad news was fully priced, not when it ended. A recurring pattern in this study.

- Fed easing underway: the Fed had begun cutting in July 1990 and continued through 1992 — policy support was in place at the low.

- Washed-out breadth and small-caps: the Russell 2000 and average NYSE stock had been declining since 1989; the median stock's bear market was far older than the index's.

The recovery was immediate in the index and painfully slow in the economy — a "jobless recovery" with unemployment rising into mid-1992, eighteen months after the market low. The 1990s bull grew directly out of this trough. Lessons: an exogenous shock landing on an already-weakening, credit-constrained economy produces a real bear, but a shallow one when valuation is mid-range and policy responds; markets bottom at peak uncertainty rather than at resolution; and the average stock can be deep in its own bear market long before the cap-weighted index tops — the 1989–90 small-cap divergence rhymes with 1972 and 2021.

## 6. 2000–02: The Dot-Com Unwind

> Top: March 24, 2000 (S&P 500 1,527) · Bottom: October 9, 2002 (S&P 500 777) · Drawdown: −49% (NASDAQ −78%) over 31 months · Recession: Mar – Nov 2001 (NBER), mild

### Context

The purest valuation top in the study. By March 2000 the S&P traded at CAPE 43.8 — the highest reading in the metric's 120-year history — and roughly 24.5x forward earnings. The NASDAQ traded near 80x forward. The equity risk premium was approximately zero-to-negative: Treasuries yielded more than the equity market's earnings yield. Capital was funding pre-revenue business models at scale, and the IPO window was the hottest ever recorded — 1999 saw median first-day pops above 60%.

The internet narrative was correct — the technology did transform everything — but the capital cycle overwhelmed it. Telecom carriers alone deployed hundreds of billions in fiber against demand that took a decade to materialize.

### Underlying Drivers

- Capital exhaustion: the marginal buyer ran out. Once burn-rate companies could no longer raise, the reflexive loop reversed — falling prices closed the funding window, which produced failures, which justified falling prices.

- Policy tightening: the Fed hiked from 4.75% to 6.5% between mid-1999 and May 2000; the curve inverted in early 2000.

- Earnings reality: S&P earnings fell roughly 25% peak-to-trough through 2001; tech-sector earnings far more. The multiple compressed onto a shrinking denominator.

- Trust shock: Enron (late 2001) and WorldCom (mid-2002) turned a valuation unwind into a crisis of financial-statement credibility, extending the bear a full year beyond the recession's end.

### The Top — What Signaled

- Valuation, historically: CAPE 43.8 and a ~zero equity risk premium were unprecedented. This is the one top where index-level valuation alone was a sufficient warning.

- Breadth divergence (very long lead): the NYSE advance-decline line peaked in April 1998 — nearly two years before the index top. The average stock spent 1998–2000 in a stealth bear while a narrowing tech cohort carried the index.

- Curve inversion: February 2000, one month before the top.

- Credit: HY spreads had been widening since mid-1998 and stood near 575bp at the equity top — credit never confirmed the 1999–2000 melt-up.

- Speculative-supply signals: record IPO issuance, first-day pops, and insider selling — the "paper flood" that marks distribution.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- Momentum, until after the fact: the index made its high with trend measures fully intact; the 200-day average was not decisively lost until October 2000.

- The economy: the recession did not begin until a year after the market top and was among the mildest on record — the bear was three times deeper than the downturn "justified." Markets can lose half their value on multiple compression alone.

- Volatility: VIX at the top was ~22 — mid-range, not complacent. 2000 topped on churn, not calm.

### The Descent

Thirty-one months, the longest bear in the study, in repeated legs: the spring-2000 NASDAQ break, a failed summer recovery, the 2001 recession leg, a post-9/11 crash and sharp rally, then the final 2002 accounting-scandal leg to the October low. Each rally failed at a lower high; the S&P halved while the NASDAQ lost nearly four-fifths.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- Valuation normalization: CAPE back to ~21.5, forward P/E ~14.5 — not historically cheap (a persistent surprise to bears anchored on 1974/1982), but reasonable against 4% Treasuries.

- Credit extreme: HY spreads above 1,000bp in October 2002 — a genuine default cycle (telecom, energy-merchant) climaxing.

- Capitulation breadth and sentiment: sub-20% of issues above 200-day averages; equity fund outflows persistent through 2002.

- Policy floor: Fed at 1.75% heading to 1.00% — maximum accommodation for that era.

- The double bottom: July and October 2002 lows within 2% of each other — the retest structure common to grinding bears (1974 similar).

The ten-year return from the March 2000 top was negative — the "lost decade" — because the entry multiple consumed a decade of earnings growth and a second crisis (2008) landed inside the window. From the 2002 bottom, returns were solid but historically modest for a bear-market trough, reflecting the merely-normal valuations at the low. Enduring lessons: extreme index valuation is sufficient warning even when everything else looks fine; breadth can lead by years, not months; a bear can be far deeper than its recession; and the technology being real does not protect the capital that overpaid for it — the internet delivered, and its 2000 investors still lost half.

## 7. 2007–09: The Global Financial Crisis

> Top: October 9, 2007 (S&P 500 1,565) · Bottom: March 9, 2009 (S&P 500 677) · Drawdown: −57% over 17 months · Recession: Dec 2007 – Jun 2009 (NBER), severe

### Context

The credit top. Equity valuation at the October 2007 peak was moderate — forward P/E ~15.5x, CAPE ~27 — and the recognized excess sat in housing and structured credit: subprime origination at fraudulent scale, securitization chains (CDOs, CDO-squareds) that dispersed and obscured risk, monoline insurers and SIVs adding hidden leverage, and bank balance sheets levered 30:1 against assets whose values depended on house prices never falling nationally.

The warning sequence was already running at the equity top: subprime lenders began failing in early 2007, two Bear Stearns credit funds collapsed in June, BNP Paribas froze redemptions in August — the interbank funding market was seizing in plain view while the S&P made its high in October, on rate-cut optimism, five days after the Fed's first 50bp cut.

### Underlying Drivers

- Housing bust into a levered system: national home prices peaked in 2006; every basis point of decline propagated through mortgage credit into securities held, levered, at the core of the banking system.

- Funding-run dynamics: the crisis proceeded as a sequence of runs on wholesale funding — SIVs, Bear Stearns (March 2008), the GSEs (September), Lehman (September 15), money-market funds breaking the buck, AIG — each forcing fire sales that transmitted losses system-wide.

- Pro-cyclical deleveraging: mark-to-market losses forced capital raises and asset sales into falling markets — the doom loop 1930s economists would have recognized.

- Policy sequencing: aggressive but reactive until the Lehman failure made the systemic dimension undeniable; TARP, guarantees, and eventually QE arrived after the worst of the cascade.

### The Top — What Signaled

- Credit, with textbook lead: HY spreads troughed near 258bp in June 2007 and stood ~410bp at the October equity top — roughly +59% off the trough while the stock market made new highs. IG and interbank spreads (LIBOR-OIS) told the same story louder. Credit led equities by four months and was screaming by early 2008.

- The 2006 curve inversion: led the December 2007 recession start by roughly 17 months.

- Housing and financials: homebuilders peaked in 2005, bank stocks in February 2007 — the epicenter sectors led the index by eight-plus months.

- Breadth: NYSE advance-decline peaked in June 2007, a modest but real divergence at the October high.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- Index valuation: 15.5x forward earnings gave no warning at all. A valuation-weighted framework scored October 2007 near-neutral — this study's calibration work confirmed the composite missed this top for precisely that reason. The signal lived in credit levels' rate of change, not in equity multiples.

- Reported earnings: financial-sector profits were fictitious in hindsight — stated earnings peaked with the market, so the P/E looked reasonable against a numerator about to be revealed as fantasy.

- Volatility: VIX ~18 at the top — complacent, given what the credit market was already saying.

### The Descent

Seventeen months in two regimes: an orderly first year (−20% by mid-2008) as the recession set in, then the September–November 2008 systemic cascade — Lehman, AIG, money-fund runs, commercial paper freezing — producing the fastest wealth destruction since 1929. A final grinding leg into March 2009 as nationalization fears hit the surviving banks. The −57% total made it the deepest bear since the Depression.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- Valuation extreme (the real kind): forward P/E ~10.3x, CAPE ~13.3 — the cheapest since 1982 — with the equity risk premium at multi-decade highs.

- Credit climax and turn: HY spreads peaked near 1,825bp in December 2008 and were narrowing by March 2009 — credit bottomed before equities, leading at the trough as it had led at the top.

- Policy overwhelming force: QE1's expansion to \$1.25T of MBS purchases was announced March 18, 2009 — nine days after the low. The bottom coincided with the arrival of the policy tool that finally matched the problem's scale. Mark-to-market accounting relief (early April) removed the doom loop's accelerant.

- Total capitulation: under 5% of stocks above 200-day averages at the low; VIX near 50 (off its 80 peak); fund outflows and sentiment surveys at all-time extremes.

## 8. 2020: The Pandemic Crash

> Top: February 19, 2020 (S&P 500 3,386) · Bottom: March 23, 2020 (S&P 500 2,237) · Drawdown: −34% over 33 days (fastest bear ever) · Recession: Feb – Apr 2020 (NBER), shortest ever recorded

### Context

The exogenous top. The market made its high on February 19, 2020 with the cycle late but intact: unemployment at fifty-year lows, credit tight-but-stable, valuation elevated (forward ~19x, CAPE ~31) after a strong 2019 driven entirely by multiple expansion, and volatility compressed (VIX ~14). COVID-19 was already spreading — Wuhan had locked down January 23 — but markets treated it as a China-regional supply story until the last week of February.

No domestic indicator gave warning, because the shock was not cyclical. This episode is included primarily as a study of entry conditions and of the bottom, which rewrote the policy playbook.

### Underlying Drivers

- A deliberate economic stop: governments ordered activity closed. GDP fell at a ~31% annualized rate in Q2 2020 — not a demand cycle but an enforced cessation with no modern precedent.

- A dash-for-cash liquidity spiral: in mid-March even Treasuries and gold sold off as levered basis trades unwound and everything was liquidated for dollars — a market-plumbing failure on top of the economic shock.

- Structural fragility of modern liquidity: dealer balance-sheet constraints (post-Dodd-Frank) meant no shock absorber; the Treasury market, the world's risk-free benchmark, became dysfunctional for roughly a week.

### The Top — What Signaled

- Essentially nothing domestic, in advance. The honest entry in this study: no breadth divergence, no credit warning, no curve signal specific to the event (the 2019 inversion had preceded it, arguably coincidentally). Complacent volatility (VIX 14) and elevated valuation defined vulnerability — the size of the drop — but not timing.

- Foreign markets and commodities moved first by days: copper, oil, and Asian equities broke in late January; the S&P ignored them for three weeks.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- Every traditional cyclical indicator: claims, ISM, credit, earnings revisions — all fine in February 2020, and all correct about the economy that existed until the shutdown order.

### The Descent

Thirty-three days, −34%, including the four largest daily point declines to that date and circuit-breaker halts on four separate days — compression of a full bear market into five weeks because the cause was binary (closed/open) rather than cyclical.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- Policy, to the day: the bottom printed March 23, 2020 — the morning the Fed announced unlimited QE plus corporate-credit facilities (buying IG bonds and even HY ETFs, crossing a Rubicon). The CARES Act (\$2.2T) passed the same week. The recovery began the moment policy scale exceeded shock scale, exactly the 2009 lesson compressed from months to hours.

- Classic capitulation extremes, hyper-compressed: VIX 82.7 (an all-time closing high), ~5% of stocks above 200-day averages, HY spreads ~1,100bp, record fund outflows — the full bottom signature, achieved in a month.

Lessons: exogenous shocks are untimeable, so entry valuation and vol pricing determine damage, not warning; liquidity plumbing is now a crisis vector in its own right (the Treasury-market seizure previewed the funding-stress channel this framework's liquidity overlay now monitors); and the policy floor, once corporate credit purchases were proven, permanently altered how credit prices tail risk.

## 9. 2022: The Inflation and Rates Bear

> Top: January 3, 2022 (S&P 500 4,796) · Bottom: October 12, 2022 (S&P 500 3,577) · Drawdown: −25% (NASDAQ −36%) over 9.5 months · Recession: None

### Context

The market peaked on the first trading day of 2022 at CAPE 39.4 — the second-highest top valuation on record after 2000 — with the funds rate still effectively at zero and QE still running. CPI printed 7.0% for December 2021 the week after the top. The gap between the inflation reality and the policy stance was the entire story: a market priced for permanent accommodation confronting a central bank about to unwind it at the fastest pace since Volcker.

Speculative excess had already peaked internally: the SPAC boom, IPO froth, meme-stock mania, and unprofitable-tech cohort all topped in February 2021, eleven months before the index — a two-tier structure directly echoing 1972 and 1999–2000, with mega-cap quality carrying the index while the speculative tier collapsed underneath.

### Underlying Drivers

- The fastest tightening in four decades: 525bp in sixteen months, plus QT. The entire decline is attributable to the discount rate: multiples compressed from 21.7x to ~15x forward while earnings held roughly flat.

- Duration mathematics: the longest-duration assets fell furthest — unprofitable tech −60/80%, NASDAQ −36%, the equal-weight index meaningfully outperforming cap-weight. Bonds provided no shelter: the 60/40 portfolio had its worst year since the 1930s as stocks and bonds fell together, the signature of an inflation-driven bear.

- No earnings recession, no credit event: S&P earnings were approximately flat in 2022; HY spreads peaked near 583bp — a bear made of multiple compression alone.

### The Top — What Signaled

- Inflation, for a full year: CPI crossed 5% in mid-2021 and 6% by the fall while policy stayed at emergency settings — the collision was visible to anyone who believed the inflation was not transitory. The Fed's own hawkish pivot (November–December 2021) preceded the top by weeks.

- Speculative-tier breakdown: the February 2021 peak in SPACs, IPOs, and meme stocks was an eleven-month leading indicator, the modern rhyme of the 1998 A/D-line peak.

- Valuation: CAPE ~39 and an equity risk premium near 1.4% flagged historic vulnerability, exactly as in 2000.

### The Top — What Stayed Silent

- Credit, entirely: HY spreads were ~315bp at the top — tight — and never signaled. This bear had no credit dimension, which is precisely why spread-based frameworks (including this study's composite) scored January 2022 near-neutral.

- Breadth at the index top: over 70% of S&P constituents were above their 200-day averages in early January — headline breadth was healthy; the divergence lived one tier down, in the speculative cohort, visible only in segmented data.

- Volatility: VIX ~17 — complacent.

- Growth data: the economy was strong at the top; the widely-predicted 2022–23 recession never arrived.

### The Descent

An orderly staircase, unique among the deep declines in this study: nine and a half months of lower highs tracking each upside CPI surprise and each Fed escalation, with no crash day, no credit event, no funding stress — until the September gilt/LDI episode in the U.K. hinted at what forced deleveraging under rising rates could look like. The final leg ended the day before the October 13 CPI report.

### The Bottom — What Marked It

- The inflation inflection itself: October 2022's CPI print (reported October 13) was the last upside shock; the bottom printed the day before it, and the disinflation trend from that month forward carried the recovery. The market bottomed on the second derivative of inflation, not on any traditional capitulation.

- What was absent is the finding: no vol spike (VIX peaked ~33), no credit blowout (HY ~583bp), no Fed pivot (hikes continued into 2023), no recession. Of the classic bottom signature only washed-out breadth (~12% above 200-day) appeared. This study's calibration missed the 2022 bottom for exactly this reason — the macro conditions that caused the bear were still worsening at the low; the market turned on their rate of change.

- Positioning and the retest: the October low undercut the June low, ran stops, and reversed on the CPI morning in a classic failed-breakdown structure — sentiment surveys at that low matched 2009 levels despite the far milder fundamentals.

The 2022 episode's lessons are the freshest and arguably most relevant: a valuation top can resolve through an orderly multiple-compression bear with no credit event and no recession; stocks and bonds fall together when inflation is the driver, removing the traditional hedge; the speculative tier can lead the index top by nearly a year; and bottoms in macro-driven bears form on inflections in the driving variable — not on capitulation mechanics — which is why frameworks built on 2008-style bottom signatures require a separate lens for the 2022 type.

## 10. The Near-Misses: Corrections That Did Not Become Bears

Six times since 1990, the market fell hard enough — or fast enough — to look like an incipient bear, and recovered without one. These episodes matter as much as the bears: they are the false positives any top-calling framework must survive. The pattern across all six is consistent enough to state as a rule: corrections become bears when a recession or a credit event follows; absent both, every drawdown in this set resolved within months. The diagnostic work, in real time, is therefore always the same — is credit deteriorating, and is the economy rolling over?

### 1994: The Bond Massacre

The Fed's surprise February 1994 hike began a 300bp tightening cycle that produced the worst bond-market year in decades, the Orange County bankruptcy, and the Mexican peso crisis — yet the S&P's maximum drawdown was under 9%. Equities churned sideways for a year while the economy absorbed the rate shock without recession, then launched the 1995–99 run. Lesson: rate shocks alone, landing on a strong economy with a young credit cycle, produce time corrections rather than price corrections.

### 1998: LTCM and the Russian Default

A −19.3% decline in ten weeks (July–October) as Russia defaulted and Long-Term Capital Management's leveraged positions unwound toward systemic risk. VIX reached the mid-40s; credit spreads blew out. The Fed brokered a private recapitalization of LTCM and cut rates three times; the market regained its high within two months of the October low and melted up through 1999. Lesson: a genuine financial-stress event, contained by fast policy action while the real economy stays strong, resolves as a V — and the resolution itself (emergency liquidity into a strong economy) can fuel the final, most dangerous leg of a bubble. The 1998 rescue is a direct ancestor of the 1999 blow-off.

### 2011: The Debt-Ceiling and Eurozone Scare

−19.4% from April to October 2011, driven by the U.S. debt-ceiling standoff, the S&P downgrade of Treasury debt (August 5), and existential doubt about the eurozone. VIX reached 48; HY spreads widened past 800bp; the macro data flirted with stall speed. What prevented the bear: no U.S. recession materialized, U.S. credit stress stayed a repricing rather than a default cycle, and the ECB's late-2011 liquidity operations (and 2012 'whatever it takes') removed the tail. The October 2011 low scored as a bottom on this study's calibration — reasonably, since the market delivered a full bottom signature (capitulation breadth, vol spike, credit stress) even though the index decline stopped a fraction short of the bear threshold.

### 2015–16: The China Devaluation and Earnings Recession

Two legs totaling roughly −14%: the August 2015 flash-crash on China's surprise devaluation, and the January–February 2016 leg as oil collapsed to \$26 and an industrial-sector earnings recession bit. ISM manufacturing spent five months below 50; HY spreads reached 887bp — driven by a genuine default cycle in energy credits. This is the study's best example of a sector recession that never went economy-wide: services held, consumer held, and once oil stabilized and China's outflows were stemmed, the market recovered fully by mid-2016. Lesson: even a real credit-default cycle, if confined to one sector, does not produce a bear — breadth of stress matters as much as intensity.

### 2018: The Autopilot Correction

−19.8% from September 20 to December 24, 2018 — the closest near-miss in the study, missing the bear threshold by two-tenths of a percent. Drivers: the Fed hiking into visibly slowing global growth while shrinking its balance sheet on 'autopilot,' compounded by the U.S.–China tariff escalation. Credit widened (HY to ~545bp) but did not break; the economy slowed but did not contract. The resolution was purely monetary: Powell's January 4, 2019 pivot ('patient,' flexible on the balance sheet) ended the decline within days, and 2019 returned over 30%. Lesson: a policy-error correction ends the moment the error is retracted — and the speed of that resolution is itself evidence that no organic recession or credit cycle was underway. This study's calibration used December 24, 2018 as a control; the framework correctly scored it neutral, firing no bottom signal.

### 2024: The Yen-Carry Unwind

The smallest and fastest: roughly −8.5% into August 5, 2024, when a Bank of Japan hike triggered forced unwinding of the yen-funded carry complex. VIX printed 65 intraday — a level previously seen only in 2008 and 2020 — against a single-digit index decline, the starkest positioning-versus-fundamentals divergence on record. Recovery took two weeks. Lesson: modern market structure (levered carry, short-vol strategies, dealer hedging feedback) can manufacture crisis-grade volatility readings with no underlying economic event whatsoever — the 1987 mechanism at higher frequency and smaller scale. Volatility spikes are no longer sufficient evidence of cyclical stress; they must be corroborated by credit and macro data, a design principle embedded directly in this framework's overlay architecture.

### What separates corrections from bears

- Recession: every bear in this study except 1987 was accompanied by one; no near-miss was. The single highest-value question during any drawdown remains: is a recession actually beginning?

- Credit: bears feature broad credit deterioration (2007, 1990, 2000-02) or arrive with valuations so extreme that multiple compression alone suffices (2022). Near-misses feature contained or sector-specific credit stress (2016 energy) or none at all (1994, 2018, 2024).

- Policy direction: near-misses end when policy turns supportive quickly (1998 cuts, 2019 pivot, 2011 ECB). Bears persist because policy either cannot turn (inflation regimes: 1973, 1980, 2022) or turns too slowly against a deleveraging spiral (2007–09).

- Vol spikes and breadth washouts appear in both populations — they mark stress but do not discriminate. This is why the framework treats them as bottom-confirmation inputs rather than standalone signals.

## 11. Synthesis: Patterns Across the Cycles

### Tops are idiosyncratic; bottoms rhyme

The single most important structural finding, confirmed independently by this study's quantitative calibration: the eight tops share no common signature. 1973 was a two-tier valuation top with inflation arriving; 1980 was cheap and made entirely by policy; 1987 was mechanical; 1990 was an exogenous shock onto a credit-constrained economy; 2000 was pure valuation and capital exhaustion; 2007 was pure credit with reasonable equity multiples; 2020 was exogenous; 2022 was a valuation-policy collision with no credit dimension. Any single-lens top framework fails on most of this list — which is the empirical case for running parallel signals (valuation/concentration, credit acceleration, liquidity) rather than one composite.

Bottoms, by contrast, share a recognizable signature in six of eight cases: washed-out breadth (typically under 15–20% of issues above their 200-day averages), a volatility extreme, a credit-spread climax, and a policy inflection — with valuation reset as the fuel for the recovery's magnitude. The exceptions are instructive: 1990 bottomed on peak fear without a vol-index extreme (the data barely existed), and 2022 bottomed with almost none of the signature present — no vol spike, no credit blowout, no pivot — turning instead on the inflection of the macro variable driving the bear. Macro-driven bears bottom on second derivatives; credit-driven bears bottom on policy scale.

### A taxonomy of triggers

- Inflation/policy tops (1973, 1980, 2022): the Fed is forced to tighten into a slowing market. Valuation may be high (1973, 2022) or low (1980) — the driver is the policy bind. Stocks and bonds fall together; the bear ends when the inflation variable inflects.

- Credit tops (2007, and 1990 in part): the excess lives on lenders' balance sheets, not in equity multiples. Equity valuation is silent; credit spreads' rate of change off the trough is the tell. Deepest drawdowns in the study.

- Valuation/capital-exhaustion tops (2000, and 1973's Nifty Fifty tier): extreme multiples meet the end of the marginal buyer. Long grinding bears; breadth divergence gives the earliest warning, sometimes by years.

- Mechanical tops (1987; near-misses 1998, 2018, 2024): positioning and market structure produce the decline; the economy is fine. Fast, deep, fully retraced. Distinguished in real time by silent credit and stable leading indicators.

- Exogenous tops (2020, 1990's trigger): untimeable by construction. Entry valuation and volatility pricing determine the damage; policy speed determines the duration.

### What leads, what coincides, what lags

- Breadth divergence leads valuation tops by months to years (1972 by ~1yr, 1998–2000 by ~2yrs, 2021's speculative tier by 11 months) but is absent at policy and exogenous tops. Long lead, low precision.

- Curve inversion leads recessions — and therefore recession bears — by 6 to 18 months (1973, 1980–81, 1989, 2000, 2006, 2019, 2022) but says nothing about non-recession drawdowns and has fired without a bear following on schedule.

- Credit-spread acceleration off the trough led the 2007 top by four months and confirmed 2000; it stayed correctly silent in 1987, 2018, 2020, and January 2022. High precision for credit bears specifically — the reason it warrants its own parallel signal.

- Valuation never times anything, in either direction. It sets the asymmetry: entry CAPE explains most of the variance in ten-year forward returns across this study (CAPE 7–13 bottoms produced the +300% decades; the CAPE 44 top produced the lost decade), but markets topped at CAPE 9.5 and bottomed at CAPE 27.

- Momentum is intact at every top by definition — price is at highs — and confirms rather than anticipates. Its value is on the other side: trend-following exits late but exits, and avoided the middle of every extended bear in the study.

- Policy pivots coincide with bottoms in credit and mechanical bears almost to the day (Aug 1982, Oct 1987, Mar 2009, Mar 2020) but not in inflation bears, where the pivot comes long after the low (1974, 2022).

### Depth, duration, and the shape of recoveries

Recession bears in this study average roughly −40% over 18 months (1973, 1980, 1990, 2000, 2007, 2020); non-recession bears average roughly −29% over 6 months (1987, 2022). Recoveries to prior highs ranged from five months (2020) to seven years in nominal terms (1973, 2000-adjacent) — and the difference is almost entirely explained by two variables: the valuation at the top (how much multiple had to be given back) and the speed of the policy response. The near-misses resolved fastest of all, uniformly within weeks of a credible policy turn, precisely because no earnings or credit damage needed repair.

### Implications for the framework

- No composite can catch all top types — the calibration's 0-for-3 on tops with the original weights was structural, not parametric. The parallel-signal architecture (concentration for valuation tops, credit acceleration for 2007-types, liquidity for plumbing stress) maps one overlay to each historically distinct failure mode.

- Bottom detection should stay composite-driven (the signature is common) but requires a second lens for macro-driven bears: the rate of change of the driving variable, which caught 2022 where capitulation mechanics did not exist.

- Controls are the standard of proof: a framework's value is established less by the tops it flags than by the 1994/1998/2015/2018/2024-type events it correctly refuses to call. Specificity is the scarce commodity.

## 12. Today Against History (May 2026)

This chapter — deliberately the only place the present appears — maps the May 2026 environment against every episode in the study. Current readings: S&P 500 near 7,500; CAPE ~42; forward P/E ~21x; top-ten index concentration ~40%; equity risk premium ~0.5%; VIX ~17; high-yield spreads ~286bp, roughly 8% above their trailing-twelve-month low; CPI 3.8% and drifting up; the funds rate at 4.25% with the curve flat after a long inversion and re-inversion at the front end; Fed net liquidity contracting; bank reserves approaching the system's estimated comfort floor.

### Where today resembles each episode

- 2000 — the valuation and narrative comp: CAPE 42 versus 43.8 is the tightest valuation match to any prior top; the equity risk premium near zero matches; a single transformative-technology narrative (internet then, AI now) justifies the premium; marquee private names approach the IPO window. Differences: today's leaders have enormous real earnings and margins where 2000's long tail did not; credit was already wide and widening in March 2000, while today it is tight; and volatility churned in 2000 where today it is complacent.

- 2007 — the policy-stance and credit-cycle comp: a restrictive Fed near the same level (4.25 vs 4.75%), a curve recently un-inverted, inflation in the same 3.5–3.8% band, VIX similarly calm. The decisive difference is cycle position: HY spreads in October 2007 were +59% off their trough with the banking system fracturing; today they are +8% off the trough — by the credit-acceleration lens, today resembles late 2006 more than late 2007. Banks are far better capitalized; the untested channel is the \$2T private-credit market, where defaults (5.2%) already diverge from placid public spreads — a bifurcation with no 2007 analog.

- 2022 — the concentration and complacency comp: forward P/E within a point (21.1 vs 21.7), VIX within a point, tight spreads, a speculative apparatus running. Differences: the Fed is at 4.25% rather than zero, so the 2022 trigger — a violent repricing of the policy path from emergency settings — is not available in the same form; and concentration at 40% is far beyond 2022's 28%.

- 1973 — the two-tier structure comp: a revered cohort commanding premium multiples while the median stock lags; 53.7% of issues above their 200-day averages against an index at highs echoes the 1971–72 divergence. The macro rhyme is looser but present: inflation above target with the policy room to fight it constrained — then by politics, now by debt service and fiscal arithmetic.

- 1987 and 2024 — the market-structure comp: elevated momentum, compressed volatility, and a large mechanical complex (then portfolio insurance; now short-vol strategies, levered ETFs, dealer gamma, carry trades) capable of manufacturing a violent air pocket without a cycle turn. The 2024 unwind demonstrated the mechanism at small scale.

### Where today matches no precedent

- Concentration: 40% of index capitalization in ten names exceeds every prior reading in this study — 2000 (~25%), 2007 (~19%), 2022 (~28%). Whether this reflects durable scale economics or index-flow mechanics is the study's largest open question, and no historical episode resolves it.

- The fiscal-monetary configuration: debt-to-GDP over 100%, deficits near 6% of GDP in an expansion, net interest at record share of revenue, and real rates at or above real growth — a constraint set no prior top faced. It cuts both ways: less room to rescue, and more incentive to tolerate inflation and suppress real rates, which historically favors real assets over duration.

- The AI capex cycle: ~\$320B of annual hyperscaler investment funded from operating cash flow rather than leverage — structurally unlike 2000's debt-funded telecom build. If the demand materializes, the multiple is defensible; if it does not, the unwind hits earnings rather than solvency. Neither 2000 nor any other episode is a clean template.

- Private credit at ~\$2T: a lending channel that did not exist at scale in any prior episode, currently showing the study's only visible credit stress. Whether it transmits or contains that stress is untested through a full cycle.

### The honest synthesis

Today combines 2000's valuation, 2007's policy stance, 2022's concentration and complacency, 1973's two-tier structure, and 1987's mechanical fragility — while lacking, as of this writing, every historical trigger: credit is not accelerating (the 2007 tell), the Fed is not being forced to tighten (the 1973/2022 tell), no funding event has broken (the 1987/2020 tell), and the capital window for the favored cohort remains wide open (the 2000 tell). The study's framework reads this precisely: a composite in overextended territory with the concentration overlay fully active describes the asymmetry — the entry conditions resemble the worst starting points in fifty-six years — while quiet credit-acceleration and liquidity overlays describe the missing timing. History's clearest instruction is about which tripwires to watch, in order: the rate of credit widening off the trough, the plumbing of dollar funding, and the moment the inflation drift forces the policy bind. History's second instruction is humility about the first: every top in this study arrived through a door most observers were not watching.
