### Reader's note

Most of the time markets do what they did yesterday, and most of the library is about how they do it. This paper is about the minority of days and weeks when that stops: the top that ends a bull market, the bottom that ends a bear, the bubble that ends in a crash, and the funding failure that turns a decline into a crisis. They are few, they are where most of a decade's return is made or lost, and they are where a trader's process is most likely to fail. The paper is written for the operator who must act at them, in real time, with the information that existed on the day.

It inherits a study. Tops and Bottoms, the library's episode paper, examined every major U.S. turning point since 1970: eight bear markets, six near-misses, and a synthesis whose central sentence — tops are idiosyncratic, bottoms rhyme — has survived everything since. The Top & Bottom harness has tested that sentence against its own calibration, and the Top & Bottom Report Manual records what it found. Part I keeps the eight episodes and the near-misses and reads them again with the harness's findings. The study's own text is preserved in the archive, and nothing in it is contradicted here. It is extended.

Parts II to VI add what the study did not cover: bubbles as an anatomy, banking crises, liquidity crises, emerging-market crises and tops that begin outside the United States. Each is written as a mechanism, the way a crisis actually propagates, with the tells the stack already prints at each stage. Part VII is the hardest problem in the paper, reading a turn from inside, and it gives the system's three instruments for doing so and the study that shows their limit. Part VIII is the operating chapter: what each book does, which report prints which signpost, and which environments the mechanisms belong to.

Three boundaries keep the paper honest. **Debt Cycles, Regimes and Environments owns the long-cycle tops and the debt-cycle lens.** Its Part III narrates 1929, 1989, 2000 and 2007 as the ends of long cycles, and this paper reads those years only as turning points: what the tape and the indicators showed, not why the cycle ended. **The Top & Bottom Report Manual owns the T&B methodology**: the composite, the triggers, the overlays and the calibration. This paper reports their findings and does not restate how they are computed. **The present is quarantined in Appendix B**, dated, with no forecast. The body describes how turns work. It does not say where the market is.

### Prerequisites

The paper assumes the four books of the Operating Doctrine's Part IV, defined here where first used. **Book A, allocation**, holds positions for one to six months and is run by exposure bands set from the regime; its question at a turn is how much to own. **Book B, swing**, holds positions for one to six weeks on trend, momentum and variant perception; its question is which stage of a move to trade. **Book C, tactical**, holds positions for one to five days on event reactions, dealer positioning and session structure; it works at the edges of a turn, where the moves are fastest. **Book D, opportunistic**, has no fixed horizon and takes small, defined-risk positions on behavioral and alternative data, forced flows and mispriced optionality; at a turn it is where convexity lives.

It also assumes the vocabulary of Base Rates (a conditional rate is read beside its unconditional one), of the Rate and Liquidity Machine (reserves, the Treasury General Account, the repo market), of Credit (spreads as the price of default and of liquidity), and of the Dealer's Hand (gamma and the flip). Each is cited where it is needed and not re-taught.

The paper uses one convention throughout. A **bear market** is a fall of about 20 percent or more in the S&P 500 on a closing basis, the convention Tops and Bottoms adopted. A **turn** is the peak or trough that begins or ends one. A **near-miss** is a decline that looked like the start of a bear and resolved without one.

# Part I — What Tops and Bottoms Established

## Chapter 1 — The eight episodes, kept

Tops and Bottoms studied eight bear markets in the S&P 500 since 1970, and its first discipline was to describe each one strictly historically, with no comparison to the present inside an episode chapter. That discipline is kept here. The eight are summarised as turning points, each with what made the top, what made the bottom, and the type the study assigned it. The full chapters, with each episode's indicators at the top and the bottom and its standardised aftermath, are in the archived study.

**1973–74, the Nifty Fifty and the inflation bear.** A two-tier market — fifty "one-decision" growth stocks at multiples near forty while the rest of the market sat near half that — met accelerating inflation and a tightening Federal Reserve. The oil embargo of late 1973 turned a valuation problem into a stagflation problem. The decline ran from January 1973 to October 1974 and took close to half the index's value, the longest and deepest bear of the period until 2007. The bottom came at a single-digit price-to-earnings ratio, with inflation still in double digits. The market turned before inflation did, on the second derivative of the variable that drove it down. Type: inflation and policy, with a valuation tier.

**1980–82, the Volcker bear.** This top was cheap. The market's cyclically adjusted multiple was under ten, and the bear was made entirely by policy: a Federal Reserve holding the funds rate near twenty percent to break inflation, a deeply inverted curve and a recession. The decline, from late 1980 to August 1982, was a little over a quarter, shallow by the standard of the others. It ended within days of the Fed's easing in the summer of 1982, which also began the longest bull market of the century. Type: policy.

**1987, the crash without a recession.** The market rose about a third in the first eight months of 1987 while bond yields climbed. Then, in the week of 19 October, it fell by roughly a third, with the largest one-day decline on record on the Monday. The mechanism was structural: portfolio insurance programmes selling futures into a falling market, which fed the fall they were hedging. The economy did not contract, credit did not break, and the Fed's statement of liquidity support the next morning ended the panic. The index regained its prior high within two years. Type: mechanical.

**1990, the Gulf War bear and the credit crunch.** A late-cycle economy already constrained by a savings-and-loan collapse and tight bank lending was hit by an oil shock when Iraq invaded Kuwait. The decline, July to October 1990, stopped a fraction short of twenty percent on a closing basis. The study included it because a recession accompanied it and convention counts it. The bottom came at peak fear about the war, with no volatility index yet in existence to mark it. Type: exogenous shock onto a credit-constrained economy.

**2000–02, the dot-com unwind.** Here valuation was the whole story. The cyclically adjusted multiple stood at an all-time high, the equity risk premium was near zero, technology was the largest weight in the index, and new issuance was at a record. The top in March 2000 was preceded by about two years of narrowing breadth, as fewer and fewer stocks carried the index. The decline ran two and a half years, through a recession and a wave of accounting frauds, and took about half the index. Type: valuation and capital exhaustion.

**2007–09, the global financial crisis.** This is the study's most important episode for this paper, because it was the deepest and because it was invisible to the equity indicators. Equity multiples at the October 2007 top were moderate, implied volatility was low, and spreads were tight in level. The excess lived on lenders' balance sheets and in the funding structure of the shadow banking system. The decline ran seventeen months and took more than half the index. Its bottom in March 2009 came with policy at its maximum scale: rates at zero, the first round of asset purchases and the bank stress tests. Type: credit.

**2020, the pandemic crash.** An exogenous shock arrived on a market at a high multiple and low volatility. The decline, about a third in five weeks from February to March, was the fastest bear in the record. It ended on the day the Federal Reserve announced unlimited asset purchases and corporate credit facilities. Type: exogenous, with a liquidity crisis inside it (Part IV).

**2022, the inflation and rates bear.** A market at a cyclically adjusted multiple near forty met inflation at a forty-year high and the fastest tightening in four decades. Stocks and bonds fell together. There was no recession, no credit blow-out and no panic spike in volatility. The decline, January to October, took about a quarter of the index, and the bottom came as the inflation rate inflected, not on a pivot in policy. Type: valuation and policy collision, with no credit dimension.

The eight are summarised below as a cheat sheet. The type is the study's; the decline is the closing peak to trough *(the S&P 500 on a closing basis; Tops and Bottoms' episode set, as published May 2026; cited 6 November 2026)*.

## Chapter 2 — The near-misses, kept

The study's third principle was to include the near-misses, because a study of confirmed turns alone has its survivorship built in. Six declines since 1990 looked like the start of a bear and resolved without one, and they are the false positives any turning-point method must survive. They are summarised here because the rest of the paper leans on them.

**1994** was a rate shock. A surprise tightening began a cycle that produced the worst bond year in decades, yet the S&P 500's decline stayed under ten percent. A strong economy and a young credit cycle absorbed it as a time correction rather than a price correction.

**1998** was a financial-stress event: Russia's default and the near-failure of a large leveraged hedge fund produced a decline of nearly a fifth in ten weeks. The Fed's three cuts and a privately financed rescue resolved it within two months. The study's sharpest observation about it is that the rescue itself fuelled the final leg of the 1999 bubble: emergency liquidity into a strong economy.

**2011** was a sovereign scare: a fight over the U.S. debt ceiling, a downgrade of U.S. debt and doubt about the eurozone. The decline also stopped a fraction short of a fifth. There was no U.S. recession, credit stress stayed a repricing rather than a default cycle, and the European Central Bank's liquidity operations removed the tail. The October low carried a full bottom signature, and the study scored it as a bottom.

**2015–16** was a sector recession. China's devaluation and the oil collapse produced a genuine default cycle in energy credit, and the high-yield index widened sharply. The stress never spread beyond energy and manufacturing. The study's lesson is that breadth of stress matters as much as its intensity.

**2018** was a policy error. The Fed was tightening and running down its balance sheet into slowing global growth while tariffs escalated, and the decline stopped two-tenths of a percent short of the bear threshold. It ended within days of the Fed's January 2019 pivot. The speed of the resolution is itself evidence that no organic recession or credit cycle was underway.

**2024** was a positioning unwind. A Bank of Japan hike forced the unwinding of yen-funded carry, and the volatility index printed a crisis-grade level intraday against a single-digit decline in the index. The market recovered in two weeks. Modern market structure can manufacture crisis-grade volatility with no economic event underneath it.

From the six, the study drew the rule that runs through the rest of this paper. **Corrections become bears when a recession or a credit event follows; absent both, every drawdown in the set resolved within months.** Recession accompanied every bear but 1987, and no near-miss. Bears featured broad credit deterioration or valuations extreme enough that multiple compression alone sufficed, while near-misses featured contained credit stress or none. Near-misses ended when policy turned supportive quickly, and bears persisted because policy either could not turn, as in the inflation regimes, or turned too slowly against a deleveraging. Volatility spikes and breadth washouts appeared in both populations, so they mark stress without telling a correction from a bear *(the six near-misses of 1994, 1998, 2011, 2015–16, 2018 and 2024 against the eight bears; Tops and Bottoms, May 2026; cited 6 November 2026)*.

What the operator does differently because of this chapter is to ask the two diagnostic questions on the first day of any sharp decline, and to keep asking them: is credit deteriorating broadly, and is the economy rolling over? Everything else is texture.

## Chapter 3 — What the harness has since learned

The Top & Bottom report scores the market against thresholds calibrated on these episodes, and its calibration is the most rigorous test the study's conclusions have faced. The Top & Bottom Report Manual describes it in full. Three findings matter for this paper, and each sharpens the study rather than overturning it.

**Bottoms are catchable.** Thirteen episodes — three tops, six bottoms and four controls — were run through the report's composite at their turning dates. Under the original weights, the composite classified four of the six bottoms and all four controls correctly *(the report's calibration of 3 September 2026; thirteen episodes, each scored once at its date; cited 6 November 2026)*. A bottom is a washout: spreads blown out, breadth destroyed, sentiment at its worst, positioning flushed. A composite built from those readings sees it, because every washout has those parts even when its cause differs. The two bottoms it missed had the study's two exceptions in them. 1990 bottomed without a volatility extreme, because the index barely existed. 2022 bottomed with almost none of the washout present, turning on the inflection of the variable that drove it. Macro-driven bears bottom on second derivatives; credit-driven bears bottom on the scale of the policy response.

**Most tops are not catchable by a composite.** The same calibration classified none of the three tops correctly. A top has a cause, and the cause differs: valuation in one episode, credit in another, policy in a third. An average across categories under-reads whichever category carries the episode. The report's response was to add three overlays independent of the composite, one for each historically distinct way a top forms: concentration and complacency for valuation tops, high-yield spread acceleration for credit tops, and liquidity and funding stress for plumbing tops. The study's sentence — tops are idiosyncratic, bottoms rhyme — is now built into the report's architecture.

**The 2007 top is structurally uncatchable by the composite.** A sweep of the composite's weights showed that no reweighting of the existing indicator set catches a 2007-type top. The multiple was elevated but not extreme, implied volatility was low, breadth was acceptable and spreads were tight in level. The fragility sat in leverage and in the structure of funding, which the composite did not measure. The top was not late in the indicators; it was absent from them. The high-yield acceleration overlay is the answer: a credit top read in the rate of change of spreads rather than their level. The study had already found that spread acceleration off its trough led the 2007 top by about four months and stayed correctly silent in 1987, 2018, 2020 and January 2022.

Two later results qualify the three. The harness's first point-in-time edition could not reproduce the composite's record on the box's own store, because the store does not yet hold the long histories the episodes need. Its verdict was "insufficient", not "failed" *(the T&B harness ledger, 26 October 2026; 13 episodes, 17 decision dates)*. The false-bottom audit tested the bottom side against the rallies that looked like bottoms and were not. It found one confirmation element that discriminated, relative breadth after the trough, against a pre-registered requirement of two *(the false-bottom audit, 16 rallies, ledger of 18 October 2026)*. The audit failed its own bar. So the catchable side is catchable at its washout, and much less so in the weeks after, when a rally must be told from a recovery.

## Chapter 4 — What survives: the taxonomy of tops

What survives from Part I is a taxonomy, and the rest of the paper is organised around it. The study sorted the eight tops into five kinds. The harness's overlays now map one instrument to each of the three kinds that leave a measurable trace in advance.

**Inflation and policy tops** — 1973, 1980 and 2022 — happen when the central bank is forced to tighten into a slowing market. Valuation can be high or low; the driver is the policy bind. Stocks and bonds fall together, and the bear ends when the inflation variable inflects, usually well before policy turns. The tell is the inflation rate and the curve, read through the Monthly's plumbing and the market-state object's Macro dial. The harness has no overlay for this kind. The study's second lens for macro-driven bears — the rate of change of the driving variable — is the instrument, and it lives in the Monthly rather than in the report.

**Credit tops** — 2007, and 1990 in part — happen when the excess lives on lenders' balance sheets. Equity valuation is silent. The tell is the rate of change of credit spreads off their trough, which the high-yield acceleration overlay reads, and the bank-stress panel of Part III. These produce the deepest drawdowns in the record.

**Valuation and capital-exhaustion tops** — 2000, and 1973's Nifty Fifty tier — happen when extreme multiples meet the end of the marginal buyer. Breadth divergence gives the earliest warning, sometimes years early, and concentration is the measurable form. The concentration overlay reads this kind, and Part II's bubble anatomy describes it.

**Mechanical tops** — 1987, and the near-misses of 1998, 2018 and 2024 — happen when positioning and market structure produce the decline while the economy is fine. They are fast, deep and fully retraced, and in real time they are distinguished by silent credit and stable leading indicators. The liquidity overlay and the dealer read are the instruments, and Part IV describes the plumbing.

**Exogenous tops** — 2020, and the trigger of 1990 — are untimeable by construction. The valuation at entry and the pricing of volatility decide the damage, and the speed of policy decides the duration. No instrument catches them in advance. The system's answer is sizing and convexity, not prediction.

| Kind of top | Episodes | Lead tell | The stack's instrument | Catchable in advance? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inflation and policy | 1973, 1980, 2022 | The driving variable's rate of change | The Monthly; the Macro dial | Partly, by the macro lens |
| Credit | 2007, 1990 in part | Spread acceleration off the trough | HY acceleration overlay; bank stress | Yes, by rate of change, not level |
| Valuation and exhaustion | 2000, 1973 tier | Narrowing breadth; concentration | Concentration overlay; froth breadth | Early but imprecise |
| Mechanical | 1987; 1998, 2018, 2024 | Positioning; silent credit | Liquidity overlay; dealer read | Rarely; recognised as it happens |
| Exogenous | 2020; 1990's trigger | None | Sizing and convexity | No |

What the operator does differently because of Part I is to stop asking "is this the top?" and ask instead "which kind of top would this be, and is that kind's instrument firing?" A valuation instrument that is silent at a credit top is not wrong, and it is not reassurance either.

# Part II — Bubbles as an Anatomy

## Chapter 5 — The Minsky–Kindleberger sequence

A bubble is not a price that is too high. Prices are often too high for years without ending in a crash, and a definition that waits for the crash is a definition by outcome. The useful definition is a sequence: a way that credit, belief and price feed on each other through recognisable stages until the feeding stops. The sequence was set out by Hyman Minsky, whose financial instability hypothesis described how stability itself breeds the borrowing that makes a system fragile. Charles Kindleberger turned it into a history of manias across three centuries *(Minsky's Stabilizing an Unstable Economy, 1986, and his 1992 statement of the hypothesis; Kindleberger's Manias, Panics, and Crashes, first edition 1978; cited 6 November 2026)*. It has five stages, and each has a mechanism.

**Displacement.** Something real changes the outlook for some part of the economy: a technology (railways, radio, the internet, AI), a financial innovation, a change in policy or the end of a war. The displacement is genuine. That is why the sequence is so persuasive and so hard to resist. The profits it creates at first are real, and the early buyers are right. Nothing about the first stage is a mistake.

**Boom.** Credit expands to finance the opportunity, prices rise, and the rise attracts capital. Minsky's contribution is the change in how the borrowing is structured. Early borrowers can repay principal and interest from their income, which Minsky called hedge finance. As the boom continues, borrowers increasingly can pay only the interest and must roll the principal (speculative finance), and finally some can pay neither and rely on the asset's price continuing to rise (Ponzi finance). The share of Ponzi finance is the system's fragility. It grows in the good years because the good years make it look safe.

**Euphoria.** The price rise becomes its own justification. New buyers arrive who would not have bought the asset on its fundamentals, because it has risen. Issuance surges as owners and promoters sell to them. Valuation frameworks are replaced by new ones that justify the price ("this time is different", "a new era"). Concentration rises as the leaders of the displacement become the index. The stage is marked by a change in who is buying, from the informed to the uninformed and from the patient to the levered.

**Profit-taking, or distress.** The informed begin to sell. Prices stop rising and drift; the leaders lose momentum while the rest of the market looks fine. Credit begins to tighten at the margin, because lenders notice the Ponzi borrowers. This is the stage Kindleberger called distress: a period, sometimes long, in which the bubble neither grows nor bursts and in which nobody can tell whether it is a pause or the end. Most of the money lost in bubbles is lost by people buying in this stage, because the price is lower than the peak and the story is unchanged.

**Panic, or revulsion.** Something forces sales: a failure, a fraud, a rate rise, a margin call. The Ponzi borrowers must sell to meet obligations, their selling lowers prices, and lower prices force more borrowers into the same position. The sequence that built the bubble now runs in reverse, with credit contracting as fast as it expanded. Revulsion follows, a refusal to touch the asset at any price, which carries prices below any measure of value. That overshoot is the next cycle's displacement opportunity.

The sequence is a mechanism, not a calendar. Stages can last months or years, can be skipped, and can repeat: a panic inside a boom becomes a correction, and the boom resumes. The 1998 near-miss of Chapter 2 is the canonical case of a panic arrested by policy, after which euphoria resumed. And not every displacement ends in a bubble. The best large-sample test found that a sharp run-up in an industry's price, taken alone, does not predict a negative return on average. It does raise the probability of a crash, and the probability rises further when the run-up comes with high volatility, heavy issuance, rising turnover and an accelerating price path *(Greenwood, Shleifer and You, U.S. industries from 1928 and international industries from 1985, with run-ups of 100 percent or more over two years; published 2019; cited 6 November 2026)*. Those features are the stack's signposts, and Chapter 6 maps them.

| Stage | Mechanism | Who is buying | What changes in credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Displacement | A real change in the outlook | The informed early | Little |
| Boom | Credit finances the opportunity | The informed, then the trend | Hedge finance turns speculative |
| Euphoria | The rise justifies itself | The uninformed and the levered | Ponzi finance grows; issuance surges |
| Distress | The informed sell; leaders stall | Dip buyers | Tightens at the margin |
| Panic and revulsion | Forced sales feed on themselves | Nobody, then value at the overshoot | Contracts as fast as it grew |

## Chapter 6 — The signposts the stack carries at each stage

The sequence is useful to an operator only if its stages can be recognised from data available on the day. The stack carries six families of signpost that bear on the bubble sequence. None of them identifies a stage alone; read together, they narrow the range. Each is described here by what it measures and what it has historically shown, and the cheat sheet at the end maps each to the stages where it moves.

**Valuation** is the slowest signpost and the most often misused. The cyclically adjusted multiple, the forward multiple and the equity risk premium measure how much optimism is in the price. They set the asymmetry of a decline — how far there is to fall — and they explain much of the variance of ten-year returns across the episode study. They do not time anything. The study recorded tops at a cyclically adjusted multiple under ten and bottoms near thirty *(Tops and Bottoms, the eight episodes, May 2026; cited 6 November 2026)*. In the sequence, valuation rises through boom and euphoria and stays high through distress, so a high reading says the bubble has formed but not which stage it is in.

**Concentration** measures how much of the index the displacement's leaders have become. It rises through the boom and peaks near the top of the euphoria, because euphoria is when the leaders are bought for their own momentum. Concentration is the measurable form of the breadth divergence that led the valuation tops by months to years. The concentration overlay reads it, together with complacency in volatility. The routed table of Chapter 8 gives it a historical ceiling, and the concentration twin — the cap-weighted index against its equal-weighted version — prints it daily.

**Issuance** measures the supply response. In euphoria, owners and promoters sell to the new buyers through initial offerings, secondary offerings and convertible debt. Issuance in the bubble's sector rising faster than the sector's earnings is one of the clearest stage markers there is, because it means the informed are selling. The stack carries the hyperscalers' filed capital spending and financing from EDGAR, and the private AI-financing ledger the register proposes covers what does not reach the public bond data.

**Leverage** measures the Ponzi share. Margin debt, leveraged exchange-traded product assets, levered single-stock products and the funding of the bubble's builders all rise through the boom and euphoria and fall in the panic. Leverage's turn is a better marker of the transition from distress to panic than price, because forced selling begins when leverage starts to contract. The stack's fragility panel holds margin debt and its share of household equity, and the leveraged-ETF rebalance. Batch 25 adds free credit balances and a deleveraging flag, a two-month decline of ten percent or more.

**The froth gauge** measures speculation at the edge: how many assets are in a parabolic state, and how much of the index they carry. The register's froth-breadth count (item 113's amendment of item 31) counts the S&P 500 stocks whose six-month return is more than two standard deviations above normal with an accelerating price path, by sector. The Digital Assets addendum's froth gauge does the same for new crypto listings. Froth breadth is the euphoria signpost. Its most useful property is that it shows where froth is moving. A rotation can carry froth from the bubble's sector into defensive sectors, which then become the crowded hiding place.

**The Capitulation Count** measures the panic and the revulsion. It is the system's five-family bottom checklist: volatility, equity-only put/call, the dealer gamma flip, short interest against price, and flows with sentiment. Part VII describes it. In the sequence, it moves only at the end, and it measures exhaustion of selling, not the end of the bubble's cause. A count of four or five in the panic says the selling is exhausted for now. It does not say the revulsion is over.

| Signpost | What it measures | Rises through | Peaks or turns at | The stack's read |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valuation | Optimism in the price | Boom, euphoria | Stays high into distress | CAPE, forward P/E, ERP; the Monthly |
| Concentration | The leaders' share of the index | Boom | Late euphoria | Concentration overlay; the twin; item 31's band |
| Issuance | The informed selling to the new buyer | Euphoria | Late euphoria | EDGAR capital spending; the financing ledger |
| Leverage | The Ponzi share | Boom, euphoria | Its contraction starts the panic | Fragility panel; margin debt; levered ETFs |
| Froth breadth | Parabolic assets and their weight | Euphoria | Euphoria; it rotates in distress | Item 113's count; the crypto froth gauge |
| Capitulation Count | Selling exhausted | — | Panic | Item 97's five families |

What the operator does differently because of this chapter is to read the six together and ask which pattern they make, not whether any one of them is extreme. High valuation with rising concentration, issuance and leverage is euphoria. High valuation with stalled leaders, falling froth and leverage beginning to fall is distress. Only the second is a reason for Book A to act.

## Chapter 7 — A hold-and-trade playbook

A bubble poses a different question to each book, because each book lives on a different clock. The worst mistake is to answer one book's question with another's: a Book A stance moved on a Book C signal, or a Book C trade sized on a Book A conviction. The Doctrine's Rule 19 (top-down sets the budget; a lower horizon may veto but never enlarge) already forbids the second. This chapter says what each book does at each stage. It is a playbook of roles, not a prediction of which stage the market is in. The Doctrine owns the rules and Portfolio Construction owns the sizes.

**Book A holds through.** The allocation book's job in a bubble is the hardest and the simplest: stay inside its band and let the regime, not the narrative, move it. Through displacement and boom, Book A owns the market at the band the regime sets, which means owning the bubble's leaders at their index weight. Underweighting them because they look expensive is the error the Doctrine's Rule 29 names, the cost of being out, and in a long boom it is the largest cost in the record. Through euphoria, Book A does not sell because the market is expensive. It lets the concentration overlay and the regime's stamps move the band, and the high-yield acceleration overlay is the trigger it most needs to respect. In distress, Book A's question is whether the regime has turned, and the T&B composite and overlays are the instruments. A stalled leader is not a turned regime. In the panic, Book A holds its floor, and re-entry after the panic waits for the false-bottom gate, not the first rally (Chapter 8). The book's discipline at a bubble is to be wrong slowly and recover fully, rather than right early and out of the market for years.

**Book B trades the stages.** The swing book is where a bubble's stages are traded directly, because its one-to-six-week clock matches the length of a stage's moves. In boom and euphoria, Book B trades with the trend in the leaders, and its exit discipline (the written exit, the time stop) is what keeps a trend trade from becoming a belief. In distress, Book B's edge is variant perception: the leaders stall while the index holds, and the relative trade — the leaders against the equal-weighted index, or the bubble's sector against defensives — is cleaner than the directional one. In the panic, Book B's counter-trend trades carry the five-session time stop of the Doctrine's Rule 27. A short in the panic is closed on the Top & Bottom bottom signal, not on conviction (Doctrine Rule 26). The swing book's characteristic error in a bubble is to short the euphoria because it is irrational, and the study's 1999 lesson is that irrational markets go further than anyone's patience.

**Book C works the edges.** The tactical book trades the violent sessions at the turns: the blow-off days in late euphoria, the first failed rallies of distress, the capitulation sessions of the panic. Its instruments are the dealer read (negative gamma amplifies; a flip back above the gamma level marks exhaustion), the Capitulation Count, and the event-reaction setup. Book C does not decide what stage the market is in. It takes the edge's mechanical trade at Book C size and leaves the stage question to Books A and B. Its characteristic error is to treat a capitulation session as the bottom and hold it into Book A's horizon.

**Book D holds the convexity.** The opportunistic book's role is the cheap option on the panic. Its tail budget buys defined-risk protection in euphoria, when implied volatility is low because the market is calm, and spends it in the panic, when protection is expensive and pays. Book D is also where forced flows are traded: the levered-ETF rebalance and the margin-call liquidation, each with defined risk.

## Chapter 8 — The routed items: peak concentration and the bear rally

The signal-triage register routes two items on bubbles to this paper. Each is read here as a mechanism, with the caveats the register filed with it.

**The peak-concentration table (item 31).** BofA Global Investment Strategy's table of the top basket's share of market capitalisation at the peaks of historic bubbles puts the share in a narrow band. Railroads in the nineteenth century reached about 63 percent; the utilities, telecommunications and industrials of the 1920s about 36 percent; the Nifty Fifty about 40 percent; Japan about 44 percent of the world index in 1989; technology, media and telecom about 41 percent in 2000 *(BofA Global Investment Strategy's chart, relayed through secondary reporting; filed with Batch 6, 15 October 2026; cited 6 November 2026)*.

The register's ruling carries two caveats that this paper repeats because they matter more than the numbers. The baskets are defined after the fact, by people who knew which stocks led. And the measures differ: Japan's is a country's share of a world index, not a basket's share of a domestic one. The table says that when the leaders of a displacement reach roughly two-fifths of the market, the market has usually been at or near the end of a bubble. It does not say that concentration at that level causes a top, or that a top must follow.

The actionable half of the table is the half the register asked for and has not yet built: what followed a 40 percent share, measured as the leaders' relative drawdown, the time to the trough and the behaviour of breadth. The ruling added a peak-band flag on the box's own top-ten share against the 40 to 44 percent historical band, as a calibration of the concentration overlay's existing input with no new weight. The present reading belongs in Appendix B. In the bubble sequence, the table locates late euphoria, and the operator reads it with the other five signposts of Chapter 6, never alone.

**The bear-rally structure (SR-8).** The register's SR-8 entry describes the other end of the sequence. Inside a bear, rallies are large, frequent and indistinguishable in real time from the start of a new bull. The Nasdaq Composite from March 2000 to October 2002 produced five rallies of 12 to 45 percent inside a decline of 78 percent, each a "bottom" in real time *(the Nasdaq Composite, March 2000 to October 2002; the register's SR-8 entry, filed 19 September 2026)*.

The mechanism is the panic's own structure. Forced sellers overshoot. Short sellers and the dip-buying cohort cover and buy, producing a violent rally. The rally attracts trend-followers. When the forced selling resumes — because the cause of the bear has not been repaired — the rally fails. Each rally is fuelled by positioning, not by a change in the cause. That is why the register's confirmation set looks for a change in the cause. It asks for a breadth thrust, leadership by new groups rather than the most-shorted prior leaders, spreads tightening through the rally, a volatility term structure back in contango, and a successful retest. It also carries a time prior: bears off valuation peaks have run two to three years, so a bottom four months in is a low-probability call on the base rate alone.

The system's response is a gate. Once the Top & Bottom top side has fired and price is below a falling 200-day average, Book A's re-entry needs the bottom composite plus two of the confirmation set, and rallies in that state are Book C material. The false-bottom audit tested the confirmation set against the rallies that failed and found only relative breadth after the trough discriminated (Chapter 3). The gate's rule therefore stands on one admitted tell. The proposed Doctrine rule words the behavioral point: in a declared bear regime a rally is a mechanism, not a signal, and size should assume that a bear rally and a new bull are indistinguishable in real time.
