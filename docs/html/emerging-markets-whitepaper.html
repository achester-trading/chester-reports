Purpose and How to Use This Document

The library has had no emerging-markets paper. Emerging markets have appeared in it as a paragraph in the International Equities paper, a pillar in the Monthly manual, two scenarios in The Twenty-Five and a mechanism in the Currencies paper — each correct, each partial, and none of them the place a reader could go to understand the asset class as one thing. The Alternative Assets Quarterly gives emerging markets a deep chapter with China inside it, and a chapter needs a frame that holds still while the quarter moves. This paper is that frame.

The frame is not new; it is assembled. Four sentences already in the library carry most of the weight, and this paper's first job is to put them side by side, quoted rather than paraphrased, so that nothing here becomes a new claim by accident of rewording:

  1. Emerging markets in aggregate are "best understood by this system as a dollar-cycle instrument with a commodity overlay", and "that makes the aggregate a poor vehicle for a country view and a reasonable vehicle for a dollar view" — the International Equities paper, Chapter 12.
  2. "Dollar moves tend to lead EM equity and credit performance by weeks", and "a portfolio that is long EM equity, long commodities, and short dollars has one position, not three" — the Monthly manual, Chapter 7 (Predictive Properties; How Institutions Use It).
  3. "The consistent pattern is that contagion runs through funding channels rather than through trade linkages", and the cross-currency basis "is the one that would fire first in a disorderly version" — The Twenty-Five, Scenario 15 (Precedent; Tripwire).
  4. "Depreciation raises the local-currency value of dollar debt, which forces selling of local assets, which drives further depreciation" — the Currencies paper, §3, which calls it "the mechanism behind every emerging-market currency crisis of the past forty years".

Everything else in the paper either elaborates one of those four, tests it, or says where it stops being true.

Three principles govern the writing.

The aggregate and the country are different objects. The first sentence above is a statement about an index, and an index is a construction — which countries a provider classifies as emerging, how much of each is accessible to a foreigner, and which sectors happen to be listed. Part I spends a section on that, because on 30 September 2026 the construction had drifted far enough that a reader who still pictures "emerging markets" as Brazilian iron ore and Chinese banks is reading a different index from the one that is quoted. The country-level mechanics — the funding channel, the loop, the vulnerability lenses — are older and more stable than the aggregate's composition, and the paper keeps them apart.

Lenses, not a score. The vulnerability framework in Part IV is a table of lenses with no composite. That is the architecture's §26.8 moratorium applied, and it is also the right answer on the merits: every published composite of emerging-market vulnerability has a decade it would have scored well and a crisis it missed, and the miss is always in the lens the composite averaged away.

The crisis history is not here. The reserved Markets paper Turning Points, Bubbles, and Crises carries the emerging-market currency–banking–sovereign crises — 1994, 1997–98, 2001, 2015, 2018 — as narratives. This paper names those episodes only as pointers, where a mechanism needs an instance, and does not retell them. The boundary is deliberate: a mechanism paper that also narrates its crises ends up fitting the mechanism to the narratives.

What the paper is not. Nothing in it is a trade, a recommendation or a ranking of countries to own. The scenario probabilities in Part VIII are priors for the Quarterly's thesis register, written to be graded, and the falsification tests in Part VII are written in measurable form because the Quarterly's chapter encodes them verbatim. The paper follows the library's confidence convention: unmarked prose is mechanism; an empirical claim carries its sample and its date in a closing italic clause; a level carries its series and its as-of date.

PART I — THE MAP

1. What "Emerging" Means

"Emerging market" is not an economic category. It is an index provider's classification, and the classification turns less on income than on access: whether a foreign investor can get money in and out, whether the market's settlement, custody and ownership rules meet a standard, whether the size and liquidity of the listed universe clear a bar. Korea and Taiwan have per-capita incomes above several euro-area members and remain emerging markets in the MSCI scheme on market-access grounds; Greece was reclassified down in 2013 and is again emerging; several Gulf markets were reclassified up into emerging status in the last decade. The MSCI Emerging Markets Index on 30 September 2026 covered twenty-four countries and 1,165 constituents, capturing about 85% of the free-float capitalisation of each (MSCI factsheet, 30 Sep 2026). Frontier markets sit below it with their own index, and the line between the two moves at the provider's annual market-classification review each June.

Three consequences follow, and each matters to how the rest of the paper is read.

The aggregate's composition is set by rules that are not about economics. When a large market is added or reweighted — China A-shares' partial inclusion from 2018, Saudi Arabia's in 2019, Kuwait's in 2020 — the "emerging-markets" exposure of every benchmarked fund changes on an announced date for reasons no growth or currency model contains. The forced flows around those dates belong to the Positioning & Flows paper's index-rebalance holders; here the point is only that the object called "EM" is redefined periodically by committee.

Free float, not output, sets the weight. China is roughly a fifth of the index and about seventeen percent of world output (the International Equities paper, Chapter 1.1, mid-2026); Taiwan is under one percent of world output and nearly thirty percent of the index (MSCI, 30 Sep 2026). The index weights the listed, accessible, free-floating equity of each country, and that is a statement about financial structure and about which sectors happen to be listed, not about economic size.

Graduation is a one-way exit for the winners. A market that develops far enough is reclassified to developed and leaves the index — Israel in 2010, and the long-discussed Korean case — which means the emerging aggregate systematically sheds its most successful members and keeps the rest. This is a survivorship effect in reverse, and it is one reason the aggregate's long-run return understates what an investor in "the countries that were emerging in 1988" would have earned.

2. Who Is How Big

In capitalisation. The MSCI Emerging Markets Index's free-float capitalisation was \$12.3 trillion on 30 September 2026 (MSCI factsheet, USD). In the global index the emerging share has historically sat near a tenth; the International Equities paper's map puts China, India, Taiwan-plus-Korea and the rest of EM together at roughly ten to fourteen percent of global equity capitalisation (Chapter 1.1, mid-2026, approximate and drifting), against roughly two-thirds for the United States.

In output. Emerging and developing economies are a little over two-fifths of world output at market exchange rates and close to three-fifths at purchasing-power parity (IMF World Economic Outlook aggregates, April 2026 edition, rounded; the scorecard's WEO writer stores the country rows, not these aggregates). The gap between the output share and the capitalisation share is the largest single fact in the map: most emerging-market economic activity is either unlisted, state-held, or listed in a form foreigners cannot fully own.

3. Three Instruments, Three Exposures

"Emerging markets" names at least three different exposures, and a view that does not say which one it means is under-specified in the same way the International Equities paper says a "China position" is.

Hard-currency debt — sovereign and corporate bonds issued in dollars (and some euros) under foreign law. Its return is a U.S. Treasury return plus a credit spread. The holder takes no currency risk directly: the bond pays dollars. What the holder takes instead is the issuer's ability to find dollars, which is exactly the variable the EM loop of §12 attacks. The spread is the price of that risk, and it widens in a dollar squeeze not because the bond's currency moved but because the borrower's did. The benchmark families are J.P. Morgan's EMBI (sovereign) and CEMBI (corporate), which are proprietary; the free daily proxies are the ICE BofA emerging-markets corporate option-adjusted spreads on FRED, whose public history has been shortened to three years.

Local-currency debt — government bonds issued in the borrower's own currency under local law, held by foreigners through index funds and directly. Its dollar return is three terms: the local yield, the change in local yields, and the change in the currency. For most of the last fifteen years the third term has dominated the first two, which is why the asset class's long-run dollar return disappointed against its carry: the yield was paid and the currency took it back. Owning local debt is mostly owning the currency with a yield attached.

Equity — listed shares, held through the index, country funds and single names. Its dollar return is local earnings growth, the change in the multiple, the dividend and buyback yield, and the currency. In an aggregate as concentrated as §2 describes, it is also a sector bet that dwarfs the country bet: a dollar-based holder of the index on 30 September 2026 held more information technology than any other single exposure, including any single country's currency.

The table is a cheat sheet for one idea: the dollar reaches all three, through three different doors. In hard-currency debt it comes in through the spread; in local debt through the exchange rate; in equity through both, plus the earnings of companies with dollar costs or dollar revenues. That is why the three usually fall together in a dollar squeeze even though their contracts have little in common, and it is the mechanical content behind the Monthly manual's "one position, not three".

Original sin, and its partial redemption. For most of the 1980s and 1990s emerging sovereigns could borrow abroad only in dollars — the condition the economists Eichengreen and Hausmann named "original sin" — and every depreciation therefore raised the burden of the sovereign's own debt. Over the 2000s and 2010s the larger emerging markets built local-currency bond markets that foreigners bought, and much sovereign borrowing moved into local currency. The redemption was partial in two ways that matter. The risk did not disappear; it moved to the foreign holder of the local bond, who now sells the currency rather than the bond's issuer defaulting — "original sin redux", in the later literature's phrase — so the loop of §12 now runs through capital flight instead of through debt service. And corporate borrowers did not follow the sovereigns: emerging-market corporate dollar debt exceeds sovereign dollar debt in several large markets, which is The Twenty-Five's Scenario 15 mutation ("the borrower is a corporate rather than a sovereign") and the reason the free daily spread proxy this system stores is a corporate one.

4. The Aggregate Is an Index Decision

The International Equities paper's sentence has two halves: a dollar-cycle instrument with a commodity overlay. The first half is a statement about funding and the balance sheet, and it holds for any country with dollar liabilities, whatever it exports. The second half is a statement about composition, and composition moves.

In the 2000s, when the frame's evidence was accumulating, the aggregate's large weights were in materials, energy and financials, and its large countries included Brazil, Russia and South Africa; the commodity overlay was a direct exposure of the index's earnings. On 30 September 2026 materials and energy were together 9.2% of the index, and information technology 43.8% (MSCI, 30 Sep 2026). The overlay has not vanished — it lives on in the currencies of the exporters, in the terms of trade of the importers, and in China's demand for metals — but at the level of the index's own earnings it has been substantially replaced by a different overlay: the global semiconductor cycle, which is driven by the AI capital-expenditure cycle the Disruptive Themes report watches and the Equities paper's concentration chapter describes.

This changes how the frame should be read, without overturning it. The dollar half is a claim about mechanism and survives composition change. A strong dollar still tightens the funding of every emerging borrower and still pushes the currencies down, and a dollar-based holder of a North Asian technology index still takes the won and the Taiwan dollar. The overlay half is a claim about composition and has to be re-specified as the index changes. The honest statement for 2026 is that the aggregate is a dollar-cycle instrument with two overlays — a commodity one that dominates its currencies and its smaller members, and a semiconductor one that dominates its weight. Which overlay governs a given quarter is an empirical question with a measurable answer, and Part VII's test T7 asks it.

There is a practical corollary for the reader of a Quarterly chapter. When "EM" outperforms, the first question is not "is the dollar falling" but "which EM" — the index, the index ex-China (yfinance.mkt_emxc), the equal-weighted country basket of the shock screen, or the commodity exporters. The SR-14 ruling already asks for this decomposition ("USD vs. local; EM ex-China alongside; semis share noted"), and Part VI places it in Tier 1.

PART II — THE RECORD

5. Emerging Against Developed Since the Index Histories Begin

The MSCI Emerging Markets Index has a history from 31 December 1987; it was launched on 30 June 1988, and the six months before the launch are back-tested. Over the whole history it has compounded faster than the developed world and with far more pain: 10.5% a year in dollars against 9.0% for the MSCI World and 8.9% for the ACWI, with an annualised volatility of 17.5% against the ACWI's 14.7% over ten years (17.2% against 12.0% over three) and a maximum drawdown of 65.1% from October 2007 to October 2008, against 57.5% for the World (gross USD, 31 Dec 1987 – 30 Sep 2026; MSCI factsheet of 30 Sep 2026). The long-run premium is small relative to the variance, and it was earned almost entirely in two windows.

6. The Dollar Cycle Laid Over It

The SR-14 ruling stated the empirical claim this paper rests on, and stated it carefully: the relative cycle "is the equity expression of the dollar cycle: every completed turn (1988, 1994, 2001, 2010) sat within about a year of a dollar turn". Four completed turns, four dollar turns within a year. That is a small sample — the Evidence and Inference paper would say four observations give the sign and not the size — and it is a co-movement, not a lead, at the resolution of a turning point.

At annual resolution the record is noisier and still clear. In the fourteen calendar years from 2012 to 2025, the year's change in the broad trade-weighted dollar and the year's emerging-minus-ACWI return had opposite signs in eleven, and a correlation of −0.54 (FRED DTWEXBGS year-end to year-end; MSCI EM and ACWI gross USD calendar returns from the 30 Sep 2026 factsheet; computed 11 Oct 2026, n = 14). The three exceptions are informative rather than embarrassing:

  • 2016 — the dollar rose 4% and emerging markets still beat the ACWI by three points. The commodity rebound from the February 2016 low and China's credit stimulus outran the dollar. The overlay won a year.
  • 2019 — the dollar was roughly flat and emerging markets lagged by eight points. The U.S.–China tariff escalation was a China-specific, trade-channel shock in a year with no funding stress.
  • 2023 — the dollar fell 2.5% and emerging markets lagged by twelve points. China's post-reopening disappointment and the property drag dragged the aggregate while the rest of the index was flat to the dollar.

All three exceptions are China or commodities — the overlays — overriding the dollar for a year. None is a year in which the dollar rose sharply and emerging markets prospered. The dollar half of the frame has not failed in this sample; the overlay half has, three times out of fourteen, overridden it.

The 2016–18 false start is the case the SR-14 ruling calls instructive, and it is instructive about the frame's limits. Emerging markets beat the developed world strongly through 2016 and 2017 as the dollar fell from its January 2017 high, and the move looked like the beginning of a cycle. It ended in 2018 when the dollar rose again on Fed tightening and U.S. fiscal expansion, and Argentina and Turkey went through the EM loop in the summer. The relative line made a high, not a turn. The lesson the ruling drew — "stay tilted while the dollar-down regime holds, exit on the flip" — is a statement that the frame is a regime classification and not a forecast: it tells a reader which way the wind is blowing, not how long it will blow.

Lead, not just co-movement. The Monthly manual's claim is stronger than co-movement: "dollar moves tend to lead EM equity and credit performance by weeks". The mechanism is the funding one of §9: a stronger dollar raises the local-currency cost of dollar debt and tightens dollar funding before it shows up in earnings, reported flows or ratings. The claim is plausible on mechanism and is not yet measured in this system; the store holds the broad dollar daily since 2006 and the emerging-market ETFs daily, which is enough to test it, and test T2 of Part VII is written to do so.

7. What the Leadership Cycles Have in Common

The International Equities paper found that each U.S.-against-international flip had three ingredients — a currency turn, a relative multiple at an extreme, and a change in which region's earnings were growing — and that "no flip in the sample has occurred on valuation alone". The emerging-market cycles have the same three and a fourth.

A dollar turn. Present at all four completed turns, by the SR-14 record. It is the necessary ingredient in the sample.

A commodity turn. The 1988–94 and 2001–10 emerging runs coincided with rising commodity prices and the 1994–2001 and 2010–24 developed runs with falling or flat ones, most clearly the 2011–2015 commodity bust. The commodity turn and the dollar turn are themselves correlated — a weaker dollar raises dollar commodity prices mechanically and through demand — which is why the Monthly manual counts long-EM, long-commodities and short-dollar as one position.

A Chinese credit turn. From the 2000s onward, the turns in China's credit impulse — the change in new credit as a share of output — lead turns in global cyclicals and in emerging-market earnings, and the 2009 stimulus, the 2015–16 stimulus and the absence of one in 2023 each left a mark on the relative line. The International Equities paper calls the credit impulse "the most reliable short-horizon lead on Chinese activity and, historically, on global cyclicals". This system does not store it (Part VI, Tier 3: no free source).

An extreme in relative valuation. Present at the turns but never sufficient. Emerging markets were cheap relative to developed markets for most of 2012–2024 and stayed cheap; on 30 September 2026 the index traded at 14.9 times trailing earnings against 21.4 for the ACWI, a 30% discount, and at 9.7 times forward earnings against 16.3 (MSCI factsheet, 30 Sep 2026). A discount that persisted for a decade is a starting point for a ten-year projection, not a signal for a twelve-month one.

The fourth ingredient is the one particular to emerging markets: a funding event at the turn that ends a developed-led run's predecessor. The emerging runs ended in funding episodes — 1994 and 1997–98 at the end of the first, 2008 as an interruption of the second (which resumed into 2010 on Chinese stimulus) — and the developed runs ended without one. Emerging leadership is ended by a funding shock; developed leadership is ended by a dollar turn. This asymmetry is the reason Part III spends more time on funding than on growth.

8. Where Today Sits

As of 11 October 2026, the record puts the reader in an unusual configuration, and it is worth stating it as two readings in tension rather than resolving it.

The facts, dated. The relative line made what the SR-14 ruling called a twenty-five-year low with a one-year uptick (Topdown Charts / LSEG, as ruled 24 Sep 2026). Since then emerging markets beat the ACWI by 11.5 points in 2025 and by 10.4 points in 2026 to 30 September (gross USD). The broad dollar fell 7.4% in 2025 and has risen 1.4% in 2026 to 2 October (FRED DTWEXBGS). The emerging-market dollar index — the Federal Reserve's broad index restricted to emerging-market currencies — stood at 129.7 on 2 October 2026, 7% below its 9 April 2025 high (FRED DTWEXEMEGS). The ICE BofA emerging-markets corporate spread was 1.58 points and the high-yield emerging-markets corporate spread 3.34 points on 8 October 2026, against 2.27 and 4.90 at their April 2025 tariff-shock widening (FRED BAMLEMCBPIOAS, BAMLEMHBHYCRPIOAS). Information technology was 43.8% of the index (MSCI, 30 Sep 2026).

The first reading — a new emerging cycle. A dollar turn from a fifteen-year high, an extreme relative valuation, and a relative line turning up from a multi-decade low are three of the four ingredients of §7. Every completed turn in the record had them. On this reading 2026's outperformance without further dollar weakness is the cycle maturing past its first cause, as 2003–07 did.

The second reading — a sector rally in an emerging wrapper. In 2026 the dollar has been flat-to-firmer and emerging markets have still beaten the developed world by ten points. That is one of the three-in-fourteen exceptions of §6, and this time the overlay is not commodities or China but Taiwanese and Korean semiconductors, which are half the index by weight. On this reading the emerging aggregate is, for now, a leveraged expression of the AI capital-expenditure cycle, and it will fall with that cycle whatever the dollar does — and the spread markets, sitting near the tightest of their published three-year window, are pricing an absence of funding stress, not a cycle.

What would decide between them is measurable, and Part VII states it: whether the emerging index ex-North Asia, and the equal-weighted currency basket of the shock screen, are participating (the first reading), or whether the outperformance is confined to Taiwan and Korea (the second). The paper does not settle it; Part VIII prices it, and its twelve-month scenario set gives the two readings separate rows.

PART III — THE MECHANICS

9. The Dollar Channel

The Monthly manual states the channel in four sentences, and they are the paper's foundation: "A very large share of international debt — sovereign, corporate, and bank — is denominated in dollars, including debt owed by entities with no dollar revenue. When the dollar strengthens, those borrowers' liabilities grow in local-currency terms while their income does not. Dollar strength is therefore a global tightening of financial conditions, transmitted independently of what any central bank has done. This is why emerging markets tend to struggle when the dollar rallies, regardless of their own policy settings."

The last clause is the one that makes emerging markets a dollar instrument rather than a collection of countries. A central bank in Jakarta or Mexico City can run an orthodox policy, hold inflation at target and keep a primary surplus, and still watch its financial conditions tighten because the Federal Reserve tightened, or because a risk-off episode sent money into dollars. The policy that matters most for an emerging market's funding is set in Washington, and the variable that transmits it is the exchange rate.

The channel works through four connected balance-sheet effects, each faster than the trade effect that textbook depreciation is supposed to deliver:

The debt-service effect. A borrower earning pesos and owing dollars pays more pesos for the same coupon. If the peso falls 20%, the coupon costs 25% more in pesos. The borrower's income has not changed; its solvency has.

The collateral effect. Banks and markets lend against collateral valued in local currency. When the currency falls, the collateral is worth fewer dollars, lenders cut lines, and borrowers who did nothing wrong lose access to credit. The Bank for International Settlements' work on the "risk-taking channel" of the exchange rate is the formal version: a weaker dollar loosens lending to emerging borrowers by improving their balance sheets as seen from the lender's side, and a stronger dollar tightens it.

The portfolio effect. A foreign holder of local-currency bonds who measures returns in dollars sees losses from the currency on top of any losses from rates, and the holder's mandate — a benchmarked fund with redemptions, a risk limit — often forces a sale into the move. This is the channel that original-sin redux created (§3): the foreign holder's exit pushes the currency further.

The reserve effect. A central bank defending its currency spends dollar reserves. Reserves are finite, the market can count them, and the closer they come to the short-term dollar obligations of the economy, the more a defence invites an attack.

A worked example, with numbers. A Brazilian-style corporate earns 1,000 in local currency a year in operating profit and owes 2,000 in dollar debt at a 7% coupon, with the exchange rate at 5.0 local units per dollar. Its debt is 10,000 in local terms; interest is 700 in local terms; interest cover is 1,000 ÷ 700 = 1.43 times. The dollar rises 20% against the currency, to 6.0. Nothing about the business changes. The debt is now 12,000 in local terms and the interest 840; interest cover falls to 1.19 times, and net debt to operating profit goes from 10× to 12×. If the company's covenant was set at 1.25 times cover, it is now in breach — because of a decision taken by the Federal Open Market Committee. Multiply the company by a corporate sector, and add banks whose dollar funding is short-term, and the arithmetic of a dollar rally becomes a credit event that no domestic indicator anticipated. (Illustrative numbers; the shape is the point.)

Why emerging equities move with leverage to the dollar. The Monthly manual's Tradability section says "EM equity ETFs and hard-currency EM debt both respond strongly to dollar direction, generally with more amplitude than the dollar itself". The amplitude comes from stacking: a dollar holder of emerging equities takes the currency move once directly, then again through the earnings of companies with dollar debt or dollar-priced inputs, then a third time through the multiple, because the discount rate applied to emerging cash flows rises with the funding stress. A 10% dollar rally does not produce a 10% fall; in the episodes the record contains, it has produced more.

The dollar smile, briefly. The Currencies paper's dollar smile — the dollar strengthens when the U.S. outperforms and in global crises, and weakens in the benign middle — maps directly onto emerging markets: both arms of the smile are bad for them, for different reasons (U.S. outperformance draws capital to the U.S.; crisis draws it to dollar safety), and the bottom of the smile is where the emerging runs of §5 took place. The paper does not re-derive the smile; it uses it as the reason "weak dollar" is not one regime for emerging markets but the good one of three.

10. The Commodity Overlay

The overlay is the second-order term in the International Equities paper's sentence, and it is second-order because its sign depends on who is looking.

For exporters, a commodity price rise improves the terms of trade, the current account and the fiscal balance, and the currency appreciates — Brazil, Chile, Peru, South Africa, Indonesia, the Gulf, Colombia. For importers it does the opposite — India, Korea, Taiwan, Thailand, Turkey, the Philippines, and China for energy. The aggregate's response to commodities is therefore a weighted net, and the weights are the index's composition, which is why §4 matters.

The coupling with the dollar. Commodity prices are quoted in dollars, and a weaker dollar raises them mechanically for a holder of any other currency; it also raises global demand by loosening the funding conditions of §9. So the commodity overlay and the dollar channel usually point the same way: weak dollar, firm commodities, strong exporter currencies, emerging leadership. That co-movement is what the Monthly manual's "one position, not three" observes.

When they part, the sign is the information. The Alternative Assets change order states the reading rule for oil, and it generalises: if the oil price and the dollar rise together, it is a supply shock with a haven bid — "maximum pain for energy-importing EM", because the importers pay more dollars for oil while their dollar funding tightens. If they fall together, it is a demand shock, and the exporters suffer while importers get partial relief. The usual negative oil–dollar co-movement is the benign case. The Energy paper's Hormuz episode of 2026 is the live instance of the first: an energy shock that hit importers through two channels at once.

Copper, gold and China. The copper-to-gold ratio is The Twenty-Five's "elegant instrument" for the China tripwire because it "nets the growth signal against the debasement signal in a single line". For emerging markets it is the overlay's best single gauge: copper is construction and grid demand, much of it Chinese; gold is the reserve and debasement bid. A falling ratio with a stable dollar points at Chinese demand rather than at funding, and points the reader at Part V rather than at §11.

What the overlay does not do is protect emerging markets in a funding squeeze. In a dollar-funding crisis commodities fall with everything else, and the exporters' terms-of-trade cushion arrives a quarter later, after the funding damage. The overlay is a cycle variable; the dollar channel is a crisis variable.

11. The Funding Channel and the Basis

The Twenty-Five's Scenario 15 makes a claim that is easy to state and easy to forget in a calm year: "The consistent pattern is that contagion runs through funding channels rather than through trade linkages." Its precedents are 1997–98 for the full sequence and 2018 and 2022 for the contained versions.

Why funding rather than trade. Trade linkages between emerging markets are real but slow — a fall in one country's imports reduces another's exports over quarters — and they are small relative to the size of the moves that crises produce. Funding linkages are fast and large. The same foreign investor holds Mexican and Brazilian bonds in one benchmarked fund; the same international bank lends to Thai and Korean corporates; the same carry-trade capital is funded in yen or dollars against a basket of high-yielders. When that investor, bank or carry book takes a loss in one country, it cuts risk in all of them, because the risk limit and the redemption are at the level of the portfolio, not the country. Two economies with no trade between them are joined at the funder. The Positioning & Flows paper's discretionary holders' mechanical failure modes — degrossing and redemptions — are the micro version of this; here it is the reason "contagion" in emerging markets is a statement about who owns the assets, not about what the countries sell each other.

The cross-currency basis. The Monthly manual calls it "the premium paid to swap another currency into dollars beyond what interest rate differentials imply", and "the cleanest real-time indicator of global dollar funding stress". In a frictionless market covered interest parity holds and the basis is zero: borrowing dollars directly or borrowing another currency and swapping it into dollars costs the same. Since 2008 it has not held. When dollars are scarce — when banks are balance-sheet-constrained and everyone wants dollars at once — the price of obtaining dollars through the swap market rises above the price implied by rates, and the basis turns negative. The Twenty-Five names it as Scenario 15's connector to the funding family: "Cross-currency basis is the instrument that connects this scenario to Family A, and it is the one that would fire first in a disorderly version."

The order matters. The basis fires before the spread and the currency because it measures the funding itself rather than the consequence of funding stress for a particular borrower. A widening basis with spreads and currencies calm is the funding channel loading; a widening basis with spreads and currencies following is the channel transmitting.

The honest gap. This system does not store a cross-currency basis. The Twenty-Five's routing note says so ("Cross-currency basis is not currently instrumented and goes to the build queue, where it earns priority for being shared with scenarios 1 and 2"), the Currencies paper's monitoring tier lists it, the Alternative Assets change order lists it as a chapter structure row, and no free daily source has been found: the cleanest data, as the Monthly manual says, "is on institutional terminals rather than free sources". Part VI carries it as not stored, with two partial substitutes: the Federal Reserve's central-bank liquidity-swap usage (weekly, in the H.4.1 balance sheet — a lagging, policy-response measure, but free and unambiguous when it moves), and the offshore-onshore renminbi gap and CNH HIBOR for the one funding currency whose stress the store can see. Neither is the basis, and the paper does not pretend otherwise.

Swap lines as a firebreak. The Federal Reserve's standing swap lines with the major developed central banks, and its temporary lines with nine others in March 2020 (including Brazil, Korea, Mexico and Singapore), are the reason the basis did not stay wide in 2020. Countries with a line are partly outside the funding channel during a squeeze; countries without one are fully inside it. The FIMA repo facility extends a version of this to any central bank holding Treasuries. Whether a scorecard country has access is a lens the scorecard does not carry, and it is one the reader should hold in mind when reading reserves (§16).

12. The EM Loop

The Currencies paper's sentence is the mechanism's whole statement: "depreciation raises the local-currency value of dollar debt, which forces selling of local assets, which drives further depreciation." It is a feedback loop with four stages, and each stage is a place it can be broken.

  1. The trigger — a dollar rally, a rise in U.S. real rates, a commodity fall for an exporter, a domestic political shock, or contagion from a neighbour through the common funder of §11.
  2. The balance-sheet hit — the dollar debt of §9 grows in local terms; collateral shrinks; foreign holders of local bonds take currency losses.
  3. The forced sale — borrowers sell local assets or buy dollars to hedge or repay; foreign holders redeem; banks cut lines. Every one of these sells the currency.
  4. Further depreciation — which returns the loop to stage 2 with a larger hit.

What breaks the loop. Four things, and their presence is what the vulnerability lenses of Part IV measure:

  • Reserves large enough to meet the short-term dollar demand of stage 3 without exhausting the defence — measured against short-term external debt and in months of imports.
  • A floating exchange rate that lets stage 4 happen early and smaller, rather than late and in one step. The violent episodes in the record are disproportionately pegs and managed rates that held until they could not; the Currencies paper's list of attacks on pegs is the evidence.
  • Local-currency liabilities — debt the sovereign can service in its own currency. This moves the loop's force from the sovereign's solvency to the currency and the foreign holder (original sin redux), which is better for the sovereign and not necessarily for the currency.
  • An external backstop — a swap line, an IMF arrangement, a credible regional facility. The IMF is the backstop of last resort, and the count of its new programmes is the subject of §13.

What makes it worse. A current-account deficit, which means the economy needs a steady inflow of dollars just to stand still; a short maturity profile of external debt; a banking system funded in dollars; and a central bank whose credibility is in question, because then a rate rise to defend the currency reads as panic rather than resolve. A negative real policy rate is the observable form of the last: an economy paying savers less than inflation is inviting them to buy dollars.

A worked example, with numbers. A country carries reserves of \$60 billion against short-term external debt of \$50 billion (a ratio of 1.2), runs a current-account deficit of 4% of a \$400 billion output (\$16 billion a year), and has \$80 billion of local-currency government bonds held by foreigners. A dollar rally takes the currency down 10% in a month. Foreign holders, now 10% down in dollars on their bonds before any rate move, redeem a quarter of their holdings: \$20 billion of currency sold. The central bank sells \$15 billion of reserves to slow the fall — reserves are now \$45 billion against \$50 billion of short-term debt, a ratio of 0.9, below the Greenspan–Guidotti threshold of one. That number is public within weeks, and it is the number that invites the next round. The currency falls another 10%; the dollar debt of the corporate sector grows; the loop is turning. The same country with reserves of \$100 billion meets the same redemption at a ratio that stays above one and does not invite the second round. (Illustrative; the ratios and the threshold are the lenses of Part IV.)

Where the loop shows first. In the hard-currency spread of the weakest credits, in the currency's one-week move relative to its own history (the EM shock screen's 2.5-sigma flag), and in the reserves print — in that order, because the spread is daily, the currency is daily but managed, and reserves are monthly and lagged.

13. The Re-specification Rule: Three IMF Programmes in a Quarter

The Twenty-Five gives Scenario 15 a rule that converts it from tail to condition: "A cluster of three or more IMF programmes in a single quarter converts this from tail to current condition." It is the scenario's only quantitative re-specification trigger, and it needs a counting convention before it can be applied mechanically, because the IMF approves arrangements of very different kinds.

What the paper counts, proposed for the operator to ratify. A programme is a new financing arrangement approved by the IMF Executive Board for a member classified by the IMF as an emerging-market or developing economy, of one of these kinds: a Stand-By Arrangement, an Extended Fund Facility, an Extended or Standby Credit Facility under the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust, or an emergency disbursement under the Rapid Financing Instrument or Rapid Credit Facility. The quarter is the calendar quarter of the Board approval date. Not counted: reviews and augmentations of existing arrangements; non-financing instruments (the Policy Coordination and Policy Support Instruments); stand-alone Resilience and Sustainability Facility arrangements, which finance climate and pandemic preparedness rather than a balance-of-payments need; and new or renewed precautionary lines — the Flexible Credit Line, the Precautionary and Liquidity Line, the Short-term Liquidity Line — which are granted to strong-fundamentals countries as insurance and whose renewal is not a stress signal.

Why this convention. The rule exists to detect a wave of countries losing market access at the same time, which is the signature of the funding channel acting on many borrowers at once. A new arrangement is the evidence of lost access; a review is not; a precautionary line is evidence of the opposite. Counting emergency instruments is a choice: they fired in large numbers in the second quarter of 2020, when dozens of members drew on them in weeks, and that was a correct firing — it was the funding channel acting on the frontier as a group, even though the developed-market swap lines contained it within weeks.

What firing means. It does not mean a crisis in the large emerging markets of the scorecard. Most IMF programmes are frontier and small emerging economies — the 2022–23 wave included Sri Lanka, Zambia, Ghana, Pakistan and Egypt, none of which is in the scorecard — and a wave confined to the frontier can coexist with tight spreads in the index's large members. Firing means that the scenario's mechanism is operating on the weakest borrowers, that the funding channel is active, and that the reader should look up the vulnerability table for the scorecard countries that share the weak borrowers' profile (a large current-account deficit, reserves below short-term debt, a negative real rate). In the thesis register, it moves Scenario 15 from the tail watch's Tier C to a current condition named in the Monthly's global pillar and in the Quarterly's chapter, and it re-opens the twelve-month scenario probabilities of Part VIII out of cycle.

Where the count comes from. The IMF publishes its lending arrangements and each Board approval as a press release. The count is not stored by any writer; the Quarterly's scan (the change order's §8) can collect it as a sourced figure, and Part VI carries it as not stored with that as the candidate.

PART IV — THE VULNERABILITY FRAMEWORK

15. Why Lenses, Not a Score

Every emerging-market strategist carries a vulnerability table, and most of them sum it. The sum is attractive — one number per country, a ranking, a chart — and it is the wrong object for two reasons.

The binding lens differs by crisis. The 1997 Asian crises were short-term external debt against reserves, in countries with fiscal surpluses and low inflation; a composite that gave fiscal balance and inflation two of its seven weights scored Korea and Thailand as healthy. The 2018 Turkish and Argentine episodes were current-account deficits financed short-term, plus in Turkey's case a deeply negative real policy rate. The 2013 taper tantrum hit the large current-account deficits and, as later work found, the large and liquid markets regardless of fundamentals. A composite averages the lens that binds with six that do not, and it does so at exactly the moment the binding lens is the only one that matters.

The architecture forbids it. §26.8's moratorium holds: no new composite. The Alternative Assets change order states the scorecard's design accordingly — "Free, annual-to-quarterly, no composite: a table of lenses" — and the scorecard's configuration header repeats it: "A table of lenses -- country x lens, each cell the latest value, its own ten-year percentile and the change over the quarter, ranked per column -- and NEVER a composite."

What the table does instead is rank within each lens. A country that is first (most vulnerable) on reserves against short-term debt and middling on everything else is a different case from a country that is fourth on every lens, and the reader is meant to see that difference rather than have it summed away. Each cell also carries its own ten-year percentile, so that a country can be read against its own history as well as against its peers — a current-account deficit of 3% is alarming for Korea and ordinary for India.

16. The Eight Lenses

The scorecard carries eight lenses in its configured column order. The commissioning order named seven measures; the scorecard splits debt into external and government, which is the right split, because the two cause different crises.

Current account, percent of GDP (em.weo.ca_gdp.<iso3>; IMF WEO; annual; worse is lower). The flow of dollars an economy needs from abroad each year to stand still. A deficit is not itself a crisis — it is the counterpart of foreign investment, and fast-growing economies run them — but it is the variable that decides whether a sudden stop of capital inflows forces an immediate adjustment. It is the loop's fuel: a deficit country in a dollar squeeze must find the dollars or cut imports.

Reserves in months of imports (em.lens.reserves_import_months.<iso3>; IMF reserves over twelve months' average goods imports; monthly; worse is lower). The oldest adequacy rule, from an era when crises were trade crises: three months was the conventional floor. It measures how long the economy could pay for imports if every inflow stopped. It understates vulnerability in a capital-account crisis, where the dollars leave through the financial account, not the trade account.

Reserves against short-term external debt (em.lens.reserves_st_debt.<iso3>; IMF reserves over World Bank QEDS short-term external debt; quarterly; worse is lower). The Greenspan–Guidotti ratio: reserves should cover the external debt falling due within a year, so that the economy could meet a year's maturities with no rollover at all. Below one is the conventional warning. It is the lens built for capital-account crises, and §18 says why it is the one with the best record.

External debt, percent of GDP (em.lens.ext_debt_gdp.<iso3>; QEDS gross external debt over WEO GDP; quarterly; worse is higher). The size of the stock the loop can act on. It does not distinguish currency or maturity — a long-dated local-currency stock held abroad is far less dangerous than short-dated dollar debt — and it should be read beside the short-term ratio, never alone.

Real policy rate (em.lens.real_policy_rate.<iso3>; the BIS policy rate less IMF CPI inflation, year on year; monthly; worse is lower). Whether the central bank pays savers to hold the currency. A deeply negative real rate is an invitation to buy dollars, and it is the most direct observable of a central bank under fiscal or political pressure. A very high real rate is not a clean bill of health either — it can mean a central bank fighting a recent credibility loss — but in the ranking, lower is more vulnerable.

Inflation against target (em.lens.inflation_gap.<iso3>; IMF CPI inflation less the configured target; monthly; worse is higher). Credibility, measured. A central bank persistently above its own target is one whose promises the currency market discounts. Four of the thirteen have no formal target (Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Malaysia, Taiwan), and their cells print "not tracked: no inflation target" rather than a gap against an invented one.

Fiscal balance, percent of GDP (em.weo.fiscal_gdp.<iso3>; WEO general government net lending; annual; worse is lower). The government's own borrowing need, and therefore the supply of local debt that foreigners and banks must absorb. Fiscal crises in emerging markets are mostly domestic-debt crises — a sovereign that cannot sell its bonds at home — and they become currency crises when the central bank is pressed to finance the deficit.

Government debt, percent of GDP (em.weo.debt_gdp.<iso3>; WEO general government gross debt; annual; worse is higher). The stock behind the fiscal flow. High government debt in local currency held domestically can persist for decades (the Japanese case the Rates & Liquidity paper discusses); high government debt in foreign currency or held by foreigners cannot. The lens does not distinguish; the reader must.

18. What Each Lens Has and Has Not Predicted

The early-warning literature on emerging-market crises is large, and its findings are more modest than its volume. Three general results first, then the lenses one at a time.

The general results. First, the lenses rank vulnerability; they do not time crises. The timing comes from outside — the dollar, U.S. rates, a commodity break, a neighbour's crisis — and the lenses say who breaks when it comes. Second, every lens has a high false-alarm rate on its own: Kaminsky, Lizondo and Reinhart's signals approach found that even the best single indicators — the real exchange rate's overvaluation, export weakness, broad money relative to reserves — issued many more warnings than crises followed (currency crises in twenty developing and industrial countries, 1970–1995; published 1998). Third, the lenses that measure the short-term dollar position have the best record in capital-account crises, and the lenses that measure the fiscal and inflation position the worst.

Reserves against short-term external debt — the best record. In the 1997–98 Asian crises the ratio of short-term external debt to reserves separated the countries that suffered severe reversals from those that did not more cleanly than any other single variable (Rodrik and Velasco, 1999; the 1997–98 crisis sample), and the Greenspan–Guidotti threshold of one was proposed in 1999 on that evidence. Korea, Thailand and Indonesia all had short-term debt well above reserves before 1997. What it has not predicted: the ratio says nothing about crises driven by domestic residents buying dollars — capital flight by locals is not "short-term external debt" — which is how several Latin American episodes developed, and it is blind to corporate dollar debt booked offshore by subsidiaries.

Current account — real but conditional. Large current-account deficits were present in Mexico 1994, Thailand 1996 (about 8% of GDP) and Turkey and Argentina 2018, and the 2013 taper tantrum hit the "Fragile Five" — Brazil, India, Indonesia, Turkey and South Africa, all with sizeable deficits — hardest among the large emerging markets (the 2013 episode; the label is a sell-side coinage of that summer). What it has not predicted: Frankel and Rose's study of currency crashes found the current account was not a significant predictor across their broad sample (105 developing countries, 1971–1992; published 1996), and Eichengreen and Gupta's study of the taper tantrum found that the size and liquidity of a country's financial market explained more of the selling than its fundamentals did (May–August 2013; published 2014) — the funding channel hitting what was easiest to sell. The current account tells the reader who must find dollars; it does not say who will be sold first.

Reserves in months of imports — the weakest record in modern crises. Its three-month floor was built for the trade-financing crises of the Bretton Woods era. Countries well above it have suffered capital-account crises, because the reserves that cover a year of imports may not cover a quarter of portfolio outflows. It remains useful for the frontier and for commodity importers in a terms-of-trade shock, and as a sanity check beside the short-term ratio.

External debt — the stock that the loop multiplies, with a composition problem. High external debt is a precondition of the loop's worst form, and the Argentine 2001 default is the textbook instance. What it has not predicted: the level alone misclassifies; countries with high external debt that is long-dated and partly local-currency have carried it through dollar cycles, and the more informative quantity is the short-term and dollar-denominated part — which is why the scorecard pairs it with the short-term ratio.

Real policy rate — a sharp signal in the cases it fits. Turkey in 2018 and again in 2021, with real policy rates driven deeply negative under political pressure on the central bank, went through the loop on both occasions (the two Turkish episodes; the cases are the reserved Turning Points paper's). A negative real rate is the observable form of a central bank that has lost the argument. What it has not predicted: in the 2020–22 global inflation surge real rates were negative almost everywhere, developed and emerging, and the lens's cross-sectional ranking — who is most negative — carried more information than its sign. Brazil's very high positive real rate in 2025–26 (+9.8 points in August 2026, as the scorecard's trial run read it) is the opposite case: a lens reading that signals defence, not complacency.

Inflation against target — a credibility lens that lags. Persistent overshoots preceded several currency collapses, but the lens moves after the currency in most episodes — depreciation raises inflation — and it reads the consequence as often as the cause. Its best use is the trend: a central bank whose overshoot is widening while its real rate falls is the configuration to notice.

Fiscal balance and government debt — the lenses that missed 1997. The Asian crisis countries had balanced budgets or surpluses and low public debt, and a fiscal lens rated them healthy on the eve of the worst crisis in the asset class's history. Fiscal lenses predict domestic-debt and monetisation crises — Brazil's 2015 downgrade and recession, Argentina's repeated episodes — well, and capital-account crises poorly. In the current decade they matter more than they used to, because the large emerging markets now borrow mostly in local currency at home, and a domestic-debt crisis is the form their sovereign stress would now most likely take.

What the table is for, then. Not prediction of when; ranking of who. In a dollar squeeze the countries at the vulnerable end of the reserves-to-short-term-debt and current-account columns, with a negative or falling real rate, are the ones the loop of §12 reaches first. Part VII's test T6 checks whether that ranking has held in the system's own data.

PART V — CHINA AS THE SPECIAL CASE

19. Three Markets, Two Economies — By Reference

China has a part of its own in the International Equities paper — Chapter 10, "China: three markets, two economies, and whether winning the industry means earning a return" — and this paper does not rewrite it. The reader who needs the central fact (two decades of high-single-digit real growth and roughly nothing earned by a dollar holder of the offshore index over its last decade), the four explanations (dilution, state capital allocation, the listed universe not being the growth economy, a rising risk premium), the technology record sector by sector, the trap ("winning the industry is not the same as earning a return"), the headwinds, the nine observables of 10.6 and the four ten-year scenarios of 10.7 should read Chapter 10 there.

Three of its sentences carry directly into the emerging-markets frame, and they are quoted here so that the Quarterly's China sub-chapter can cite one place:

  • Capacity discipline — "capacity utilization, module and cell price indices, and the survival rate of subscale manufacturers" — is "the single highest-value thing to monitor in the Chinese equity story over the next ten years".

20. China Inside the Emerging Aggregate

What this paper adds is China's role as a component and a driver of the aggregate, which is different from its role as a market.

As a component, China was 19.8% of the MSCI EM index on 30 September 2026, down from over two-fifths at its late-2020 peak (MSCI; approximate), and the index ex-China (yfinance.mkt_emxc) is now the common way to separate the China decision from the emerging-markets decision. The SR-14 ruling asks for "EM ex-China alongside" for this reason: a reader who holds the aggregate holds a China weight set by an index committee, which is a China decision nobody made.

As a driver, China reaches the rest of emerging markets through three of the mechanisms of Part III at once. Through the commodity overlay, because Chinese construction and grid investment are the marginal buyer of industrial metals and a large buyer of energy (§10). Through the currency, because the renminbi is the anchor of the Asian currency bloc and a weaker renminbi presses Korea, Taiwan, Thailand and Malaysia to follow or lose competitiveness. And through goods prices, because Chinese export prices set the price of manufactures that other emerging exporters compete with.

The renminbi's management. The People's Bank sets a daily central parity (cfets.cny_fix) around which onshore USD/CNY may trade within 2%; offshore CNH trades freely in Hong Kong (cnh.usdcnh, branch aq-3-series-1). The gap between the two, and between each and the fix (calc.cnh_cny_bp, calc.cny_fix_gap_bp, calc.cnh_fix_gap_bp, branch aq-3-series-1), is the market's measure of pressure on the managed rate. The Currencies paper's §25 is the reference on the yuan's structural position; what matters here is the devaluation tell of the SR-4 ruling, which is the one China signal with a direct emerging-markets consequence: "the fix stops leaning against the previous close, the CNH–CNY gap widens with CNH weaker, and state banks stop selling dollars" (the third leg a by-hand field, manual.state_bank_dollar_selling). The SR-14 ruling makes the tell the kill switch for the emerging-markets tilt, with August 2015 as the precedent: a deliberate devaluation is the single fastest route by which China becomes everyone's problem.

The offshore funding currency. The renminbi is also a funding currency, and the People's Bank controls offshore CNH liquidity directly. The SR-4 ruling records that "overnight CNH HIBOR reached 66% in January 2016" (hktma.cnh_hibor_on), which is the form an offshore squeeze takes: abrupt and policy-driven. For the funding channel of §11, CNH HIBOR and the CNH–CNY gap are the one corner of emerging-market funding the store can see daily.

21. The Three-Channel China Event

The Twenty-Five's Scenario 12 — "China property or LGFV event" — earns its slot because one event transmits through three channels: it "exports deflation through goods prices, as excess capacity is dumped into export markets at falling prices", it "applies currency pressure, since the policy response involves easing into capital-flight risk", and it "delivers a commodity demand shock, because construction is the marginal buyer of industrial metals". The scenario's own warning is the reason the Quarterly reads it carefully: "The three channels point in different directions for different assets, which is why the scenario is frequently misread as unambiguously deflationary."

For emerging markets the three channels sort the countries:

Channel Who is hurt Who is relieved
Goods deflation exported Emerging manufacturers competing with Chinese exports — Korea, Taiwan, Thailand, Mexico, Indonesia, India in specific sectors Importers of manufactures; consumers everywhere
Renminbi pressure The Asian currency bloc, which follows the renminbi; any emerging market where a weaker renminbi triggers the funding channel Few; a disorderly renminbi move is, in the scenario's words, "destabilizing for everything else"
Commodity demand shock Metal exporters — Chile, Peru, Brazil, South Africa, Indonesia; the copper-to-gold ratio Commodity importers through cheaper inputs

The scenario names its first movers — "renminbi, copper-to-gold ratio, Chinese export prices" — and its alias: "Aliasing — with 15, since a China event and an emerging-market dollar squeeze share the strong-dollar signature." That aliasing is the practical difficulty. A reader who sees a stronger dollar, weaker Asian currencies and wider emerging spreads cannot tell from those three alone whether the cause is a China event or a dollar squeeze; the discriminators are the copper-to-gold ratio (falls in a China event, not necessarily in a dollar squeeze), the CNH–CNY gap and the fix (move first in a China event), and the cross-currency basis (moves first in a dollar squeeze). The re-specification trigger is the scenario's own: "A disorderly renminbi move of sufficient size would convert this from a credit scenario into a currency-regime scenario requiring its own scoping."

22. The Reading Rules the Quarterly Uses

The Quarterly's China sub-chapter reads Chinese policy, and Chinese policy is the hardest input in the library to read honestly. The Alternative Assets change order adopts three rules from the external research architecture it compared against, and the operator ruled a fourth. This paper restates them as the frame the chapter reads, because a rule stated in a change order is a rule a future session can miss.

The policy ladder: announced → authorised → funded → implemented. Every policy measure is placed on the ladder, "and only the last two count as demand". A Politburo statement that property will be "stabilised" is announced. A State Council document setting out a programme is authorised. A budget line, a special bond quota or a policy-bank lending quota is funded. Disbursement, started projects and purchased housing stock are implemented. The record of Chinese stimulus since 2022 is a long list of measures announced and authorised that never reached funded, and a reader who counts announcements as demand will be wrong in the same direction every time. For emerging markets the rung matters most for the commodity overlay: copper and iron ore respond to announcements and then give back the move when the funding does not follow.

Authoritative releases against commentary. The authoritative sources — the National Bureau of Statistics, the People's Bank, the Ministry of Finance, the State Administration of Foreign Exchange, the customs administration and the State Council — are distinguished from commentary and rumour, "with the original-language text preserved where material". Commentary includes foreign sell-side readings of a statement, which are often the fastest English source and the least reliable on what was actually said.

The local-government and financial-stability map. The local-government and LGFV position (explicit and off-budget debt, refinancing swaps, land-revenue dependence, regional variation) and financial stability (banks, trusts and wealth products, developer and local exposures, deposit rates, household saving) are read once a quarter from the IMF's Article IV consultation and its local-debt work, as the loss-bearer map behind the property series. This is the map The Twenty-Five's Scenario 12 needs to say who would bear a property or LGFV loss, and it changes slowly enough that a quarterly read is the right cadence.

The model writes no political judgement. Ruled 10 October 2026 (decision 4): the political environment enters from sourced reporting through the quarterly scan and the voices register, from a hand-seeded calendar of the Party and State (plenums, Politburo meetings, the National People's Congress, the Central Economic Work Conference), and from an operator's political note each quarter, written by hand and stored as an operator-tier voices row, "so that nuance the sourced reporting cannot carry … enters the chapter through the one hand the rules trust". The chapter quotes the note and the stored reporting; it does not generate a reading of its own. This paper follows the same rule: it makes no claim about Chinese politics beyond what its sources state.

PART VII — FALSIFICATION TESTS

State in advance what would disconfirm each claim, then check honestly. Each test below is written to be encoded verbatim by the Quarterly's chapter, which prints one line per test each quarter — holds, strained or failed, with the evidence. Each names its series by registry key; where a series is not yet stored, the test says so and stays not yet testable rather than being graded on a proxy.

T1 — "Emerging markets in aggregate are a dollar-cycle instrument." Falsified if the ratio of the MSCI Emerging Markets Index to the MSCI World Index, both in dollars (manual.msci_em_usd ÷ manual.msci_world_usd), completes a turn — a move of 20% or more from its prior extreme, held for twelve months — with no turn in the broad trade-weighted dollar (fred.dxy) of 8% or more from its own extreme within twelve months either side. One such turn strains the claim; two in the record fail it.

T2 — "Dollar moves lead EM equity and credit by weeks." Falsified if, over the trailing three years of weekly data, the cross-correlation between weekly changes in the broad dollar (fred.dxy) and weekly changes in the EM-against-DM ratio (yfinance.mkt_eem ÷ yfinance.mkt_acwx), and separately in the EM high-yield corporate spread (fred.em_hy_oas), is largest at a lag of zero or with the EM series leading, at two consecutive annual reviews. The spread leg is not yet testable until c1-move-screen merges and three years of the series are stored.

T3 — "Long EM equity, long commodities and short dollars is one position, not three." Falsified if the average pairwise correlation of weekly returns among EM equity (yfinance.mkt_eem), commodities (yfinance.mkt_gunr) and the inverse of the broad dollar (−fred.dxy) over a rolling 156 weeks falls below 0.3 and stays there for two consecutive quarters.

T4 — "Contagion runs through funding channels, not trade." Falsified if, in a rolling five-year window, two or more single-country shocks pass to the basket under the SR-46 contagion rule (screen.em_contagion) while the funding indicators did not move first — the EM high-yield spread (fred.em_hy_oas) widened by less than 50 basis points and the broad dollar (fred.dxy) rose by less than 1% over the five sessions before the passage. Where the cross-currency basis is stored, it joins the funding indicators.

T5 — "The EM loop reinforces itself in an economy with heavy external debt." Falsified if, over a rolling five-year window, more than half of the depreciations of 15% or more against the dollar within three months among the thirteen scorecard countries whose external debt exceeds 40% of GDP (em.lens.ext_debt_gdp.<iso3>) are followed by a recovery of at least half the move within the next three months without an IMF arrangement, a swap line or capital controls — that is, if the loop's second turn usually fails to arrive on its own.

T6 — "The vulnerability lenses rank who breaks in a dollar squeeze." Falsified if, in two consecutive dollar-squeeze episodes — the broad dollar (fred.dxy) up 8% or more within six months — the scorecard countries in the most vulnerable third on reserves against short-term external debt (em.lens.reserves_st_debt.<iso3>) and on the current account (em.weo.ca_gdp.<iso3>), read at the episode's start, do not depreciate more on average against the dollar than the least vulnerable third.

T7 — "The aggregate is a dollar instrument with a commodity overlay, not a semiconductor instrument." Falsified if, over four consecutive quarters, the rolling 52-week correlation of the EM-against-DM ratio (yfinance.mkt_eem ÷ yfinance.mkt_acwx) with Taiwan and Korea's average return (yfinance.mkt_ewt, yfinance.mkt_ewy) relative to the broad basket — the equal-weighted seven non-North-Asian, non-China country ETFs of the shock screen, as §24 defines it — is higher than its correlation with the inverse of the broad dollar (−fred.dxy). If T7 fails, the frame's overlay half is re-specified in writing as a semiconductor overlay, and the twelve-month scenario set is re-weighted toward the rows that depend on the AI capital cycle.

T8 — "A deliberate renminbi devaluation is an emerging-markets event, not a China event." Falsified if, in a month in which the devaluation tell's two market legs fire together (calc.cny_fix_gap_bp and calc.cnh_cny_bp) and USD/CNY (fred.usd_cny) rises by 3% or more, the EM currency basket ex-China — the H.10 and screen rows — moves by less than 1% in the same direction over the following month.

Eight tests. T1, T3 and T6 test the frame's large claims on long data and change verdict rarely; T2, T4 and T7 test its timing and its composition and can move in a year; T5 and T8 test the two mechanisms with the fewest episodes and will often read not yet tested, which is a verdict, not an omission.

25. The Ten-Year Prior

The ten-year question is whether emerging markets beat developed markets in dollars over the decade from 1 October 2026. The International Equities paper's building blocks (its Chapter 15.2) put an emerging-markets central estimate at roughly 7–9.5% a year in nominal dollars against roughly 6–7% for U.S. large caps and 7.5–9% for developed international (inferred; September 2026), which, with the World index about two-thirds American, leaves emerging markets a central advantage of about a point a year — and a distribution far wider than the advantage.

Aggregating: the probability that emerging markets beat the World over the next ten years is roughly even — 50 to 55% — with a central advantage near one point a year and a fifteen-percent tail that is not a valuation risk. The tail deserves its sentence. On 30 September 2026 Taiwan and China were together 48.7% of the index; the International Equities paper gives its China rupture scenario about 15% over ten years and says it "cannot be sized by valuation". A holder of the aggregate holds that tail at index weight, which is a decision about Taiwan and China that the index committee made, and the International Equities paper's response to a downside that includes zero — "a small position or none, not a wider discount rate" — applies to the aggregate's North Asian half with the same force it applies to China alone.

What would falsify each. Dollar-down decade is falsified by the real broad dollar (fred.real_dollar_broad) making a new high after 2027. Convergence is falsified by the valuation discount of §7 widening further over three years while the dollar falls — the configuration that says the discount is earned. Dollar strength persists is falsified by a completed relative-line turn in emerging markets' favour (test T1's definition) that holds for three years. North Asia rupture is monitored by The Twenty-Five's Taiwan ladder and the International Equities paper's foreign-participation observables, not by anything in this paper.

These enter the thesis register as four hypotheses with a ten-year horizon and an annual review.

APPENDICES

Appendix A: Glossary

Contagion rule — The SR-46 ruling's test that a single-country shock becomes a system matter only if it passes to the EM currency basket or EM spreads within five sessions.

Credit impulse — The change in new credit as a share of output; for China, total social financing relative to output. Not stored.

Cross-currency basis — The premium paid to obtain dollars through the FX swap market beyond what interest differentials imply; negative when dollars are scarce. Not stored.

Devaluation tell — The SR-4 ruling's three-legged signal of a deliberate renminbi devaluation: the fix stops leaning against the previous close, the CNH–CNY gap widens with CNH weaker, and state banks stop selling dollars.

Dollar smile — The Currencies paper's description of the dollar strengthening both when the U.S. outperforms and in global crises, and weakening in the benign middle.

EM loop — The Currencies paper's mechanism: depreciation raises the local-currency value of dollar debt, which forces selling of local assets, which drives further depreciation.

EMBI, CEMBI, GBI-EM — J.P. Morgan's benchmark indices for emerging-market hard-currency sovereign debt, hard-currency corporate debt and local-currency government debt. Proprietary.

FIMA repo facility — The Federal Reserve's standing facility that lets foreign central banks borrow dollars against their Treasury holdings.

Greenspan–Guidotti rule — Reserves should at least equal external debt maturing within one year.

Hard-currency debt — Bonds issued by an emerging borrower in a foreign currency, usually dollars, under foreign law.

LGFV — Local-government financing vehicle; the off-budget borrowers through which Chinese local governments funded infrastructure, largely against land revenue.

Original sin — The inability of a country to borrow abroad in its own currency; original sin redux is the later observation that when foreigners do hold local-currency debt, the currency risk moves to them and the loop runs through their exit.

Policy ladder — Announced → authorised → funded → implemented; only the last two count as demand.

Re-specification rule — The Twenty-Five's trigger that converts Scenario 15 from tail to current condition: three or more IMF programmes in a quarter.

Swap line — A standing or temporary arrangement under which a central bank can obtain dollars from the Federal Reserve against its own currency.

Closing Note

Emerging markets reward the reader who keeps two things apart that the phrase runs together: an index and a set of countries. The index is a construction that, on 30 September 2026, was half North Asian technology; the countries are economies whose funding is set in Washington and whose currencies carry the strain when it tightens. The four sentences this paper started from are about the second, and they have held in the record — the dollar turns with the cycle, contagion travels through funders rather than through trade, and depreciation feeds on dollar debt. The thing to watch in the next few years is whether they still describe the first.

The framework is built to make that question answerable: the lenses rank who breaks without pretending to say when; the monitoring tiers name what the system can see and, as plainly, what it cannot — the basis, the credit impulse, the flows; and the falsification tests say in advance what would make the frame wrong. If one idea survives from this paper, it should be §11's: in emerging markets, the countries are joined at the funder, and the funding channel moves first.
