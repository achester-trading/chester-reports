Purpose and How to Use This Document

Credit is where cycles turn first and where they are hardest to see. This paper exists to make both halves of that sentence operational: to explain why credit leads, to specify the instruments that read the lead, and to map where the current cycle's stress has migrated — out of the public bond market, where it would be visible in a spread series, and into liability-management exercises and privately-marked loans, where it is not.

The paper carries two emphases, agreed in advance. Private credit and the bifurcation thesis — the migration of corporate lending into a \$2 trillion market with quarterly, manager-determined marks, and the argument that aggregate returns from that market can look fine while its tail deteriorates — gets the longest Part. Corporate credit as a cycle-timing tool — the lead-lag relationship between credit and equities, and the specific signals that exploit it — organises Part II and the whole of Part VI. The Top & Bottom report already runs an HY-acceleration overlay and carries the bifurcation thesis as a working hypothesis; this paper is where both get their full derivation.

Three principles govern the writing. The paper treats the lending-standards cycle, not the spread cycle, as the underlying object — spreads are the last thing to move, and a framework built on them is a framework that reads the news after it happens. It treats dispersion as the signal and level as the noise, because the 2020s' default cycle is happening inside averages that look benign. And it is explicit about what is untested: private credit at its current scale and structure has not been through a full cycle, and the paper refuses to pretend the historical record for bank-syndicated loans transfers cleanly.

Boundaries. Rates & Liquidity owns the price of money and the Treasury–Fed plumbing; this paper takes the funding channel as given and references it — but Section 29 trades the curve directly, because the long yield is the common bus of the whole book and nothing else in the library expresses a view on it. Currencies owns dollar-funding stress. The Top & Bottom report owns the implementation of the credit overlays in the scorecard. Volatility owns credit vol as a cross-asset signal. This paper owns the borrower's ability to pay, the lender's willingness to lend, and the instruments that price both.

Timing note. Written on 31 August 2026 in a configuration that is the paper's own thesis in miniature. The ICE BofA high-yield index option-adjusted spread sits at 269 basis points, in the richest decile of its history against a long-run median near 450; investment grade at 81; BBB at 100. The leveraged-loan payment default rate is 0.93% by amount — yet liability-management exercises account for roughly two-thirds of default activity by issuer count, and the dual-track default rate that includes them runs several points higher. The distress ratio has ticked up to 6.89%. Private credit has just been through its first genuine liquidity event: record redemption requests of 12.1% of net assets from non-traded BDCs in the first quarter, with one manager receiving requests for 40.7% of its technology-focused vehicles while reporting non-accruals of 0.6% — a gap between what investors believe and what marks say that is the bifurcation problem in a single number. The Fed is at 3.50–3.75% and debating hikes, which means the floating-rate cost shock that leveraged borrowers have carried since 2022 is not being relieved. The July Senior Loan Officer survey reported commercial-and-industrial standards easier than their post-2005 midpoint while every other category sits at the tight end of its range. That is a late-cycle configuration: the loosest standards in the segment with the most leverage.

PART I — THE ANALYTICAL FRAME

1. What a Credit Spread Actually Prices

A credit spread is the additional yield a borrower pays over the risk-free rate for the same maturity. It is routinely described as "the price of default risk," and that description is wrong enough to cause systematic error.

A spread decomposes into at least four components, and the proportions shift across the cycle.

Expected loss. The probability of default multiplied by the loss given default. For investment-grade credit this is a small fraction of the spread — historical five-year cumulative default rates for BBB issuers run near 1.5%, implying expected annual losses of perhaps 15–20 basis points against spreads of 100. For high yield it is larger but still a minority of the spread in most years.

Risk premium. Compensation for bearing the variance of losses, which is negatively skewed and correlated with everything else going wrong. This is the credit analogue of the variance risk premium that Volatility describes, and it is the largest component of the spread in normal times.

Liquidity premium. Compensation for the difficulty of selling. It is small in calm markets and can become the entire spread in stress, when bids vanish. The March 2020 widening of the high-yield spread to 1,087 basis points was mostly liquidity; expected losses had not moved that far.

Technical and structural components. Supply and demand for the specific paper — issuance calendars, index inclusion, insurance-company demand for long-dated investment grade, CLO demand for loans. These can dominate for extended periods and are the reason the same borrower's bond and loan can trade at different implied default probabilities.

Why the decomposition matters for timing. A spread that tightens because the liquidity premium is compressing (a benign technical) and a spread that tightens because expected losses are falling (a fundamental) are the same number and different situations. A spread that widens because risk premium is expanding (fear) and one that widens because expected losses are rising (deterioration) call for opposite responses. The level tells you none of this. The composition — inferred from dispersion across ratings, from the relationship between spreads and default forecasts, and from liquidity measures — is where the information is. The current 269 basis points is, on this decomposition, almost entirely risk premium and technical: expected losses are low, liquidity is deep, and demand for yield has compressed the premium to a level that leaves little room for either to change.

2. The Lending-Standards Cycle

The credit cycle is conventionally drawn as a spread cycle — spreads tight at the top, wide at the bottom. It is more accurately drawn as a lending-standards cycle, of which spreads are the lagging expression.

Minsky's sequence as an underwriting progression. Hyman Minsky described three financing postures. Hedge borrowers can service both interest and principal from cash flow. Speculative borrowers can service interest but must refinance principal. Ponzi borrowers can service neither and depend on rising asset values or new lending to stay current. His argument was that stability itself drives the migration from hedge to speculative to Ponzi, because a long calm period makes lenders progressively more willing to extend credit on progressively weaker terms.

That migration is observable, and it is observable before it appears in spreads or defaults. It appears in covenant quality — the share of loans issued without maintenance covenants rose from under 20% in 2010 to above 90% by 2019 and has stayed there, which is lenders surrendering their early-warning rights. It appears in structural terms — the erosion of restrictions on asset transfers, on priming debt, on incremental facilities, which is what made the liability-management exercises of Section 8 possible. It appears in payment-in-kind provisions — the share of private-credit loans allowing interest to be paid in additional debt rather than cash rose from roughly 6% to 10% between early 2022 and early 2026, which is lenders agreeing in advance to be paid later. And it appears in bank survey data — the Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, which asks lenders directly whether they are tightening or loosening, and which has led spreads and defaults by two to four quarters in every cycle since it began.

The mechanism of the lag. A borrower who was underwritten on weak terms in year one does not default in year one. The loan performs; the covenants that would have flagged deterioration were never included; the PIK toggle defers the cash test. The default arrives in year three or four, when the loan matures and must be refinanced in a market that has changed. Spreads widen when the defaults arrive. Standards loosened three years earlier, and the loosening was visible at the time to anyone reading the terms rather than the prices.

The 2026 reading. The July 2026 SLOOS special question — asking banks to place their current standards within the range since 2005 — produced a striking answer: C&I standards are easier than their historical midpoint, while every other category (CRE, residential, consumer) sits at the tighter end. Banks had tightened C&I standards in the fourth quarter of 2025 and the first quarter of 2026, then held them unchanged in the second. The category with the most leverage and the most direct exposure to a cost-of-capital shock has the loosest standards relative to history. In the framework of this section, that is the observation that matters, and the 269-basis-point spread is the lagging indicator of a cycle whose leading indicator is already flagging.

3. Why Credit Leads Equities — and the Timing Tool That Follows

Credit turns before equities. The lead is not a curiosity; it follows from the structure of the claims, and understanding why it exists is what makes it usable.

The lender's payoff is asymmetric. A lender's best outcome is repayment at par plus coupon. A lender's worst outcome is total loss. The distribution is capped on the upside and open on the downside. An equity holder's payoff is the reverse: capped loss, unbounded gain. This asymmetry means the lender and the equity holder respond to the same information differently. News that raises the probability of a bad outcome by a small amount is a small negative for equity — one scenario among many, offset by the upside scenarios — and a large negative for the lender, for whom the bad outcome is the only one that matters. The lender is the first to say no, because the lender has nothing to gain from saying yes to a marginal credit.

The refinancing channel enforces it. Equity is permanent capital; debt matures. A firm whose prospects have deteriorated does not need to raise equity, but it must refinance its debt. The refinancing is the moment at which the lender's changed assessment becomes binding — the loan is not rolled, or is rolled on terms that consume the equity's cash flow. Credit conditions therefore transmit to equity fundamentals with a lag equal to the maturity schedule, and the credit market prices the transmission before it occurs.

The evidence. High-yield spreads began widening in mid-2007, fourteen months before the equity market's September 2008 collapse. They began widening in the second half of 2014 (led by energy), eighteen months before the early-2016 equity low. In 2022, the first sustained widening came in January, coincident with the equity top rather than ahead of it — but the dispersion (CCC versus BB) had been widening since late 2021. The lead is not constant and it is not always at the index level, which is why Part VI's timing toolkit uses acceleration and dispersion rather than level.

The timing tool. If credit leads and the lead lives in the second derivative and in the distribution rather than in the level, then the operational signals are: the rate of change of spreads (acceleration — a spread that widens 50 basis points from 270 carries more information than one sitting at 500); the dispersion across ratings and across public and private marks (bifurcation — the tail deteriorating while the average holds); the divergence between credit and equities (credit widening while equities make highs — the lender saying no while the equity holder says yes); and the standards series that leads all three. The Top & Bottom overlay implements the first; this paper adds the other three and specifies the combination.

4. The Taxonomy, and the Migration

Credit is not one market. The segments behave differently in a downturn, and the differences are the analytical content.

Segment Size (approx.) Rate exposure Marking Cycle visibility
Investment-grade bonds ~\$9 trillion Fixed; duration-dominated Daily, liquid High — but the spread is mostly duration
High-yield bonds ~\$1.4 trillion Fixed Daily, liquid High — the canonical cycle series
Leveraged loans ~\$1.4 trillion Floating Daily, less liquid Medium — LMEs obscure defaults
CLOs ~\$1 trillion Floating (tranched) Daily for tranches; portfolio marks lag Medium
Private credit / direct lending ~\$2 trillion Floating Quarterly, manager-determined Low — the paper's central problem
CMBS ~\$700 billion Fixed Daily; loan-level monthly High for delinquencies; extensions obscure
Consumer ABS and bank consumer books Several trillion Mixed Monthly performance data High

Public versus private. The decisive distinction. Public credit is marked daily by a market; private credit is marked quarterly by the manager, subject to valuation-committee review and third-party opinion. In a downturn, public marks fall immediately and sometimes overshoot; private marks fall slowly and sometimes never catch down until the loan is restructured. The lag is not fraud; it is the structure. But it means the private market's cycle is invisible in its own reported performance until it becomes a liquidity event.

Floating versus fixed. Since 2022 the distinction has been the cost-of-capital shock. Floating-rate borrowers — leveraged loans and essentially all of private credit — saw their interest costs roughly double as the Fed moved from zero to above 5%, and with the Fed at 3.50–3.75% and debating hikes, the relief that the 2024–2025 cuts provided has been partial and is not being extended. Fixed-rate borrowers locked low coupons and face the shock only at refinancing. The floating-rate segments are where the cash-flow stress is concentrated and where PIK, amend-and-extend, and LMEs are most prevalent.

Secured versus unsecured. Recovery rates. First-lien loans have historically recovered 60–70 cents; unsecured bonds 30–40; subordinated debt less. The historical figures are now unreliable, because the LME era has introduced priming — new debt inserted ahead of existing first-lien holders — that has produced first-lien recoveries in the 20s and 30s in specific cases. Seniority is no longer a guarantee of position; it is a starting point for a negotiation.

The migration. The structural fact of the 2020s is that lending moved. Post-2008 regulation made bank balance sheets expensive for leveraged loans; the syndicated market absorbed the volume through 2019; and then private credit — direct lenders, BDCs, insurance balance sheets — absorbed the incremental growth, reaching roughly \$2 trillion. The migration moved the cycle from the segment with the best visibility to the segment with the worst. That is the reason this paper exists.

PART II — CORPORATE CREDIT AS A CYCLE-TIMING TOOL

This Part treats the public corporate-credit segments — investment grade, high yield, loans, CLOs — with a specific purpose: not to describe them, but to extract from each the signal it provides about where the cycle is.

5. Investment Grade: Duration in a Credit Costume

Investment-grade corporate bonds are the largest credit segment and the least informative about the credit cycle, because their spread is a small fraction of their yield and their returns are dominated by duration. An IG index with a seven-year duration and an 81-basis-point spread moves seven times as much on a 50-basis-point Treasury move as on a 50-basis-point spread move. Most IG "credit" performance is a rates bet.

What IG does tell you. Two things. First, the BBB share and the fallen-angel risk. BBB-rated issuers now constitute roughly half of the IG index, up from a third two decades ago, because issuers have optimised leverage to the last investment-grade notch. In a downturn, downgrades push BBB paper into high yield, where the buyer base is smaller and the forced selling by IG-mandated holders is mechanical. The "BBB cliff" has been forecast for a decade and has not arrived at scale — Section 34 lists it among the false signals for that reason — but the mechanism is real, and the size of the BBB cohort is the size of the potential supply shock. Second, the IG-HY spread ratio. At 81 basis points IG against 269 HY, the ratio is roughly 3.3x. When the ratio compresses — HY tightening faster than IG — reach-for-yield is dominant, which is a late-cycle marker. When it expands — HY widening faster — risk aversion is rising in the segment with the most default exposure, which is the early-cycle-turn marker. The ratio is a dispersion measure and belongs in Section 9's toolkit.

The 2026 supply story. AI-infrastructure capital expenditure — data centres, power, fibre — is being financed substantially in the IG market by hyperscalers and utilities. The issuance is large enough to have become a technical: more supply from high-quality issuers, modestly wider IG spreads for reasons that have nothing to do with credit deterioration. This is an example of the technical component of Section 1 dominating a spread series, and a reason to read IG spreads with the issuance calendar in hand.

6. High Yield: The Default Cycle and the CCC Tail

High yield is the canonical cycle series — the one with the longest clean history, the deepest liquidity, and the strongest documented lead over equities. It is also the one most contaminated by index construction and by the migration of the weakest credits out of it.

The default cycle. High-yield default rates have run between roughly 1% and 3% in expansions and spiked to 10–14% in the three major downturns (1990–91, 2001–02, 2008–09). The 2020 spike was brief and truncated by the Fed's corporate-credit facilities. The current trailing rate near 1–1.5% is at the low end of the expansion range, and the LCD default predictor — a regression on loan prices — puts the six-month forward rate at 1.50%. On these figures the cycle is not turning.

The CCC tail is the tell. The CCC-and-lower cohort is the index's Ponzi cohort in Minsky's terms — issuers dependent on refinancing or on asset appreciation. Its spread relative to BB is a direct dispersion measure: BB-CCC compression means investors are reaching down the quality curve for yield (late-cycle excess); BB-CCC widening means the tail is being repriced while the quality end holds (the turn beginning). The June 2026 midyear outlooks were, across managers, reducing CCC exposure explicitly — "tighter spreads leave less room for error in a higher-rate environment" — which is the market's own bifurcation reading. The CCC series is on FRED (BAMLH0A3HYC) and belongs in the weekly monitoring.

The composition problem. The high-yield bond index has become higher-quality over two decades — the BB share has risen, the CCC share has fallen — partly because the weakest borrowers migrated to the loan and private markets. A tight HY spread is therefore partly a reflection of a better index, not only of better conditions. Comparing the current 269 to the pre-2010 history without adjusting for composition overstates how rich the market is. It is still rich; it is less anomalously rich than the raw comparison implies.

What the July 2026 episode showed. Between 21 and 29 July, on a 21% monthly oil spike and a 27-basis-point rise in the ten-year, the HY spread widened from 269 to 284, the VIX rose from 18.7 to 20.7, and the S&P 500 fell 2.5%. A 15-basis-point widening is nothing at the level. As an acceleration — 6% in eight days from the richest decile — it registered on the Top & Bottom overlay, and it was accompanied by an equity decline, which means it was not a divergence. The correct reading was "a shock being priced across assets simultaneously," not "credit leading." The episode is a useful calibration of what the acceleration signal does and does not say.

7. Leveraged Loans and CLOs: Floating Rate in a Hiking Regime

Leveraged loans are floating-rate, senior-secured, and held predominantly by CLOs — structured vehicles that hold portfolios of loans and issue tranched liabilities against them. The loan market is where the 2022–2026 cost-of-capital shock has been most direct, and where the default cycle has been most thoroughly rerouted.

The floating-rate transmission. A loan priced at SOFR plus 400 basis points cost the borrower roughly 4.5% in 2021 and roughly 9.5% at the 2023–2024 peak. For a borrower levered at six times EBITDA, that is a swing from roughly 27% of EBITDA consumed by interest to roughly 57%. The 2024–2025 rate cuts brought it down; the Fed's stall at 3.50–3.75% and the hiking debate have arrested the relief. The cohort of loans underwritten in 2020–2021 at peak leverage and trough rates is the cohort under the most stress, and the maturity schedule for that cohort is 2027–2028.

The loan-only issuer problem. Roughly half of leveraged-loan issuers have no public bonds outstanding. They are unrated by the bond market, unpriced by the HY index, and their defaults do not appear in the high-yield default rate. The loan default rate — 0.93% by amount, 1.25% by count in July 2026 — is the relevant series, and the distress ratio (share of loans trading below 80) at 6.89% is the leading indicator of it.

CLO structure and the equity tranche. A CLO's liabilities are tranched from AAA down to equity; losses are absorbed by equity first, then BB, then BBB. The AAA tranche has never taken a principal loss in the structure's history, which is the reason CLO AAA demand is deep and stable. The equity tranche takes the first loss and receives the residual cash flow, and its distributions are the canary: when CLO equity distributions fall because overcollateralisation tests are failing or because managers are trapping cash, the loan portfolio is deteriorating in a way the tranche prices have not yet reflected. CLO equity is the private-credit marking problem's public cousin — the first-loss piece of a portfolio whose stress is visible in the distributions before it is visible in the marks.

The CLO bid as a technical. CLO issuance is the marginal demand for leveraged loans. When CLO formation is strong, loans are bid regardless of fundamentals; when it stalls — because AAA spreads have widened and the arbitrage does not work — loans lose their marginal buyer and the distress ratio rises. CLO issuance volume is a supply-demand technical that belongs in the monitoring.

8. Liability Management: Where the Defaults Went

The defining institutional change in the current cycle is that defaults have been rerouted through liability-management exercises — out-of-court restructurings in which a borrower and a subset of its lenders agree to terms that impose losses on other lenders without a bankruptcy filing or a missed payment.

The mechanics. The covenant erosion of Section 2 removed the protections that would have prevented these transactions. Drop-downs transfer assets to an unrestricted subsidiary outside the lenders' collateral package and raise new debt against them. Uptiers have a majority of lenders exchange their existing debt for new, more senior debt, subordinating the minority who did not participate. Priming inserts new money ahead of existing first-lien holders. The borrower gains time and liquidity; the participating lenders gain seniority; the non-participating lenders take the loss.

The accounting consequence. An LME is rated as a "distressed exchange" or "selective default" by the agencies — S&P treated the July 2026 System1 transaction, in which lenders received roughly 72 cents, as "tantamount to a default." But it is not a payment default and does not appear in the headline default rate. PitchBook now publishes a dual-track rate: the legacy payment-default rate by count sits near 1.25%, and the rate including LMEs has run several points higher, with LMEs constituting roughly two-thirds of default activity by issuer count. The trailing twelve-month LME count fell to 18 by July 2026 from 37 a year earlier, and June's count of 16 was the lowest since August 2023 — a genuine improvement, and one to read carefully: fewer LMEs can mean fewer distressed borrowers or fewer borrowers with LME-eligible documents left.

Why it matters for timing. The headline default rate is the series every model was calibrated on, and it has been structurally suppressed. A framework using it to time the cycle will see defaults at 1% and conclude the cycle has not turned, when the dual-track rate says restructuring activity has been running at levels that in prior cycles corresponded to spreads of 500–600 basis points. The right series is the dual-track rate; the right level comparison is against the dual-track history; and the right supplementary read is the recovery on the LMEs, which has been running far below the historical first-lien norm.

The "90/10" market. PineBridge's 2026 framing captures the resulting structure: roughly 90% of leveraged-finance issuers are stable and performing, and roughly 10% are highly leveraged credits subject to complex restructurings that should be avoided at any price near par. An index spread of 269 is the market-weighted average of those two populations. It describes neither.

9. The Timing Toolkit

The signals that exploit credit's lead, ranked by how early they fire and specified operationally.

1. Lending standards (SLOOS). Quarterly; leads spreads and defaults by two to four quarters. The net percentage of banks tightening C&I standards is the headline series; the special questions on the level of standards and on lending to non-depository financial institutions are the current-cycle additions. Bull signal: net easing with standards below the historical midpoint (the current C&I reading, which is a late-cycle marker). Bear signal: net tightening for two consecutive quarters, which preceded every recession since the series began.

2. Dispersion. Weekly. The BB-CCC spread; the HY-IG ratio; the loan distress ratio against the loan default rate; and — the current cycle's addition — public versus private marks for comparable credits, and the discount of listed BDCs to their own net asset values. Bull signal: compression, meaning the tail is being bid. Bear signal: widening while the index holds, meaning the tail is being repriced beneath a benign average.

3. Acceleration. Weekly. The rate of change of the HY spread over four to eight weeks, scaled by its level — a 50-basis-point widening from 270 is a larger signal than from 500. The Top & Bottom overlay implements this. Bear signal: widening at a rate that exceeds the recent realised volatility of the spread, sustained for more than two weeks.

4. Credit-equity divergence. Weekly. Spreads widening while equities make new highs, or the HY index declining while the S&P 500 rises over a four-to-eight-week window. The lender saying no while the equity holder says yes. Historically the highest-value single configuration, because it means the asymmetric-payoff holder has changed its assessment and the symmetric-payoff holder has not yet noticed. Its inverse — spreads tightening while equities fall — has marked the end of drawdowns.

5. The dual-track default rate and LME count. Monthly. The true restructuring rate, and its direction. Bull signal: the LME count declining for reasons of improving fundamentals (verify against the distress ratio). Bear signal: LME count rising while the payment default rate stays flat, which is the cycle turning invisibly.

6. CLO equity distributions and issuance. Monthly. The first-loss canary and the marginal-demand technical.

7. Spread level. Last. It confirms what the others have said. A level in the richest decile is a statement about how much room there is to widen, not about whether widening is coming.

The combination rule. Standards tightening plus dispersion widening plus acceleration firing plus divergence from equities — all four together — has preceded every credit-led equity drawdown in the available history. Any one alone is noise. The discipline, as in Volatility, is the count.

10. Corporate Credit: Bull, Bear, and Range

The bull case

  • Default rates near 1%, forward predictors near 1.5%, and LME counts at three-year lows — the fundamentals are not deteriorating on the public measures
  • Demand for yield is structural: insurers, pensions, and the retail income complex absorb issuance at tight spreads regardless of cycle position
  • The 2024–2025 rate cuts relieved the floating-rate cohort, and the worst of the cost shock is behind
  • Index quality has improved; the current spread is less rich than the raw historical comparison implies
  • Recession, the necessary condition for a default cycle, is not the base case with initial claims at the low end of seasonal ranges

The bear case

  • Spreads in the richest decile leave no compensation for a cycle turn; the risk premium has been competed to the floor
  • The default cycle has been rerouted into LMEs and the headline rate is structurally suppressed — the dual-track rate is the honest series and it is several points higher
  • The Fed at 3.50–3.75% and debating hikes means the floating-rate relief has stalled with the 2020–2021 vintage's 2027–2028 maturities approaching
  • C&I standards easier than their post-2005 midpoint is the loosest-standards-in-the-most-leveraged-segment configuration that precedes turns
  • The 90/10 split means the index average is describing a population that does not exist; the 10% is where the losses are, and it is priced near par in the aggregate

The range-bound case

  • Spreads oscillate between 250 and 350 as the reach-for-yield technical and the fundamental caution offset; dispersion widens slowly; the cycle turns in the private market first and the public market watches

What would settle it

Two consecutive quarters of net SLOOS tightening in C&I, combined with the BB-CCC spread widening while the index holds. That combination has no false positives in the available history.

PART III — PRIVATE CREDIT AND THE BIFURCATION

This Part carries the paper's first emphasis. It is the longest because the subject is the least well covered anywhere else: the public analysis of private credit is dominated by managers who sell it and by critics who have not read the filings, and the space between those positions is where the actual risk lives.

11. What It Is and How Big It Got

Private credit is lending by non-bank, non-public-market institutions — direct-lending funds, business development companies, insurance balance sheets, and the private-credit arms of the large alternative managers — to companies that are typically sponsor-owned, middle-market, and too small or too levered for the syndicated market. The loans are floating-rate, senior-secured in structure, bilateral or club rather than syndicated, and held to maturity by the originator. There is no secondary market of consequence.

The scale. Roughly \$2 trillion globally, from under \$300 billion in 2010. The growth was structural: post-crisis capital rules made leveraged lending expensive on bank balance sheets; the syndicated market's minimum efficient deal size left the middle market underserved; private-equity sponsors valued the speed and certainty of a bilateral lender over the syndication process; and the yield — SOFR plus 500–700 basis points for first-lien paper — attracted institutional allocators, then insurers, then, through non-traded BDCs and interval funds, retail.

The pitch, stated fairly. Senior-secured exposure to the middle market at a premium to public credit, with lower reported volatility, a direct relationship with the borrower that permits early intervention, and a track record — the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index — showing returns comparable to or above public high yield with a fraction of the drawdowns. The relationship point is real: a bilateral lender can amend, extend, and restructure without the collective-action problems of a syndicate.

The problem, stated as fairly. The lower reported volatility is substantially an artefact of the marking process. The premium yield compensates for illiquidity and for the absence of a market check on the marks. The track record covers a period — 2010 to 2025 — that included one brief, policy-truncated recession, no sustained default cycle, and, until 2022, no cost-of-capital shock. And the retail channel, which is the source of the marginal capital, has redemption terms that are mismatched to the liquidity of the assets in a way that the first quarter of 2026 demonstrated.

12. The Marking Problem

A public loan is marked by the last trade. A private loan is marked by the manager, quarterly, at "fair value" as the manager determines it, subject to a valuation committee, a board, and typically a third-party valuation firm's review of a sample. The mark is an opinion about what a willing buyer would pay, in a market where there are no willing buyers because the loans do not trade.

Why the lag is structural rather than dishonest. The manager has every incentive to hold the mark — fees are charged on net assets, performance is reported on net assets, and redemptions are met at net asset value — but the mechanism does not require bad faith. A loan that is current on interest, covenant-lite, and to a borrower whose sponsor is supportive has no event that forces a mark-down. The deterioration that a public market would price in real time — margin compression, rising leverage, a weakening sector — produces no trade and therefore no mark. The mark moves when something happens: a missed payment, a covenant breach on the rare loan that has one, a restructuring. By then the loss has been accumulating for quarters.

The evidence that marks are not anchored. PIMCO's June 2026 analysis made the decisive observation: across lenders holding comparable credits, marks show low time-series volatility alongside high cross-sectional dispersion. The same loan, or loans to the same borrower, are marked at materially different levels by different managers, and each manager's marks barely move quarter to quarter. Both cannot be true if the marks are tracking a common underlying value. Low volatility across time is consistent with tracking a stable value; high dispersion across managers is consistent with each manager tracking its own assumptions. The combination means the net asset values are "increasingly driven by manager-specific assumptions rather than a shared market-clearing level" — PIMCO's phrase — and the dispersion is higher among the non-traded BDCs whose reported performance appears smoothest.

The market's own verdict. Listed BDCs — whose shares trade on exchanges even though their loans do not — have traded at discounts of 15–25% to their reported net asset values through 2025–2026. A discount of that size and persistence is the public market's estimate of how overstated the private marks are. It is imprecise, it is contaminated by sentiment and by the discount's own liquidity, and it is the only market-based read on private-credit marks that exists. Section 15 describes what happened when investors in non-traded vehicles compared their own marks to it.

Why this is the cycle-visibility problem. If a \$2 trillion market marks its own assets on a lag, the credit cycle in that market is invisible in its reported performance until the events that force marks — restructurings, non-accruals, liquidity failures — accumulate. The cycle can be well advanced in the underlying cash flows while the reported returns are flat. That is not a hypothesis; it is what the structure implies, and the first quarter of 2026 is the first observation consistent with it.

13. Structure: BDCs, Evergreens, Interval Funds, Insurance, and the Retail Channel

Business development companies are the regulated wrapper. Listed BDCs trade on exchanges and mark quarterly; non-traded BDCs — the growth vehicle of the past five years — are sold through wealth-management channels, do not trade, and offer quarterly repurchases typically capped at 5% of shares. The cap is the liquidity mechanism, and it is the whole story of Section 15.

Evergreen and interval funds are the same structure in different regulatory clothing — perpetual vehicles with periodic, capped liquidity — and are the fastest-growing retail conduit. Their marketing emphasises the yield and the "lower volatility"; their prospectuses disclose the caps and the manager's discretion over marks; the gap between the two is the retail investor's risk.

Insurance balance sheets are the largest institutional holders, either directly or through affiliated managers — the Apollo–Athene, KKR–Global Atlantic, Blackstone–insurance-partnership model. Insurers value private credit because it is rated (by the agencies, on the manager's information), yields more than public IG for the same rating, and is held at amortised cost under insurance accounting, which means the marking problem does not even arise. The concentration of private credit on insurance balance sheets is the channel through which a private-credit cycle would become a financial-stability question, and it is the reason the Fed's SLOOS now asks banks specifically about their lending to non-depository financial institutions.

Bank lending to the lenders. Banks, having exited direct leveraged lending, have re-entered it one step removed: they provide leverage facilities to the BDCs and direct-lending funds. The July 2026 SLOOS special questions on NDFI lending standards are the regulator's acknowledgement that the exposure has come back through the side door. A bank that lends to a BDC at 50% loan-to-value against a portfolio marked at the manager's discretion has an exposure whose true loan-to-value is unknown.

The retail channel is the marginal capital and the marginal risk. Wealth-management platforms distributed non-traded BDCs and interval funds to individual investors on the strength of yield and smoothed returns; those investors have quarterly liquidity expectations and monthly statements; and when the statements diverge from what the investors believe — as in the first quarter of 2026 — they redeem, and the 5% cap converts a sentiment shift into a queue.

14. Where Stress Hides

Four mechanisms allow a deteriorating private-credit portfolio to report stable performance.

Payment-in-kind. A loan with a PIK toggle allows the borrower to pay interest by issuing additional principal rather than cash. The lender accrues the income, reports it as earned, and the loan balance grows. Public BDCs now receive roughly 8% of investment income via PIK on average. The Boston Fed's tracking of BDC filings shows the share of loans with PIK usage rising from roughly 6% in early 2022 to roughly 10% by early 2026 — a 67% increase over three years — with construction-sector PIK rising from under 5% to nearly 20%, and wholesale trade and transportation more than doubling. PIMCO's read of the first quarter: PIK share flat sequentially, deteriorating year over year. PIK is the cleanest measurable form of the lending-standards deterioration in Section 2, because it is a lender agreeing, loan by loan, to be paid later in exchange for reporting income now.

Amend-and-extend. A loan approaching maturity that cannot be refinanced is amended — maturity pushed out, spread raised, perhaps a fee paid — and remains current. The loan does not default. The borrower's leverage has not fallen. The lender has converted a maturity event into a duration extension and reported no loss. This is "extend and pretend," and it is rational for both parties in the short run and cumulatively dangerous, because it defers the recognition to a moment of the cycle's choosing rather than the lender's.

Non-accrual definitions. A loan is placed on non-accrual when the manager determines that collection of interest is doubtful. The determination is the manager's. Blue Owl reported non-accruals of 0.6% while receiving redemption requests for 40.7% of its technology vehicles. Both figures are accurate; they describe different things — the manager's assessment of collectability and the investors' assessment of the manager's assessment.

Restructuring into equity. When a loan is restructured, the lender often receives equity in the borrower in exchange for debt forgiveness. The equity is marked at the manager's estimate of its value. The loss on the debt is partly offset by the mark on the equity, and the realised loss reported is smaller than the economic loss incurred.

The combined effect is that a private-credit portfolio under stress reports rising PIK income, extended maturities, low non-accruals, and modest realised losses — a set of numbers that looks stable and is the signature of deterioration. Section 31 lists the tells that see through it.

15. The 2026 Redemption Wave: The Case Study

The first quarter of 2026 produced the first genuine liquidity event in private credit's current form, and it is worth recording precisely because it will be the template.

The trigger was sentiment, not credit. In late 2025 and early 2026, concern about artificial intelligence's potential to disrupt software business models — the sector in which several large direct lenders were most concentrated — collided with the visible discount of listed BDCs to their net asset values. Investors in non-traded vehicles, who could redeem at stated NAV, observed that the listed equivalents traded 20% below stated NAV, concluded that the "real" value of their holdings was below what they would receive on redemption, and redeemed. The arithmetic is the non-traded REIT episode of 2023 transposed: when the redemption price exceeds the perceived value, redemption is rational regardless of the underlying assets.

The scale. Non-traded BDCs received redemption requests equal to 12.1% of net assets in the quarter — against 5% quarterly caps. Blue Owl received requests for 40.7% of its technology-focused vehicles and 21.9% of its credit-income funds. Most major non-traded BDCs capped repurchases at the standard 5% and prorated. Moody's recorded the first-ever net outflows from the perpetual non-traded BDC category.

The response that became the case study: Blue Owl proposed merging its non-traded vehicle (OBDC II) into its listed BDC (OBDC) at the two funds' stated net asset values. The portfolios overlapped by roughly 98%. But OBDC traded at a 20% discount to its NAV, so OBDC II investors would receive listed shares worth 20% less than the stated NAV at which they had been told they could redeem. A class action followed, alleging that the manager had described redemption pressure as not "meaningful" while withdrawals accelerated.

What it revealed. Three things. First, the liquidity mismatch is not theoretical: a 5% quarterly cap against 12% requests produces a multi-quarter queue, and the queue itself deters new inflows, which is the feedback loop PIMCO describes between stale marks and fund flows. Second, the underlying portfolios were not, on the manager's own measures, in distress — non-accruals of 0.6%, returns in line with the Cliffwater index — which means a redemption run can occur without a credit event, on the marks question alone. Third, the resolution mechanism — a merger at stated NAV into a discounted listed vehicle — converted the marking gap into a realised loss for the investors who had trusted the marks, which is exactly the outcome the marking problem predicts.

The aftermath. Deployment fell to a one-year low; leveraged-buyout financing fell to its lowest level since the third quarter of 2023; gross fundings among the top public BDCs fell to roughly \$5.7 billion, the lowest tracked in two years, with a pronounced pullback from technology. Managers argued that the redemption wave was an overreaction to sentiment, and on the credit fundamentals they had a case. But the fundamentals were not what the investors had redeemed against. They had redeemed against the marks.

16. The Bifurcation Thesis, Stated Fully

The thesis that Top & Bottom carries as a working hypothesis, derived.

The claim. In the current cycle, credit stress is concentrated in an identifiable tail — the most levered borrowers, the 2020–2021 vintage, the software and consumer-discretionary sectors, the loan-only and private-credit segments — while the aggregate measures that most participants watch describe a population that is performing. The aggregate can hold, and even improve, while the tail deteriorates to the point of loss, because the aggregate is a weighted average and the tail is small by weight. The cycle is happening inside the average.

The mechanisms that produce it. Four, all covered above. The migration of the weakest credits out of the public bond index, which improved the index and moved the risk. The rerouting of defaults into LMEs, which removed them from the headline rate. The private-credit marking lag, which removed them from reported returns. And the PineBridge 90/10 structure of the public leveraged market, in which the index spread describes neither the healthy majority nor the distressed minority.

The evidence. The spread at 269 against a dual-track default rate several points above the headline. The distress ratio rising while the payment default rate falls. PIK usage up two-thirds over three years while reported non-accruals sit under 1%. Mark dispersion across managers at elevated levels while time-series volatility stays low. Listed BDCs at 20% discounts to NAV. Record redemption requests against portfolios the managers describe as healthy. Each is a measurement of the gap between the aggregate and the tail.

Why it matters for timing. A framework watching the aggregate will not see the turn until the tail's losses are large enough to move the average — which, given the marking lag and the LME rerouting, may be after the losses have been realised by the investors who hold the tail. The bifurcation means the timing signals of Section 9 must be run on the dispersion series, not the level series, and that the private market's tells in Section 31 are the leading indicators for the public market's spread.

Why it could be wrong. If the tail is genuinely small — 10% of issuers, concentrated in sectors whose problems are idiosyncratic — and the healthy majority is genuinely healthy, then the bifurcation is a feature rather than a warning: a market correctly distinguishing between borrowers, with the losses contained to the investors who took the tail risk knowingly. That reading requires the tail to stay at 10%, which requires no recession, no further cost-of-capital shock, and no second-order contagion from the private-credit liquidity mechanics to the public market. Each of those is a testable proposition, and Part VIII tests them.

17. Private Credit: Bull, Bear, and Range — and the Untested-Cycle Caveat

The bull case

  • The asset class delivered on its promise through 2010–2025: returns above public high yield, a fraction of the drawdowns, and no systemic event
  • Senior-secured structure and bilateral relationships permit early intervention that syndicated lenders cannot match
  • The 2026 redemption wave was sentiment-driven and the underlying portfolios, on the managers' measures, performed; the episode tested the liquidity mechanics and the caps held
  • Asset-based finance — lending against receivables, equipment, and consumer assets — is a genuinely different risk profile that diversifies away from the corporate direct-lending tail
  • Insurance-balance-sheet demand is structural and long-dated; the marginal capital is not fleeing

The bear case

  • Marks are manager-determined, lagged, and — on PIMCO's evidence — not anchored to a common value; reported performance is not a cycle indicator
  • PIK usage up 67% in three years, non-accrual definitions at the manager's discretion, and amend-and-extend as standard practice mean the stress is accumulating unreported
  • The retail channel's redemption terms are mismatched to asset liquidity, and the first quarter of 2026 demonstrated that a run can occur on the marks question alone, without a credit event
  • The 2020–2021 vintage's 2027–2028 maturities arrive with the Fed at 3.50–3.75% and debating hikes — the cost-of-capital relief has stalled at the wrong moment
  • The asset class at \$2 trillion has never been through a sustained default cycle, and the historical record for bank-syndicated middle-market loans — which showed 10%-plus default rates in 2001 and 2009 — is the closest analogue

The range-bound case

  • The liquidity queue clears over several quarters as inflows recover; deployment stays subdued; PIK and non-accruals drift higher without a break; the 90/10 structure persists with the 10% restructured quietly through LMEs and equity conversions; the public market never sees a spread event

The untested-cycle caveat

The paper's honest position is that private credit's behaviour in a sustained downturn is unknown, and that the two available analogues point in different directions. The syndicated middle-market record from 2001 and 2009 says default rates in the low teens and recoveries well below the first-lien norm. The private-credit record from 2020 says a brief drawdown, sponsor support, and rapid recovery. The difference is that 2020 was policy-truncated within weeks and 2001 and 2009 were not. A framework should hold both and let the tells in Section 31 discriminate.

What would settle it

A sustained rise in non-accruals across managers — not one manager's software book, but the category — accompanied by mark-downs that reduce cross-sectional dispersion (meaning managers are converging on a lower common value). That is the signature of the marking lag resolving. Its absence through 2027, with the 2020–2021 vintage refinanced, would validate the bull case.

PART IV — SECURITISED AND CONSUMER CREDIT

18. CMBS and the Maturity Wall

Commercial mortgage-backed securities pool commercial real estate loans and tranche them. They are the most transparent segment of real-estate credit — loan-level performance is reported monthly — and the one where the cycle is most visible, because it is not being rerouted or privately marked. It is being extended, which is a different form of concealment.

The numbers. Trepp's CMBS delinquency rate stood at 7.55% in May 2026, with office at 11.69% — a level that exceeds the 2012 post-crisis peak for the property type. The seriously-delinquent rate (60-plus days, foreclosure, REO, or non-performing matured balloons) was 7.27%. And the composition of new delinquencies is the diagnostic: 70% of newly delinquent balances in May were non-performing matured balloons — loans that reached maturity and could not be refinanced — against 28% that were 30 days late on payments. The delinquency is not borrowers failing to pay interest. It is borrowers failing to find a lender at maturity.

The maturity wall. Roughly \$2–2.5 trillion of commercial real-estate debt across all lenders matures between 2024 and 2027, underwritten at 2015–2021 rates and valuations, and must be refinanced at 2026 rates against 2026 valuations. For office, the valuation decline has been 30–50% in major markets; for the sector as a whole, the combination of higher rates and lower values means many loans cannot be refinanced at their existing balance without new equity.

Extend-and-pretend as the resolution. Trepp's alternative measure — including loans past maturity but current on interest, the "performing matured balloon" category — puts the delinquency rate at 9.06%, 152 basis points above the headline. Those are loans that have been extended by special servicers or lenders rather than foreclosed, because foreclosure would crystallise a loss the lender prefers to defer. The mechanism is the CMBS analogue of amend-and-extend in Section 14, and its effect is the same: the cycle is real, it is being deferred, and the deferral concentrates the eventual recognition.

Where the losses land. Regional banks, which hold roughly half of all commercial real-estate debt and whose CRE concentration is the reason the March 2023 episode occurred. CMBS B-piece holders, who take the first loss on the securitised portion. And the private real-estate lenders and debt funds that expanded into the gap banks left. The Top & Bottom report implements the regional-bank stress indicators; this paper's contribution is the read on the securitised channel and the observation that the maturity-driven delinquency is the leading edge.

19. RMBS and Housing Credit

Residential mortgage credit is the segment that produced the last systemic crisis and is, in this cycle, the least stressed — for reasons that are structural rather than cyclical.

The lock-in. Roughly 60% of outstanding US mortgages carry rates below 4%, originated in 2020–2021. Borrowers with those rates do not refinance and rarely move, which means the mortgage credit outstanding is disproportionately high-quality, low-rate, and seasoned. New origination at 6–7% rates is small relative to the stock. The credit quality of the book is the best in the series' history.

The non-bank servicing risk. The structural vulnerability is not credit; it is operational. Non-bank servicers now handle the majority of mortgages and must advance principal and interest to securitisation trusts when borrowers are delinquent. In a delinquency spike, servicer advance obligations become a liquidity demand on entities without deposit funding. The 2020 forbearance episode tested this and the Fed's facilities covered it; it remains the channel to watch.

The read for the cycle. Mortgage delinquencies — TransUnion projects 60-plus-day mortgage delinquency at 1.65% by year-end 2026, up 11 basis points — are a lagging indicator of household stress, and a low one. Housing credit is not where this cycle turns.

20. ABS and Consumer Credit

Consumer credit is the household-cycle read, and it has a specific structure in this cycle: aggregate measures that look benign and a subprime tail that does not.

Cards. The delinquency rate on credit-card loans at all commercial banks stood at 2.9% in the first quarter of 2026 — well below the 6.8% crisis peak. But the aggregate is dominated by the largest issuers, whose post-2008 underwriting concentrated subprime exposure at smaller banks. Delinquency at banks outside the top 100 runs at 6.4% — a 3.5-point gap over the aggregate, and the cleaner measure of where household stress is. The 90-plus-day serious delinquency rate on cards is near 2.6%, roughly flat.

Auto. Sixty-plus-day auto delinquencies are projected at 1.54% by year-end 2026, the fifth consecutive annual increase, though each increase has been smaller. Subprime auto is the tell: consumer-finance lenders reported annualised net charge-offs near 3% in the first quarter, up from 2.6% a year earlier, on rising repossessions. Subprime auto has led every consumer-credit downturn because the borrower is marginal and the collateral depreciates.

The bifurcation, again. The consumer picture is the corporate picture at household scale: a healthy majority with locked-in low-rate mortgages and rising real wages, and a stressed minority — subprime, small-bank, non-prime personal loans — where delinquencies are at or near cycle highs. The aggregate consumer-delinquency series will not turn until the minority's stress spreads to the majority, which requires unemployment. TransUnion's 2026 forecast assumes unemployment rising to 4.5%; the consumer-credit cycle turns on that number more than on any other.

21. Securitised and Consumer Credit: Bull, Bear, and Range

Bull. Housing credit is the strongest in the series' history; consumer aggregates are stable; CRE stress is concentrated in office and is being worked through by extension; the maturity wall is being met with new equity and extended terms rather than foreclosure.

Bear. Office CMBS delinquency at 11.7% exceeds the post-crisis peak; 70% of new delinquencies are maturity-driven, meaning the wall is binding now; extend-and-pretend defers rather than resolves; small-bank card delinquency at 6.4% and rising subprime auto charge-offs are the household tail deteriorating; and a rise in unemployment to 4.5% would move the tail into the aggregate.

Range. CRE workouts proceed slowly over three to five years with losses absorbed by regional banks and B-piece holders without a systemic event; consumer delinquencies drift higher without a break; the securitised segment is a slow bleed rather than a cliff.

What would settle it. For CRE: the share of maturing loans that resolve by extension versus by foreclosure or discounted payoff — a rising foreclosure share means the lenders have stopped deferring. For consumers: unemployment above 4.5% and the aggregate card delinquency rate moving above 3.5%.

PART V — CROSS-CUTTING DRIVERS

22. Credit and Rates

Credit's relationship to the policy rate runs through two channels that currently point the same direction.

The floating-rate transmission. Leveraged loans and private credit reprice within a quarter of a Fed move. The 2022–2023 hiking cycle roughly doubled interest costs for the floating-rate cohort; the 2024–2025 cuts relieved perhaps half of that; the Fed's stall at 3.50–3.75% under a Chair whose first speech was hawkish has stopped the relief. For a borrower levered at six times with a 2027 maturity, the difference between the Fed at 3.5% and at 2.5% is the difference between a refinancing and a restructuring. The rate path is the private-credit cycle's most important exogenous variable.

The refinancing-wall channel. Fixed-rate borrowers face the rate shock only at maturity, which concentrates the stress in the years when the low-coupon vintages come due. For high-yield bonds, the 2020–2021 issuance — the largest vintage in the market's history, at the lowest coupons — matures 2027–2029. For CRE, 2024–2027. The wall is a schedule, and the schedule is known.

The interaction with the Treasury market. The 30-year Treasury at 5.33% in August 2026 — its highest since 2007 — raises the risk-free base against which all credit is priced, and the term-premium expansion that Rates & Liquidity and Currencies examine feeds directly into the cost of long-dated corporate issuance. IG issuers financing AI infrastructure at ten-to-thirty-year maturities are paying for that term premium. A sustained long-end selloff is a credit tightening independent of the Fed.

23. Credit and Equities: The Lead-Lag Evidence

Section 3 stated the mechanism. This section states the evidence and its limits, because the lead is the paper's timing thesis and it should not be oversold.

Where the lead was clear. 2007–2008: HY spreads widened from June 2007; the S&P 500 peaked in October 2007 and collapsed in September 2008. 2014–2016: energy HY widened from mid-2014; the broad HY index followed in 2015; the equity low was February 2016. 2018: HY widened from October; equities fell in December. In each case the credit move preceded the equity move by one to fourteen months.

Where it was not. 2020: the shock was exogenous and simultaneous; credit and equities fell together in a fortnight. 2022: the index-level HY spread widened coincident with the equity top in January, though CCC-BB dispersion had been widening since the prior autumn. 2025: the tariff shock hit both simultaneously in April.

The pattern. Credit leads in endogenous downturns — those that arise from the accumulation of leverage and the deterioration of the marginal borrower — because that is what credit prices. It does not lead in exogenous shocks, because nobody prices what nobody expects. The current cycle's stress is endogenous — vintage, leverage, cost of capital, marking lag — which is the configuration in which the lead has historically been reliable.

The divergence signal. The highest-value configuration: spreads widening while equities rise. It appeared in the summer of 2007, in the autumn of 2014, and in the autumn of 2018. It has not appeared in 2026 — the July episode was a co-move, not a divergence. Its appearance would be the single strongest timing signal the framework produces.

24. Credit and Liquidity

The funding channel belongs to Rates & Liquidity and is referenced rather than rebuilt. Three points specific to credit.

Dealer balance-sheet capacity determines whether credit reprices smoothly or gaps. Post-2008 rules reduced the capacity of banks to warehouse corporate bonds; the result is that credit markets are liquid in calm conditions and illiquid in stress, and that spread widening in a stress episode is discontinuous. The March 2020 move to 1,087 basis points was a liquidity gap, not a credit repricing.

The CLO and BDC leverage facilities are the channel through which bank liquidity reaches leveraged lending one step removed. Their terms — advance rates, mark triggers — are the mechanism by which a private-credit mark-down would become a forced-selling event.

The Fed's corporate-credit facilities of 2020 established that the central bank will backstop investment-grade and fallen-angel credit in a crisis. That expectation is embedded in the risk premium — part of the reason spreads are in the richest decile — and it is untested for the segments (loans, private credit) that the facilities did not cover.

25. Bank Lending Standards as the Earliest Signal

The Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey is quarterly, published in the first month after each quarter, and asks roughly 80 domestic and foreign banks whether they tightened or eased standards, and why, and what demand did. It is the earliest reliable credit-cycle signal in the available data, and it is underused because it is a survey rather than a price.

How to read it. The net percentage of banks tightening C&I standards is the headline. A reading above zero for two consecutive quarters has preceded every US recession since 1990 with a lead of two to four quarters; it has also produced false positives (2016, 2023). The reasons given — economic outlook, industry-specific problems, reduced risk tolerance, bank capital — distinguish a demand-side tightening from a supply-side one. The special questions vary by survey and are frequently the most informative part: the July 2026 questions on the level of standards relative to history, and on lending to non-depository financial institutions, are the current cycle's additions.

The 2026 sequence. Tighter C&I standards in the fourth quarter of 2025 and the first of 2026; unchanged in the second; C&I standards easier than the post-2005 midpoint on the level question; CRE standards easing; all other categories at the tight end of their ranges. Banks expect small-firm C&I quality to deteriorate over the year. The reading is late-cycle: standards loosened from a tight starting point in the segment with the most leverage, and the banks themselves expect deterioration at the small end.

The private-credit gap. The SLOOS surveys banks. Private credit's lending standards are surveyed by no one; they are inferred from the deal terms — PIK share, covenant terms, leverage multiples at origination — that PitchBook LCD and the managers' own filings disclose. The framework treats those terms as the private market's SLOOS.

PART V-B — POSITION CONSTRUCTION

This Part is educational description of structures commonly used, not a recommendation. Credit instruments carry default, liquidity, and structural risks that vary by segment; suitability, tax treatment, and regulatory access vary by investor; and none of this is investment advice.

26. The Instrument Set

Credit ETFs. HYG and JNK for high yield; LQD for investment grade; BKLN and SRLN for leveraged loans; the CLO-tranche ETFs (JAAA for AAA, JBBB for mezzanine); HYGH and similar for rate-hedged high yield. Liquid, accessible, and subject to a structural problem: most retail credit exposure is a duration bet. LQD's seven-year duration means its returns are dominated by Treasury yields; HYG's four-year duration is smaller but still material. An investor who wants credit-spread exposure without rate exposure needs either the rate-hedged variants, the floating-rate loan products, or a Treasury futures overlay.

CDX and CDS. The credit default swap indices — CDX IG (125 investment-grade names) and CDX HY (100 high-yield names) — are the cleanest pure-spread instruments: no duration, no coupon, liquid, and the vehicle through which institutional credit views are expressed. CDX options exist and are the source of the credit-vol signal in Volatility. Single-name CDS provides borrower-specific protection. Institutional access; retail exposure is indirect.

BDC equity. The listed BDCs — ARCC, OBDC, MAIN, BXSL, and others — are the only liquid exposure to private credit. Their discount or premium to net asset value is both an instrument and a signal: buying a BDC at a 20% discount is buying private-credit marks at 80 cents, which is either the market's honest estimate or an opportunity, and the framework's view is that it is more the former than the latter until non-accruals across the category have peaked.

Loan and CLO funds. Interval funds and closed-end funds holding loans and CLO tranches; institutional CLO tranche purchases directly. The AAA tranche has never lost principal; the equity tranche is a levered bet on the loan cycle.

Credit futures. Cboe's iBoxx IG and HY corporate bond index futures (IBIG, IBHY) — liquid enough for institutional hedging, and the instrument through which credit exposure can be adjusted without touching the underlying bonds. Volume in the IG contract reached records in 2025 as investors reset hedges.

Private-credit funds directly. Non-traded BDCs, interval funds, evergreen vehicles. Section 28 addresses how to underwrite them.

27. Near-Term Construction

The HY-IG compression and decompression trade. Long CDX HY against short CDX IG, sized by beta, expresses a view on dispersion. Compression (HY outperforming) in a reach-for-yield phase; decompression (HY underperforming) when the tail is repriced. The trade isolates the credit-cycle position from the direction of rates.

The quality trade within HY. Long BB against short CCC — expressed through the rating-bucket ETFs or through single-name selection — is the direct bifurcation expression: it profits when the tail deteriorates relative to the quality end, and it is the position the 2026 midyear outlooks were describing when they reduced CCC exposure.

The credit-equity pair. Short HYG against long SPY, or the reverse, expresses the divergence signal of Section 23 directly. When spreads widen while equities rise, the position is long credit protection against long equities; its payoff is the equity market catching down. Sized by the historical beta between HY returns and equity returns, roughly 0.4–0.5.

Curve trades. CDX has a term structure; five-year against ten-year expresses a view on whether the market is pricing near-term or long-term deterioration. Steepening (long-dated protection getting relatively cheaper) is typical of late-cycle complacency about the horizon.

Defined-risk expressions. Put spreads on HYG for a defined-risk short; CDX HY payer spreads for the institutional equivalent. Credit is negatively skewed, so option protection is structurally expensive; spreads are the standard mitigation.

The BDC discount trade. Long listed BDCs at deep discounts against short the private-credit "story" — expressed as a relative position within the segment (discounted listed against fully-valued non-traded exposure) — is a bet that the discount is excessive. It has worked in prior cycles once non-accruals peaked. It has not worked before they peaked.

28. Long-Term Construction

The credit allocation question. Credit sits between equities and Treasuries in the risk spectrum and correlates with both; its contribution to a portfolio is the spread carry and the diversification against rates. In the richest decile, the carry is thin and the drawdown risk is full. The framework's structural view is that a credit allocation is sized to the cycle position — largest when spreads are in the widest quartile and standards are tightening (the bottom), smallest when spreads are in the richest decile and standards are easy (now) — and that the current configuration argues for a below-neutral allocation with the carry sacrificed for the optionality of adding at wider levels.

Floating versus fixed through the cycle. Floating-rate credit outperforms in hiking cycles and underperforms in cutting cycles, mechanically. It also carries the cost-of-capital risk to the borrower that fixed-rate credit defers. A rotation from floating toward fixed as the Fed's next move shifts from hike to cut is the standard expression; the current hiking debate argues for floating with an explicit view on the borrower stress it implies.

Underwriting a private-credit manager. For an investor considering private-credit exposure, the questions that matter are not the yield or the track record but the marks. Specifically: the proportion of the portfolio marked by third-party valuation firms versus internally; the cross-sectional dispersion of the manager's marks against peers on comparable credits (PIMCO's test); the PIK share of income and its trend; the non-accrual policy and its consistency; the share of the portfolio in amend-and-extend; the redemption terms and the queue; and — decisively — whether the manager's listed vehicle trades at a discount, which is the only market check available. A manager whose non-traded vehicle reports smooth returns while its listed vehicle trades at a 20% discount is a manager whose marks the market has already voted on.

Asset-based finance as the diversifier. Lending against receivables, equipment, royalties, and consumer assets — the segment PIMCO and Morgan Stanley both identified as the alternative to corporate direct lending — has collateral that is marked to observable values and a risk profile that does not depend on sponsor support. It is the private-credit allocation that the marking problem affects least.

Rebalancing discipline. Additions to credit conditioned on the Part VI signals — specifically, on spreads widening and standards beginning to ease from a tight reading, which is the bottom's signature. The framework exists so that "spreads are wide, buy credit" is conditioned on whether the cycle has actually turned, which spreads alone cannot say.

29. Rates as a Trade, Not a Policy

Rates & Liquidity explains the machine — the regime, the Fed's and Treasury's instruments, the plumbing. This section is about expressing a view on what the machine produces. It belongs in the credit paper because credit is where the need is most acute: IG is duration in a credit costume (Section 5), the refinancing wall is a rate schedule (Section 22), and the 30-year Treasury at 5.33% — its highest since 2007 — is the variable that ran through the currency paper's fiscal-dominance signature, the volatility paper's MOVE read, and the metals paper's real-yield model in the same week. The long yield is the book's common bus. A book that reads it in five papers should be able to trade it in one.

The curve, 27 August 2026

Two-year 4.20%, ten-year 4.67%, thirty-year 5.19% after a 5.33% intraweek print. Two-year-to-ten-year at 47 basis points — positive after the longest inversion in the series (July 2022 to late 2024), but well short of the 100–150 of a healthy expansion. Ten-year-to-thirty-year at roughly 52, near its widest since 2021. Fed funds at 3.50–3.75% with the two-year above the policy rate, which means the front end is pricing hikes. This is a bear steepener led from the long end: the short end has repriced for a hawkish Fed, and the long end has repriced by more — for supply, term premium, and the fiscal trajectory — despite it. That combination is the curve's own statement about the regime, and every trade below is a way of agreeing or disagreeing with it.

Duration as a position

Duration is the sensitivity of a bond's price to its yield; DV01 — the dollar change per basis point — is the unit in which rates positions are sized, and it is the only unit in which a two-year, a ten-year, and a thirty-year position can be compared. A \$1 million thirty-year at 5.19% carries roughly six times the DV01 of a \$1 million two-year; equal notionals are not equal positions.

Instruments. Treasury futures — ZT (2-year), ZF (5-year), ZN (10-year), TN (ultra 10), ZB (bond), UB (ultra bond) — are the cleanest and most capital-efficient expression, deep enough for any size this book will run, and governed by the delivery mechanics in the basis subsection below. Cash Treasuries for holding. ETFs — SHY, IEF, TLT, and the ultra-long variants — for convenience, with the caveat that TLT's duration drifts with its holdings and the leveraged variants (TMF, TBT) decay on volatility the way the energy paper's futures products decay on roll. Interest-rate swaps for institutions, and swaptions as the options layer.

The convexity asymmetry. Long-duration positions gain more from a yield fall than they lose from an equal yield rise; the effect is material at thirty years and it is the reason a long-end long has an embedded option that a long-end short pays for. Sizing a short at the long end by DV01 alone understates its tail.

Steepeners and flatteners

A curve trade is a DV01-neutral pair — long one tenor, short another, sized so that a parallel shift produces no profit or loss — that isolates the shape of the curve from its level. It is the trade that expresses a regime view without a directional rate bet.

The four variants, and what each is a bet on:

Trade Construction Wins when The regime it expresses
Bull steepener Long front, short back; front rallies more Fed cuts into weakness Recession, Fed capitulation
Bear steepener Long front, short back; back sells off more Long end reprices for supply and term premium Fiscal dominance, inflation risk, foreign demand fading
Bull flattener Short front, long back; back rallies more Long end rallies on growth fear while Fed holds Flight to duration, disinflation
Bear flattener Short front, long back; front sells off more Fed hikes while the long end is anchored Credible tightening

Tenor choice matters. Two-year-to-ten-year is the policy-versus-growth spread and the recession gauge. Five-year-to-thirty-year and ten-year-to-thirty-year isolate the long end — the term-premium and supply story — from the policy story, and they are where the 2026 move has been concentrated. A view that "the fiscal trajectory reprices the long end regardless of what the Fed does" is a 10s30s steepener, not a 2s10s one; the latter would be fighting a hawkish front end it does not need to fight.

Carry and roll-down. A curve position has carry — the net coupon and financing of the two legs — and roll-down — the gain or loss as each leg ages along the curve. In a positively sloped curve a steepener typically has negative carry (the long front leg yields less than the short back leg costs to borrow) and the position must be right about the shape within a horizon the carry allows. The steeper the curve, the more the steepener pays to wait; this is the discipline that separates a curve trade from a curve opinion.

The 2026 configuration. The bear steepener has been the fiscal-dominance trade, and it has paid: the long end rose while the Fed turned hawkish, which is the combination the Currencies paper flags as the creditor-confidence signature. Its two failure modes are the two regime changes in that paper: a Fed that capitulates (the trade becomes a bull steepener — still a steepener, different leg leading) and a Fed that hikes credibly enough to anchor the long end (a bear flattener, which is the trade's loss).

Term premium

The term premium is the excess yield investors demand for holding a long bond over rolling short bonds — compensation for uncertainty about the rate path — and it is the component of the long yield that policy does not directly set. Estimates (the New York Fed's ACM model, the Fed Board's Kim–Wright) decompose the ten-year into an expected-rate path and a residual; the residual was negative for most of the 2010s and has been rising since 2022. It is the long yield's fiscal channel.

Expressing a term-premium view. Three ways, in rising purity. A 10s30s or 5s30s steepener, which captures term premium at the long end while netting out the policy path. A short in long Treasuries against a received position in swaps of the same maturity — a swap-spread trade, below — which isolates the Treasury-supply component from the general level of long rates. And long-dated payer swaptions or TLT puts, which express the view with defined risk and are cheapest when MOVE is low — which is the volatility paper's point about convexity being cheap when it seems unnecessary.

The suppressant to watch. Treasury's bond buyback programme, doubled to \$4 billion a month in the week the thirty-year touched 5.33%, and the bill-heavy issuance strategy are the policy levers against term premium; X explains the mechanics. A term-premium long is a bet that the fiscal arithmetic overwhelms those levers; its cross-check is the yields-up/dollar-down signature and the auction metrics in the currency paper's Tier 1.

TIPS and breakevens

A nominal yield decomposes into a real yield plus an inflation compensation; Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities trade the first, and the difference between a nominal and a TIPS of the same maturity — the breakeven — trades the second. This gives the book two more instruments than the nominal curve alone: a view on real rates (which is the metals paper's gold variable) and a view on inflation expectations (which is the energy paper's transmission variable).

Trades. Long TIPS against short nominals of the same maturity is long breakevens — a bet that inflation compensation is too low. The reverse is a bet that the market is over-pricing inflation. Long TIPS outright is a real-duration position: it profits if real yields fall regardless of inflation. The instruments are cash TIPS, TIP and SCHP (broad), STIP (short), and LTPZ (long); TIPS futures are not liquid, so breakeven trades are cash-versus-futures or cash-versus-cash.

The current read. CPI at 3.4% on an energy shock, breakevens elevated but anchored well below spot inflation — the market is pricing the shock as transitory. That gap is itself the trade: a long-breakeven position is a bet the energy paper's structural-power-inflation floor is real; a short is a bet the ceasefire resolves it. And real yields — the ten-year TIPS yield near the top of its post-2008 range — are the reason the metals paper's gold model "should" be working and is not; a real-duration long is the rates-market expression of the view that it will start to.

Cautions. TIPS liquidity is thinner than nominals and vanishes in stress — in March 2020 breakevens collapsed on liquidity, not on deflation. The inflation accrual is taxable annually. Seasonality in the CPI index produces predictable carry patterns in short TIPS that trade on.

Swap spreads

A swap spread is the fixed rate on an interest-rate swap minus the Treasury yield of the same maturity. In a textbook it is positive — a bank's credit over the sovereign's. At the long end it has been persistently and deeply negative for a decade: Treasuries yield more than swaps, which means the market charges a premium to hold the government's bond over a bank-counterparty contract.

Why. Supply and balance sheet. Treasuries must be held by someone with balance sheet, and post-2008 leverage rules (the supplementary leverage ratio) make that balance sheet expensive; swaps carry no such cost. When Treasury issuance rises faster than the capacity to warehouse it, Treasuries cheapen relative to swaps and the spread goes more negative. The Treasury's own borrowing advisory committee documented the pre-2008 correlation between deficits and long-dated swap spreads; the relationship broke under the zero-rate regime and has reasserted itself with the supply.

Reading and trading it. As a gauge: a long-end swap spread going more negative is Treasury supply overwhelming dealer capacity — a funding-plumbing signal that belongs beside the SOFR–IORB and basis measures in X. As a trade: a spread widener (long Treasuries, pay fixed in swaps) is a bet the cheapening reverses — through buybacks, through regulatory relief on the leverage ratio, through issuance shifting to bills; a tightener is the fiscal-dominance expression stripped of the general rate level. The instruments are institutional — futures versus swaps — and for this book the swap spread is primarily a read, and the clean way to express its direction without a swap line is the term-premium structures above.

Futures basis and roll

Treasury futures settle by delivery of a bond from an eligible basket, and the seller chooses which — the cheapest-to-deliver. The futures price tracks the CTD, adjusted by a conversion factor; the difference between the cash bond's price and the futures-implied price is the basis, and the basis embeds the financing cost to the delivery date (the implied repo rate) plus the value of the seller's delivery options. This matters for the book in three ways.

Which bond you are actually trading. A ZB or UB position is a position in the CTD, whose maturity and duration can jump when the CTD switches. A "thirty-year" view expressed in ZB may be a position in a twenty-year bond; UB is the contract that tracks the true long end. Know the CTD before sizing the DV01.

The roll. Futures expire quarterly; a held position rolls from the front contract to the next, and the calendar spread between them prices the change in CTD, financing, and delivery options. The roll is usually a small cost or gain, but it concentrates in the days before first notice and is where a position can be caught by a CTD switch. The equity-vol paper's roll discipline applies: know the calendar, roll early, and treat a mispriced calendar spread as information about financing stress.

The basis trade as a systemic variable. Hedge funds run a cash-futures basis trade — long the cash bond, short the future, financed in repo, levered fifty to a hundred times — estimated at roughly a trillion dollars. It is the marginal buyer of Treasury issuance and the marginal provider of futures liquidity, and it is the position that unwound in March 2020 when repo financing seized, contributing to the Treasury-market dysfunction that forced the Fed's intervention. A widening basis — futures cheapening to cash beyond what financing explains — is the signature of that position under stress, and it is a leading indicator for the volatility paper's MOVE read and the currency paper's funding measures. For the book: watch the ten-year and bond basis as a plumbing gauge, and treat a basis blowout as a regime signal rather than a trade.

Connecting rates to the credit book

Hedging duration out of credit. An IG position (LQD, cash IG) is roughly seven years of duration and eighty basis points of spread; a short in ZN or TN sized to the DV01 leaves the spread and removes the rate. CDX IG achieves the same result with no hedge required. The choice between cash-plus-hedge and CDX is a basis decision — the CDX-cash basis is its own gauge of credit-market liquidity, tightening in calm and blowing out in stress.

The credit-rates correlation is a regime variable. In 2022 credit spreads widened with rising rates — a rates-driven credit selloff, and the year the duration hedge would have doubled the loss in a position that thought it was hedged. In 2007 spreads widened as rates fell — the classic flight-to-quality, where duration hedges the credit. The current configuration — long end rising, spreads in the richest decile — is closer to 2022's setup than 2007's, which argues for the CDX expression over the hedged-cash one.

Sizing and risk

A DV01 budget — the dollar loss the book accepts per basis point of parallel shift, and separately per basis point of curve twist — is the discipline, and it should be set before any tenor is chosen. Rates realise 5–8% annualised in price terms at ten years and 12–18% at thirty; the thirty-year's price volatility is now comparable to an equity index's, and it should be sized as such. Event risk concentrates in the refunding announcements (quarterly), the CPI and FOMC calendar, and — the 2026 addition — the auction tails and buyback announcements that the currency paper's Tier 1 tracks. The tail to size for is the yields-up/dollar-down day, when the long end sells off with everything else and the duration that was supposed to be the hedge is the loss.

34. False Signals

The aggregate spread level. In the richest decile while the dual-track default rate runs several points above the headline. It is the lagging indicator of a cycle whose leading indicators are elsewhere.

The headline default rate. Structurally suppressed by LME rerouting. Use the dual-track rate.

"Record low defaults" during extend-and-pretend. A loan that has been extended has not defaulted and has not been repaid. The maturity-wall statistics are the honest read.

Private-credit reported returns. Manager-marked, lagged, and — on the evidence — not anchored to a common value. Reported returns are a description of the marks, not of the credits.

Non-accruals from a single manager. One manager's 0.6% is that manager's policy. The category-wide trend is the signal.

The BBB cliff. Forecast every year since 2016; has not arrived at scale because BBB issuers have had access to refinancing and because the fallen-angel volume in 2020 was absorbed by the Fed's facilities. The mechanism is real; the timing calls have all been wrong.

Index composition comparisons across eras. The HY index is higher-quality than in 2007; the loan market contains more loan-only issuers than in 2007; private credit did not exist at scale in 2007. Spread comparisons across eras compare different populations.

"Private credit has lower volatility." It has lower reported volatility because it is marked quarterly by the holder. The volatility of the underlying credits is not lower; it is unobserved.

PART VII — FORECAST RANGES

35. The Track Record

Credit forecasting has a specific failure mode: default-rate forecasts have been persistently too high during the expansion and persistently too low at the turn, and in the current cycle they have been measuring the wrong thing.

The expansion bias. From 2023 through 2025, consensus leveraged-loan default forecasts ran in the 3–5% range on the logic of the floating-rate cost shock. Realised payment defaults ended 2025 near 1.2% by amount — at the optimistic end of the range. The forecasts were not wrong about the stress; they were wrong about where it would show up. The stress was real and it was routed through LMEs, whose count peaked near 37 on a trailing basis in mid-2025. A forecaster measuring the dual-track rate was roughly right; one measuring the payment rate was wrong by a factor of three.

The turn bias. In every prior cycle, consensus default forecasts at the top of the cycle underestimated the subsequent peak: forecasts of 4–6% in 2007 against a realised 13% in 2009; forecasts near 3% in 2000 against a realised 10%. The underestimate is structural — forecasts are extrapolations of the recent rate, and the recent rate at the top is the trough.

The private-credit gap. There is no consensus default forecast for private credit because there is no consensus default data — the segment's defaults are manager-reported non-accruals, and the managers' incentives run one direction. Cliffwater's index reports realised losses; the Boston Fed's PIK tracking is the closest thing to a leading series; and the honest position is that the forecast track record for this segment does not exist because the segment has not been through a cycle in its current form.

What forecasts are good for. The dispersion of forecasts across houses is a measure of uncertainty; the revisions with stated reasons are a measure of what mechanism is moving; and the gap between payment-default forecasts and dual-track forecasts is a measure of how much of the cycle the forecaster expects to be rerouted.

PART VII-B — THE TEN-YEAR VIEW

38. Private Credit Through a Full Cycle

The question the decade answers: what happens to a \$2 trillion, manager-marked, retail-funded, insurance-held asset class in a sustained default cycle.

Orderly resolution (~40%). The 2026 redemption wave was the stress test; the caps held; the marking lag resolves through restructurings and equity conversions over several years; realised losses land in the mid-single digits, comparable to a bad public-HY cycle; the retail channel shrinks; the institutional and insurance channels persist; and the asset class emerges smaller, more concentrated, and with marks that are subject to a standardised third-party process. Private credit becomes a mature, cyclical segment like leveraged loans.

The disorderly episode (~35%). A sustained downturn produces losses at the syndicated-middle-market historical rate — low teens — concentrated in the 2020–2021 vintage. The marking lag resolves all at once when a large manager's marks are forced down by an external event (a bank facility's mark trigger, a regulatory examination, a lawsuit). A second redemption wave meets caps that cannot hold; a large non-traded vehicle gates entirely; the listed-BDC discounts reach 40%. Insurance regulators reclassify private-credit holdings; a bank with concentrated NDFI exposure takes a loss that becomes a supervisory event. The asset class survives but the retail channel is closed by regulation, and the "lower volatility" claim is retired.

The regulatory settlement (~25%). Before a crisis forces it, regulators impose marking standards, redemption-term limits, and disclosure requirements on the retail vehicles; the SLOOS NDFI questions become capital requirements; and the asset class is brought into a supervisory perimeter that removes the marking discretion. Losses are realised gradually and the structure is reformed without an event.

The observable that discriminates. Whether cross-sectional mark dispersion across managers falls — which means marks are converging on a common value, which means the lag is resolving. Rising dispersion means it is not.

39. The Bank-Versus-Non-Bank Settlement

Where does lending live in 2036? The migration of Section 4 was driven by regulation; its reversal or extension will be too.

Non-bank dominance persists (~50%). Banks remain constrained; private credit and the securitised markets are the marginal lenders; banks are the wholesale funders of the non-banks through leverage facilities. The system is more fragile because the lenders of last resort — the Fed's facilities — cover banks and IG credit but not the non-bank lenders where the cycle now lives.

Partial reintermediation (~30%). A private-credit episode and the regulatory response push middle-market lending partly back to banks, with revised capital rules that make it viable. Private credit shrinks to its institutional core.

Regulatory convergence (~20%). Non-banks are regulated as banks are — capital, liquidity, marking — and the distinction erodes.

40. The CRE Reset

How long does the commercial real-estate workout take, and where do the losses land?

Slow workout (~55%). Three to five years; extensions convert gradually to discounted payoffs and foreclosures as lenders' patience and reserves allow; office losses of 30–50% on affected loans absorbed by regional banks, B-pieces, and debt funds; no systemic event; the sector's capital base is rebuilt through new equity at reset valuations.

Acceleration (~30%). A rise in unemployment or a regional-bank event forces lenders to stop extending; foreclosures cluster; the loss recognition that has been deferred arrives in two years rather than five; a wave of regional-bank capital raises and consolidations.

Muddle-through (~15%). Rates fall enough that the maturity wall refinances; office losses are contained to the worst assets; the reset is smaller than feared.

The observable. The extension-versus-foreclosure share on maturing loans, monthly, from the special servicers.

SLOOS releases (early February, May, August, November). FOMC decisions and the rate path — the floating-rate cohort's most important variable. Quarterly BDC filings (mid-May, mid-August, mid-November, mid-March). The 2027–2028 maturity schedule for the 2020–2021 vintage. Regulatory actions on private-credit marking, redemption terms, and bank NDFI exposure. Unemployment.

Falsification Tests

"The credit cycle is a lending-standards cycle" is falsified if a spread widening of more than 200 basis points occurs without SLOOS having tightened in the prior four quarters and without dispersion having widened first — an exogenous shock excepted.

"Dispersion is the signal" is falsified if the BB-CCC spread and the listed-BDC discount widen materially and no aggregate deterioration follows within four quarters — in which case the tail was contained and the aggregate was right.

"Credit leads equities in endogenous downturns" is falsified by an endogenous equity drawdown of more than 15% that credit did not precede on any of the Section 9 signals.

"The headline default rate is structurally suppressed" is falsified if the LME count falls toward zero with the distress ratio also falling — meaning the rerouting has ended because the distress has.

"Private-credit marks are not anchored" is falsified if cross-sectional dispersion falls to public-market levels without accompanying mark-downs — meaning managers have converged on a common value that the market accepts.

"The 2020–2021 vintage is the stress cohort" is falsified if it refinances through 2028 at spreads within 100 basis points of origination.

"The 90/10 split is real" is falsified if the 10% shrinks to 5% without realised losses — the distressed cohort recovering — or grows to 20%, in which case it was not a tail.

Cadence Summary

Weekly: BB-CCC, HY-IG ratio, acceleration, divergence, listed-BDC discounts, fund flows, primary-market health.

Monthly: LCD default report (dual-track, LME count, distress ratio), CLO data, Trepp CMBS, consumer series.

Quarterly: SLOOS on release; BDC filings for PIK, non-accruals, redemptions, deployment, and the mark-dispersion analysis; rewrite the scenario probabilities; check the falsification tests explicitly.

After any event: a restructuring, a gating, a bank NDFI loss, or a regulatory action — record it against the mechanisms in Parts II and III and ask which one it validated.

APPENDICES

Appendix A: Glossary

Amend-and-extend — Modifying a loan's terms at maturity rather than refinancing or foreclosing; defers loss recognition.

BDC (business development company) — Regulated vehicle for private lending; listed BDCs trade on exchanges, non-traded BDCs offer capped periodic repurchases.

CDX — Credit default swap indices (IG and HY); pure-spread instruments with no duration.

CLO (collateralised loan obligation) — Structured vehicle holding leveraged loans and issuing tranched liabilities; equity tranche takes first loss.

Covenant-lite — A loan without maintenance covenants; the lender has surrendered its early-warning rights.

Distress ratio — Share of loans trading below 80 cents; the LME pipeline indicator.

Dual-track default rate — Default rate including liability-management exercises as well as payment defaults; the honest series.

Basis (Treasury futures) — Cash bond price minus futures-implied price; embeds financing (implied repo) and delivery options. A blowout is a funding-stress signal.

Breakeven — Nominal yield minus TIPS yield of the same maturity; the market's inflation compensation.

Cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) — The bond a futures seller will deliver from the eligible basket; determines which bond a futures position actually is.

DV01 — Dollar value of one basis point; the unit in which rates positions are sized and compared across tenors.

Fallen angel — An issuer downgraded from investment grade to high yield.

LME (liability management exercise) — Out-of-court restructuring imposing losses on some lenders without a payment default; drop-downs, uptiers, priming.

NDFI (non-depository financial institution) — Non-bank lender; the SLOOS now surveys bank lending to them.

Non-accrual — A loan on which the manager has stopped accruing interest income; the determination is the manager's.

OAS (option-adjusted spread) — Spread over Treasuries adjusted for embedded options.

PIK (payment-in-kind) — Interest paid by issuing additional principal rather than cash; income is reported, cash is not received.

Priming — New debt inserted senior to existing first-lien holders.

SLOOS — The Fed's Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey; the earliest credit-cycle signal.

Steepener / flattener — DV01-neutral curve trades isolating shape from level; bull/bear variants by which leg leads.

Swap spread — Swap fixed rate minus Treasury yield of the same maturity; negative at the long end when Treasury supply exceeds balance-sheet capacity.

Term premium — The excess yield demanded for holding a long bond over rolling short bonds; the long yield's fiscal channel.

Closing Note

The credit cycle turns first because the lender is the first to say no, and it turns invisibly when the saying-no has been rerouted through restructurings that are not defaults and through marks that are not prices. The current cycle has both. A spread of 269 basis points is describing a market that, on the dual-track rate, the distress ratio, the PIK trend, and the BDC discounts, does not exist as an average — it exists as a healthy majority and a deteriorating tail, and the framework's job is to watch the tail.

The practical consequence is a discipline: run the timing signals on the dispersion series, not the level; treat lending standards as the leading indicator they have always been; read private-credit filings for the tells rather than the returns; and hold the credit allocation to the cycle position rather than to the carry. In the richest decile, with standards easy in the most leveraged segment and the honest default rate several points above the headline, the cycle position is late — whatever the spread says.
