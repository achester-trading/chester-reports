Front matter

Reader's note

The Equities paper describes the residual claim, the index as a momentum machine, factors, and the American concentration. It is a paper about one market. This one covers the other third of the world's equity capitalization and the two sessions that trade before the New York open.

Three reasons it exists, in ascending order of importance to this book.

The allocation is a decision whether or not it is made. The United States is roughly sixty-five percent of global equity capitalization, and a portfolio holding only U.S. index exposure has made an active bet of thirty-five percentage points against the global market portfolio. That may well be the right bet. It is not a neutral one, and the Doctrine requires that an active position have a thesis, a counterparty, and an invalidation.

The overnight session is not American. A meaningful share of the S&P's cumulative return has accrued outside regular trading hours, and the price the 07:00 report reads has already been through a Tokyo session and most of a European one. A framework that explains the U.S. day and treats the overnight gap as weather is a framework with a hole in the middle of its morning.

Three of the system's live concerns are non-U.S. mechanisms. The yen carry monitor is a Japanese policy story. The AI supply chain the speculative-cohort monitor tracks runs through Taiwan and Korea before it reaches Dell. And the tail watch's most consequential geopolitical scenario is a Taiwan contingency, which is a question about two Asian equity markets and one semiconductor company before it is a question about anything else.

The paper is organized to be read once for the map and consulted thereafter by market. Part I is the map and the home-bias arithmetic. Part II is the fifty-five-year record of alternating leadership and the decomposition of the current cycle, because the decomposition — not the fact — is what tells you whether it can continue. Part III is the currency term, which for a dollar-based holder is frequently the largest term. Part IV treats the developed markets one at a time and Part V gives China a part of its own, because it is the one market where the economic and the equity story have diverged for two decades. Part VI is instruments and their traps, which is where most retail money is actually lost in international investing. Part VII is correlation — its long rise, its regime dependence, its asymmetry in drawdowns, and the short-horizon measurement artifacts that make daily correlation misleading. Part VIII is a ten-year probabilistic view of relative returns, written as a distribution with stated inputs rather than as a forecast. Part IX is the wiring into the reports.

Three readings. The U.S. share is at or near its highest since the early 1970s, and its rise over the past fifteen years is the single largest fact in global equity allocation. China's index weight is a small fraction of its economic weight, because most Chinese equity capital is in domestic A-shares that global indices include only partially, and because the state holds much of what is listed. And the ratio of capitalization to GDP varies by a factor of five across these rows, which is a statement about financial structure — how much of an economy's activity is publicly listed rather than privately or state-held — and not a statement about value.

1.2 The historical volatility of the map

The current U.S. share is not a permanent feature. Japan reached roughly forty-five percent of world equity capitalization at its 1989 peak, at which point the argument for a Japan-heavy portfolio was as compelling on trailing evidence as the argument for a U.S.-heavy portfolio is today, and rested on the same evidence: superior returns, superior corporate performance, and a structural story. The United Kingdom was the dominant market before 1914. A market's share of global capitalization is a momentum series, and it mean-reverts on a clock longer than a career. The paper does not draw a forecast from this; it draws a warning about the confidence with which the current map is treated as the natural order.

Chapter 2 — Home bias, and what it costs

Every country's investors overweight their own market relative to its global weight, by margins that no rational model explains — the classic home-bias puzzle. The traditional justifications (currency risk, information asymmetry, taxes) account for only a fraction of the observed tilt.

For a U.S.-based investor the puzzle has an unusual feature: home bias has been rewarded for fifteen years, and rewarded so consistently that the discipline required to hold international exposure has been continuously punished. This is the fact the paper has to handle honestly, because the wrong lesson from it is either "international never works" or "the reversal is overdue." Chapter 3 is the decomposition that lets a reader hold a view rather than a slogan.

The cost of the bias when it is wrong is visible in the Japanese case: a Japanese investor at one hundred percent home allocation in 1989 experienced the p95 outcome of Base Rates Chapter 13 — a thirty-four-year wait for the nominal high — while a globally diversified Japanese investor experienced an ordinary bad decade. Diversification's payoff is not higher expected return; it is the elimination of the single-country tail.

Part II — The Record, and Why the United States Won

Chapter 3 — The record: relative performance since 1970

3.1 Leadership alternates, and the cycles are long

The single most useful fact about international relative performance is that it is cyclical on a decadal clock, and that each cycle has lasted long enough for an investing career to mistake it for a permanent condition.

Period Annualized, U.S. large cap Annualized, developed international (USD) Leader What drove it
1970–1978 ~6% ~10% International Bretton Woods collapse, dollar devaluation, U.S. stagflation and multiple compression
1979–1988 ~17% ~22% International The Japanese bubble's ascent; the Plaza Accord's dollar reversal after 1985
1989–1999 ~18% ~7% U.S. Japan's collapse, U.S. productivity boom, the technology cycle, a strong dollar
2000–2007 ~2% ~7% International The U.S. "lost decade," the commodity supercycle, emerging-market industrialization, a falling dollar
2010–2024 ~13.5% ~5.5% U.S. Technology and platform earnings, buybacks, relative multiple expansion, a fifteen-year dollar bull market
2025– in progress in progress unresolved Dollar weakness, European fiscal impulse, Japanese reform — the first sustained challenge to the 2010s pattern

Source class: computed from standard index series in dollar terms; approximate, and sensitive to the exact index pair and endpoints chosen.

Five regime flips in fifty-five years, with an average run of roughly nine to twelve years and no regularity precise enough to time. The current U.S. run is the longest and the widest of the five. By the mid-2020s the trailing ten-year gap in favor of U.S. equities had reached the widest level in the modern record — which is a fact about the starting point of any ten-year projection and is why Part V treats it explicitly.

3.2 What the cycles have in common

Each flip had the same three ingredients, in varying proportions, and they are the same three components the next chapter decomposes: a currency turn, a relative multiple that had reached an extreme, and a change in which region's earnings were growing. The 1989 flip to U.S. leadership had all three; the 2000 flip to international leadership had all three; the 2010 flip back had all three. No flip in the sample has occurred on valuation alone, which is the single most important caution for anyone reading today's valuation gap as a signal.

3.3 Rolling relative performance, and where today sits

Measured as the trailing ten-year annualized difference between U.S. and developed-international returns in dollars, the series has spent most of its history between roughly minus five and plus five percentage points a year, with excursions beyond that in the late 1980s (international far ahead) and the 2020s (U.S. far ahead). The recent reading of roughly seven to eight points a year in the U.S. favor is at or beyond the historical envelope.

Two readings, and they are in tension. The mean-reverting reading: an extreme in a mean-reverting series is information about the direction of the next move, if not its timing. The regime reading: the series is not stationary, the American index of 2026 is a genuinely different asset from the American index of 1990 — far more concentrated in a sector with structurally higher returns on capital — and an extreme may be a re-rating rather than an excursion. The paper does not resolve the tension; Part V prices it as a probability rather than settling it as a view.

Chapter 4 — The three questions the record leaves open

The record supports three claims and refutes a fourth, and separating them is what allows a position rather than an opinion.

Supported: leadership alternates. Five flips, no permanent winner, average runs of a decade.

Supported: flips require a catalyst, and valuation alone has never been one. The dispersion of the valuation gap around the flip dates is wide; the presence of a currency turn and an earnings-trajectory change at every flip is universal in the sample.

Supported: the currency term is a first-order component, not a rounding error. It appears in every flip and accounts for a substantial minority of every gap.

Refuted: "the U.S. always wins." The claim is a fifteen-year sample presented as a structural fact, and the 1970s, 1980s, and 2000s are the counterexamples. The honest version of the bull case is narrower and stronger: the U.S. hosts a disproportionate share of the industries with the highest returns on invested capital, and that composition is durable. That claim is testable, and Part V tests it against its alternatives.

Chapter 5 — Decomposing fifteen years of outperformance

From roughly 2010 through the mid-2020s, U.S. equities outperformed developed international equities by something on the order of five to six percentage points a year in dollar terms — one of the widest and longest such gaps on record. The decomposition matters more than the number, because each component has a different persistence.

Component Approximate contribution Persistence
Currency — dollar appreciation against the euro, yen, and sterling Roughly a quarter to a third of the gap Mean-reverting on a long cycle; the dollar has had multi-year moves of thirty percent in both directions
Multiple expansion — the U.S. price-to-earnings ratio rose relative to the rest Roughly a third Not repeatable indefinitely; a rising relative multiple is borrowing return from the future
Earnings growth — genuine superiority in earnings per share Roughly a third The durable component, and the one the bull case rests on
Sector composition — the U.S. index is technology-heavy, the European bank- and industrial-heavy Embedded in the earnings and multiple terms Structural; changes slowly
Buybacks — share-count reduction as a component of EPS growth Material within the earnings term Structural, and now spreading to Japan and Korea

Source class: computed and cited; approximate, and the split varies by period and index pair.

The reading. Two of the three components are not the kind of thing that repeats. Currency contributions reverse; relative multiple expansion cannot compound forever without the multiple becoming arbitrary. If the next fifteen years look like the last, it will be because the earnings component alone carries it — which is a much stronger claim than the trailing performance implies, and a testable one.

5.1 The valuation gap, and how much of it is real

Non-U.S. developed equities trade at a persistent and wide discount to U.S. equities on headline multiples. A large share of that gap is sector composition: an index that is twenty percent banks and light on software should trade at a lower multiple, and comparing headline P/Es across differently composed indices is the most common error in the international debate. Sector-adjusted, the discount narrows substantially — but it does not vanish, and the residual is the honest object of the discussion. Its candidate explanations are lower returns on equity, weaker capital discipline, fragmented markets, governance, and a structural cost of capital difference. Which of those are changing is the subject of Part IV, market by market.

Part III — The Currency Term

Chapter 6 — For a dollar holder, half the decision is not about equities

6.1 The arithmetic

An unhedged dollar-based holder of a foreign equity earns the local return plus the currency's move against the dollar. Over one-year horizons the currency term is frequently larger than the equity term; over ten-year horizons it is smaller but still material, and it has historically contributed a substantial share of the difference between hedged and unhedged international index returns.

The consequence is that an international allocation is two decisions wearing one ticker: a view on foreign equities and a view on the dollar. The Doctrine's Rule 11 — the thesis and the instrument are two decisions — applies with unusual force, because the default instrument silently bundles a macro bet the operator may not have intended.

6.2 Hedged or unhedged

Unhedged Hedged
What you own Foreign equities plus a short-dollar position Foreign equities
Cost None explicit; the currency's volatility is added The interest-rate differential, paid or received, plus a small implementation cost
Works when The dollar weakens The dollar strengthens, or when the differential pays you
Volatility effect Usually raises portfolio volatility for a dollar holder in developed markets Usually lowers it
Correlation quirk Foreign currencies often fall when global equities fall, so unhedged international is a doubly pro-cyclical exposure Removes the second cycle

The rate differential is not a detail. When U.S. short rates are well above foreign short rates, a dollar-based investor hedging a foreign currency earns the differential, which for several recent years made hedged international exposure meaningfully cheaper to hold than the headline suggests. When the differential inverts, so does the carry.

The paper's ruling for Book A: the international sleeve's hedging choice is an explicit, recorded decision at the monthly session, not a property of whichever ETF was convenient. It carries its own thesis line and its own invalidation. And a hedged and an unhedged version of the same market are two different instruments in the register, never interchangeable.

Part IV — The Developed Markets

Chapter 7 — Europe

What the index is. Financials, industrials, health care, energy, and the luxury and consumer-staples complex; technology is a small share, and the largest European technology exposure is a single semiconductor-equipment firm and a handful of software names. Any comparison to the U.S. index that does not adjust for this is comparing two different economies' listed sectors and calling it valuation.

The structural constraints. A monetary union without a fiscal union, which means the central bank's transmission runs through nineteen sovereign credit curves and periodically confronts fragmentation — the spread of peripheral over core sovereign yields is the region's real financial-conditions gauge, and the reason the central bank has repeatedly built anti-fragmentation tools. Energy import dependence, which converts an energy shock into a terms-of-trade shock in a way it is not for the United States. A demographic profile older than America's. Fragmented capital markets: no single deep equity market, a savings pool that flows disproportionately into deposits and bonds, and a persistent complaint that the region's best growth companies list elsewhere.

What has changed and is worth watching. The defense and infrastructure spending shift following the security reassessment of the 2020s is the largest fiscal impulse the region has directed at a single sector in decades, and it lands on industrials and defense primes rather than on the index's traditional leadership. Banks, after a fifteen-year deleveraging, entered the higher-rate era with capital and began returning it — a European bank sector paying out meaningfully is a different asset from the one that spent 2010–2020 raising equity. And the "capital markets union" agenda, if it ever advances, is the structural reform that would matter most, which is precisely why it has not advanced.

How to read it in the reports. European equities are the cleanest large-cap expression of the global industrial cycle and of the value factor; the region's index is a rate-sensitive, cycle-sensitive, energy-sensitive instrument, and it behaves like one. Its overnight session sets a meaningful share of the U.S. opening gap.

Chapter 8 — The United Kingdom

Treated separately because its index is not European in composition: energy, materials, banks, pharmaceuticals, and consumer staples, with a large share of revenues earned outside the country, which makes the index a currency-inverse instrument — sterling weakness tends to lift it. It has traded at a discount to both the U.S. and Europe for years, and has been a persistent source of takeover activity as a result — which is itself a Book D observation: a market at a durable discount with an open takeover regime produces a steady flow of event-driven situations.

Chapter 9 — Japan

The market with the most genuine structural change of the past decade, and the one most connected to the system's existing work.

The governance reform is real and is the thesis. The exchange's request that companies trading below book value publish concrete plans to improve capital efficiency — followed by record buybacks, dividend increases, the unwinding of cross-shareholdings, and a wave of management buyouts and activist campaigns — is a genuine change in the disposition of Japanese corporate cash, which had been the structural argument against the market for thirty years. The index regained its 1989 high in early 2024 after thirty-four years. The question for the next decade is whether the reform continues into the smaller-cap tail, where the discounts and the cash piles are largest.

Deflation exit and policy normalization is the macro variable, and it is the one that connects to the rest of the system. The central bank's exit from negative rates and yield-curve control ended a two-decade regime, and its path is now the funding-currency variable in the carry monitor. The link runs both ways: policy normalization strengthens the yen, a stronger yen is a headwind for the exporter-heavy index and a trigger for carry unwinds, and a carry unwind is a global equity event. Japanese equities and the yen are a single trade with two legs, which makes the hedging decision of Chapter 4 more consequential here than anywhere else — an unhedged dollar holder of Japanese equities owns two positions that partially cancel.

What to watch: the wage-price cycle and whether the annual wage round sustains the inflation exit; the pace of cross-shareholding unwind; the buyback run-rate; the ratio of companies still below one times book; and the central bank's balance-sheet policy, which remains the largest domestic holder of the equity market through its exchange-traded-fund holdings and whose eventual disposal is a supply overhang with a decades-long horizon.

Part V — China

Chapter 10 — China: three markets, two economies, and whether winning the industry means earning a return

China gets a full part rather than a chapter because it is the only market in this paper where the economic story and the equity story have diverged for two decades, where the divergence is explicable, and where the next ten years turn on whether the explanation still holds.

10.1 The central fact

Between roughly 2005 and 2025 Chinese real output grew at an average rate in the high single digits — the largest sustained expansion in modern economic history — and a dollar-based holder of the main offshore China equity index earned approximately nothing over the last decade of that period. Any framework for the next ten years must begin by explaining that gap, because if the explanation is structural the gap continues, and if it is cyclical the entry point is extraordinary.

Four explanations, in descending order of weight.

Dilution. Chinese listed companies issued equity aggressively. Index-level earnings per share grew far more slowly than aggregate corporate earnings because the share count grew. This is the largest single component, and it is the one that has recently begun to change.

Capital allocation by the state. A material share of the listed universe is state-owned enterprises whose objective function includes employment, strategic capacity, and price stability rather than returns on capital. An economy can grow while its listed vehicles earn below their cost of capital, and China's has.

The listed universe is not the growth economy. The fastest-growing sectors were for long stretches unlisted, listed offshore in structures foreign holders do not literally own, or effectively nationalized by regulatory intervention. The index has been persistently under-exposed to what was actually growing.

Multiple compression from a rising risk premium. The 2021 platform intervention, the property crisis, and the geopolitical trajectory each raised the discount rate applied to Chinese cash flows. Some of that is permanent; a portion is cyclical.

10.2 Three markets, not one

Market What it is Who trades it The distinct risk
A-shares Domestic listings, renminbi Domestic retail dominant; foreign access via connect schemes and partial index inclusion Policy intervention; retail-driven volatility; capital-account restriction
Hong Kong listings Offshore listings of mainland companies Global institutions Flow-driven; a persistent discount to the same companies' A-shares
U.S.-listed ADRs Receipts, often over a variable interest entity Global institutions The holder owns a contractual claim on offshore cash flows, not the operating company; audit and delisting risk was acute in 2020–22 and remains a live political variable

A "China position" that does not specify which of these is under-specified. The register enforces it: three instruments, three risks, three mechanism groups.

10.3 The technology question, treated seriously

The caricature is wrong in both directions. China is not an assembler of others' designs, and it is not at comprehensive parity. The record by sector:

Won, on any reasonable definition. Solar — roughly eighty percent or more of the global supply chain across polysilicon, wafers, cells, and modules. Batteries — the largest producer holds roughly a third of the world market and the country's share of cell capacity is dominant. Electric vehicles — more produced and sold than the rest of the world combined, with a domestic champion that has passed every Western manufacturer on volume and now exports at scale. Industrial robotics — more units installed annually than the rest of the world combined, with domestic vendors taking share from Japanese and European incumbents. High-speed rail, drones, shipbuilding, and increasingly commercial aviation.

Genuinely competitive and improving fast. Artificial intelligence — frontier-adjacent models trained at a small fraction of Western reported cost and released with open weights demonstrated that export controls have raised the price of compute rather than closed the capability gap. Biotechnology — a rapidly rising share of the new drug candidates licensed by Western pharmaceutical companies now originates in Chinese laboratories, a structural change in the global innovation supply chain that attracts a fraction of the attention semiconductors do. Cloud, electronic design, materials science.

Where the constraint binds. Advanced logic semiconductors — the leading-edge process gap persists, sustained by controls on lithography equipment; mature and mid-range domestic capability is substantial and growing, and advanced-node production has been demonstrated at yields and costs that are not commercially competitive. Aviation engines. Parts of scientific instrumentation and industrial software.

10.4 The trap: winning the industry is not the same as earning a return

This is the most important analytical point in the chapter, and solar is its proof. China achieved overwhelming global dominance in solar manufacturing, and the industry destroyed capital for most of fifteen years: relentless capacity addition, state-directed and provincially subsidized, drove prices down faster than costs, and the winners of share were frequently losers of money.

The same dynamic is visible in electric vehicles and developing in batteries — a domestic price war of exceptional severity, dozens of subscale manufacturers kept alive by local governments protecting employment, margins compressed across an industry that is simultaneously conquering the world's export markets.

The mechanism is structural, not accidental. When capital is allocated by entities whose objectives include local employment and strategic capacity, the industry's supply curve does not respond to returns on capital the way a market's does, and excess capacity persists. For an equity holder the consequence is that Chinese technological success may accrue to consumers worldwide and to the state's strategic position without accruing to shareholders. That is a distributional fact rather than a bearish one — and it is the specific thing to watch for change.

The countervailing evidence is real: the central authorities have named self-defeating price competition as a problem to be addressed and have begun signaling capacity discipline in several of these industries. If that discipline is genuinely enforced, the same volumes at rational prices are a very different equity outcome. This is the single highest-value thing to monitor in the Chinese equity story over the next ten years, and it is observable: capacity utilization, module and cell price indices, and the survival rate of subscale manufacturers.

10.5 The headwinds

Property. At its peak, real estate and its linkages accounted for something in the region of a quarter to a third of Chinese activity, and it has contracted since 2021. The losses sit in household balance sheets, where the great majority of savings is housing; in local-government finances, which depended on land sales; and in the banks and financing vehicles connected to both. Arithmetically it is the largest balance-sheet event in the global economy and it is not finished.

Demographics. The population peaked in 2021–22; the working-age population is falling; fertility is roughly one child per woman, among the world's lowest. This is the most predictable variable in the paper and it subtracts from potential growth every year.

Debt and deflation. Non-financial debt is roughly three times output, concentrated in corporates and local government, while the output deflator has been negative across multiple quarters. Deflation with high debt is precisely the Base Rates paper's deflationary-liquidation branch — applied to a country that controls its own currency and has so far declined to inflate its way out.

Geopolitics. Export controls, tariffs, and supply-chain reconfiguration are a persistent tax on the export machine; the Taiwan question is the scenario no financial hedge addresses.

Two readings. The expected value is respectable and the distribution is bimodal — a high starting yield and a low multiple do most of the work in the middle two rows, and the tail is a discontinuity rather than a bad number. And the tail is not a valuation risk, so it cannot be sized by valuation. The Base Rates paper's p99 row is the precedent for the fourth scenario; the correct response to a downside that includes zero is a small position or none, not a wider discount rate.

Chapter 11 — Taiwan and Korea

Small in index weight, disproportionate in importance, because they are where the AI supply chain physically is. The world's advanced logic manufacturing is concentrated in one Taiwanese firm to a degree without parallel in any other critical industry, and memory production is concentrated in two Korean firms and one American one. For this system these markets are not a diversification decision; they are the upstream of the speculative-cohort monitor. A capex cycle that shows up in Dell's server revenue three quarters later shows up in Taiwanese monthly revenue statements first — those statements are published monthly, which makes them one of the highest-frequency fundamental series available on the AI capital cycle.

Korea additionally runs its own version of the Japanese governance reform, aimed at the persistent discount its market trades at relative to peers, with the same mechanism — disclosure requirements, incentives for capital return — and an unresolved question about whether it reaches the family-controlled conglomerates where the discount is largest.

Chapter 12 — India and the rest of emerging markets

India is the structural growth story of the group — demographics, a domestic investor base whose systematic monthly flows have become a genuine market-stabilizing force, and a corporate sector with high returns on equity — and it trades at the highest valuation in the emerging complex, which is the whole of the debate. It is the clearest case in this paper of a market where the growth story and the entry price are both true.

Emerging markets in aggregate are best understood by this system as a dollar-cycle instrument with a commodity overlay: the group's relative performance has historically been strongly conditioned on the direction of the dollar and on global growth, because dollar funding costs and commodity revenues dominate. That makes the aggregate a poor vehicle for a country view and a reasonable vehicle for a dollar view — which returns the reader to Part III.

13.1 The stale-NAV problem, and why it is an opportunity as well as a trap

A U.S.-listed fund holding Japanese shares trades all day in New York while Tokyo is closed. Its net asset value is a stale number — the last Tokyo prints — and its market price is therefore live price discovery about a closed market. The premium or discount is not an error; it is the U.S. session's opinion about where Tokyo will open.

Three consequences. Reading the fund's premium to stale NAV is a legitimate, free, real-time signal about the coming Asian session. Trading these funds in size near the U.S. close carries a real risk that the overnight session revalues the underlying against you. And the frequently cited "ETF premium/discount" of country funds is mostly a timing artifact rather than a liquidity failure — which does not make it harmless, because the artifact is exactly what a stop-loss placed on a country fund will be triggered by.

13.2 Dividend withholding and the tax boundary

Foreign dividends are typically subject to withholding at source, commonly fifteen percent under treaty for a U.S. investor and higher in several markets. In a taxable account the withheld amount is generally recoverable through the foreign tax credit; in a tax-deferred account it is generally lost outright. This makes account location a real component of expected return for an income-oriented international position — a consideration that belongs beside the Systematic Book paper's tax chapter, and one that argues for holding high-yielding international exposure in taxable rather than deferred accounts, subject to the operator's accountant.

Part VII — Correlation and What Diversification Buys

Chapter 14 — Correlation, dispersion, and how both move

14.1 The long arc

Cross-country equity correlation has risen substantially over three decades. Monthly-return correlation between U.S. and developed-international indices ran near 0.5 in the 1970s and 1980s, drifted toward 0.7 through the 1990s, and has spent most of the period since 2008 between 0.80 and 0.90.

The drivers are structural and unlikely to reverse: multinational revenue overlap, so that a "European" index is substantially exposed to the same global demand as an American one; synchronized central-bank policy; the growth of global index products that transact whole countries as one line; and cross-border capital that reprices every market on the same news within hours.

14.2 Correlation is a state, not a constant

The average conceals the fact that matters. Correlation is regime-dependent, and it moves in the direction that hurts:

Regime Approximate U.S.–international correlation, monthly Approximate average pairwise correlation within a market
Calm, low dispersion 0.65–0.75 0.20–0.35
Ordinary 0.75–0.85 0.35–0.45
Stressed 0.85–0.92 0.55–0.70
Crisis 0.92–0.97 0.70–0.90

Source class: computed and cited; approximate, and sensitive to window and index pair.

And the movement is asymmetric. The exceedance-correlation literature — Longin and Solnik's work is the standard reference — finds that international correlations rise sharply in joint downside moves and do not rise, or rise much less, in joint upside moves. Diversification therefore weakens precisely in the states where it is being relied upon, and strengthens in the states where it is not needed. This is not a flaw in diversification; it is a property to be sized around, and it is the reason the Doctrine caps heat across books at a common factor rather than trusting nominal diversification.

14.3 Rolling correlation, and where it is now

Measured on a three-year rolling window, U.S.–international correlation peaked during the global financial crisis and the euro crisis that followed, and has drifted lower since — into the high-0.7s to low-0.8s — as monetary policy diverged, fiscal responses differed, and sector composition pulled the two indices further apart. A falling rolling correlation is a mildly favorable input to an international allocation, and it is a series worth logging rather than an anecdote worth citing.

14.4 Short horizons: correlation is partly a clock artifact

Daily correlations between a U.S. index and a foreign one are systematically understated relative to the true economic relationship, for a mechanical reason: the sessions do not overlap. A Tokyo close at 02:00 New York time embeds the previous U.S. session; a New York close at 16:00 embeds a European session that closed four and a half hours earlier. The measured daily correlation is therefore a mixture of contemporaneous co-movement and lagged transmission.

Three practical consequences.

Use weekly or monthly returns for allocation decisions. Daily correlation for a non-overlapping pair is a biased estimate, and the bias runs downward — it makes diversification look better than it is.

Use the overlap window for intraday work. Between roughly 09:30 and 11:30 New York time, European markets are open alongside the U.S. and the correlation in that window is far higher than the daily figure suggests. The Daily Cascade's morning read should treat the overlap as one market.

Lead-lag is real and mostly one-directional. The U.S. session leads the following Asian and European sessions far more than the reverse; the exception is a genuinely non-U.S. shock — a policy surprise in Tokyo, a European fiscal event — which reverses the direction for a session. Distinguishing the two is exactly what Chapter 16's overnight gap attribution is for.

14.5 Dispersion: the reason correlation is the wrong statistic

Correlation describes the co-movement of the wiggle. It says almost nothing about the level of returns over long horizons. The U.S. and developed international indices have been correlated at roughly 0.85 for fifteen years and have delivered returns that differ by five to six percentage points a year over that span. Two series can be highly correlated and enormously different investments.

The right statistics for an allocation decision are therefore dispersion and its persistence rather than correlation alone: the cross-sectional spread of country returns in a given year (which remains wide, routinely twenty points or more between the best and worst major developed markets), and the autocorrelation of relative performance (which is the leadership-cycle persistence of Chapter 3). Correlation belongs to the risk calculation; dispersion belongs to the opportunity calculation.

14.6 The hedging interaction

A detail with real portfolio consequence: currency-hedged international exposure has a lower correlation to U.S. equities than unhedged, because the dollar tends to strengthen in global risk-off, which drags unhedged foreign returns down at the same moment foreign equities are falling. The unhedged instrument bundles two pro-cyclical exposures; the hedged instrument carries one. For a dollar-based holder seeking diversification specifically, the hedged version is the better diversifier and the unhedged version is the better dollar-decline expression — which is the same distinction Chapter 6 draws, arriving from the correlation side.

14.7 The honest case against diversification, and the synthesis

Stated because the Doctrine requires a steelman: U.S. companies earn a large share of revenue abroad, so an American index holder already has foreign economic exposure without foreign governance, currency, or expropriation risk; the U.S. has the deepest markets, the strongest shareholder protections, and the dominant position in the industries with the highest returns on capital; and fifteen years of evidence say the diversifier has paid a large premium for the insurance.

The synthesis this paper offers has not changed and is now better supported: international allocation is sized as insurance is sized — enough that the single-country tail is covered, not so much that the premium dominates the portfolio's return. The correlation evidence says the insurance is imperfect in exactly the states one wants it. The dispersion evidence says the payoff is nonetheless large when leadership turns. And the Base Rates Part IV distribution says what the insurance is actually against, which is not a bad quarter but a Japanese thirty-four years.

Part VIII — A Ten-Year Probabilistic View

Chapter 15 — Relative returns over the next decade, as a distribution

This chapter is the paper's only forward-looking content, and it is written as a probability distribution with stated inputs rather than as a forecast, because that is the only honest form. Its observation_type is inferred; nothing in it is trigger_eligible; and its purpose is to size an allocation and set a bar for a variant view, not to generate a trade.

15.1 The method

The building-block approach decomposes an expected long-horizon return into components that can each be estimated separately and argued about individually:

expected return ≈ starting yield (dividends + net buybacks) + real earnings growth + inflation + change in valuation + currency

The virtue of the decomposition is not accuracy — it is that a disagreement can be located. Two people who disagree about the next decade usually disagree about exactly one term, and this form makes them say which.

The table's central estimate favors international by roughly one to two percentage points a year — and the paper immediately insists that the distribution around that estimate is far wider than the gap, which is the whole point of Chapter 15.4.

15.3 Where each term is weakest

The U.S. valuation term is the least defensible number in the table. Partial mean reversion is an assumption, not a finding. A reasonable person can argue that the American index's composition — a higher share of businesses with high returns on capital, low capital intensity, and network effects — justifies a permanently higher multiple, in which case the term is zero and the U.S. central estimate rises to eight or nine percent. Valuation-based forecasts have moderate skill at ten years and almost none at one to three; the cyclically-adjusted multiple has explained something like forty percent of the variance of subsequent ten-year real returns historically, and less in recent decades.

The currency term is the least forecastable. Purchasing-power and real-effective-exchange-rate measures suggest the dollar is expensive against most developed peers, and long-horizon real exchange rates do mean-revert — but on a half-life measured in years with enormous variance, and the reserve-currency and rate-differential arguments cut the other way.

The earnings-growth gap is the term that actually matters and it is the one with the strongest recent evidence in the U.S. favor. The bull case for continued American outperformance stands or falls here, and it has a specific mechanism: if artificial-intelligence capability translates into durable productivity gains that accrue disproportionately to the firms that own the models, the platforms, and the chips, then the U.S. earnings term is structurally higher and no amount of valuation or currency mean reversion closes the gap.

15.4 Scenarios

Scenario Probability Mechanism Ten-year relative outcome
American exceptionalism persists ~30% AI productivity accrues to U.S. owners; the earnings gap holds; the multiple does not compress; the dollar stays firm on rate differentials and safe-haven demand U.S. ahead by 2–4%/yr
Convergence ~35% The dollar mean-reverts modestly; the multiple gap narrows partway; international earnings improve on governance reform and fiscal impulse without matching U.S. growth International ahead by 1–3%/yr
Reversal ~20% A sustained dollar downtrend; U.S. multiple compression from a concentrated index's disappointment; Japanese and Korean reform delivers; European fiscal impulse compounds International ahead by 4–7%/yr
Global de-rating ~15% A debt-cycle resolution, a geopolitical rupture, or a synchronized policy error; both regions poor in absolute terms Relative outcome depends on branch — see 15.5

Aggregating: the probability that developed international outperforms the U.S. over the next ten years is roughly 55–60%, with a median advantage near one percentage point a year and a distribution wide enough that both a four-point U.S. win and a five-point international win sit inside one standard deviation.

Three cautions about that sentence. It is a prior, not a signal. It has been approximately the right prior for a decade and would have been wrong for all of it. And the probability is the author's construction from the components above, not an output of a model with a track record — the least defensible figure in the paper, offered because the alternative is an implicit prior that nobody has to defend.

15.5 The de-rating branch, and why it is not symmetric

The fourth scenario's relative outcome depends on which mechanism produces it, and the two branches point in opposite directions:

Deflationary or credit-driven — a debt-deflation resolution, a credit event — and the dollar strengthens as it always has in dollar-funding stress, U.S. Treasuries rally, and the U.S. relatively outperforms even as both fall.

Inflationary or debasement-driven — a fiscal-dominance resolution in which the U.S. specifically monetizes — and the dollar falls, real assets and non-U.S. equities in appreciating currencies outperform substantially, and the American holder's home bias is his largest exposure.

This is the same fork as the Base Rates Part IV table, and it is the reason the international sleeve is not merely a return-seeking decision but a hedge against a specific resolution of the operator's own stated tail concern. A portfolio worried about U.S. fiscal dominance and holding only U.S. equities is a portfolio whose hedge and whose exposure are the same asset.

15.6 How this chapter is used, and how it is not

Used: to size Book A's international sleeve at the monthly session, with the sizing recorded as a thesis; to set the bar a variant view must clear — a decision to hold zero international exposure is now a decision against a 55–60% prior and should say why; and to define the invalidation conditions in 15.7.

Not used: to generate a packet, to time a rotation, or to justify a Book B position. A ten-year prior has no information about the next six months, and the Doctrine's half-life rules would refuse it as a signal in any case.

15.7 What would falsify each scenario

American exceptionalism is falsified by the earnings gap closing for four or more consecutive quarters while the multiple gap stays wide — the combination that says the premium is no longer earned.

Convergence is falsified by a dollar breakout to new highs alongside a widening earnings gap, or by international reform visibly stalling — Japanese buybacks rolling over, European fiscal impulse failing to reach delivery.

Reversal is falsified by U.S. relative earnings accelerating, which would make the valuation gap a fair reflection rather than an anomaly.

De-rating is monitored by the debt-cycle observables of the architecture's Part 31.2, not by anything in this paper.

These are the register's entries, dated and gradeable: this chapter enters as four hypotheses with a ten-year horizon and annual review, and its scoring is the honest test of whether the paper was worth writing.

Chapter 17 — What would change this paper

A sustained dollar downtrend, which would convert the currency term of Chapter 3 from headwind to tailwind and change the hedging ruling.

A break in the earnings component of U.S. outperformance — the durable third — which is the only decomposition line whose reversal would make the relative case on fundamentals rather than on mean reversion.

The Japanese reform reaching the small-cap tail, or stalling, which is the test of whether Chapter 7's thesis is structural or a large-cap event.

A resolution, in either direction, of the Chinese property adjustment, which is the largest unresolved balance-sheet event in the global economy.

Any material change in the Taiwan situation, which would be a tail event first and a market-structure event second, and would be handled by the tail watch rather than by this paper.

And the register's first international decisions — whether the currency leg was named, whether the hedging choice was recorded, and whether the sleeve behaved as insurance is supposed to behave when it was needed.

Appendix — The one-page card

The map: U.S. ~65% of global equity cap on ~25% of GDP and ~4% of population; Japan ~5%; Europe ~10%; China's index weight a fraction of its economic weight. Japan was ~45% in 1989. Shares mean-revert on a clock longer than a career.

Why the U.S. won: roughly a third currency, a third relative multiple expansion, a third genuine earnings growth. Two of those three do not repeat indefinitely.

Valuation gap: much of it is sector composition. Adjust before concluding.

Currency: an unhedged international position is two decisions. Hedged versus unhedged is a recorded choice with its own thesis; the rate differential is the cost or the carry.

Europe: banks, industrials, energy, luxury; fragmentation spread is the real financial-conditions gauge; the defense and infrastructure impulse is the change. UK: currency-inverse, discounted, a steady source of takeover situations. Japan: governance reform is the thesis; policy normalization is the macro variable; the equity and the yen are one trade with two legs. China: three markets, not one; the risk is not valuation, it is that state objectives and minority interests diverge with no recourse. Taiwan/Korea: the AI supply chain's physical location; monthly revenue statements are the highest-frequency read on the capex cycle. India: the growth story and the entry price are both true. EM aggregate: a dollar-cycle instrument with a commodity overlay.

Traps: stale NAV on country funds is live price discovery, not an error — and it is what your stop will be triggered by; dividend withholding is recoverable in taxable accounts and lost in deferred ones; the overnight gap is two foreign sessions, and attributing it is worth more than reporting it.

Diversification: buys insurance against the single-country tail, not against the bad quarter. Size it as insurance.
